## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A walking tour of six stops in the old center of Amsterdam, planned for Halloween evening, October 31, mostly after dark. Allow about two and a half hours at an easy pace, with a stop for champagne. The champagne break is suggested in the audio after the Oude Kerk and again at the end, but you can take it wherever you like. The route is a loose loop, roughly three kilometers in all. Each chapter is one stop, so you can pause between them and the audio carries on as you walk.

Stop positions on the map are approximate, good to about 50 meters at best, so keep a map open as well. Directions in the audio are deliberately loose. Cobbles are uneven and canal edges often have no railing, so watch your feet, and be careful with a phone in your hand in the dark. Most of these buildings are closed to visitors at night, so this is a walk of exteriors. The Begijnhof is a residential courtyard, and its opening hours change, so check that it is open and keep quiet. The Oude Kerk stands in the red light district, which is busy and lively in the evening. Please be respectful and do not photograph the windows.

Throughout, the hosts separate documented history from folk legend. Where a story is a legend, they say so. We found no well-sourced ghost story attached to every stop, and the episode does not invent any. Opening hours, queues and any construction change often, so check them on the day.

## Welcome and how this works

Anna: Hello, and welcome to a walk. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. Tonight is Halloween, and this is a walking tour of old Amsterdam, in six stops.

Anna: After dark.

Ben: After dark. With a legend or two at every stop.

Anna: Oh, I like this plan.

Ben: But we have one rule. We keep the record and the legend apart.

Anna: Meaning?

Ben: Meaning that when something is documented, we say "this is the record." And when it's a story people tell, we say "this is a legend." Every time.

Anna: A stamp. Like a customs officer. Record, or legend?

Ben: Exactly like that.

Anna: And I get to guess before you stamp.

Ben: You can guess. You'll usually be wrong.

Anna: I'm keeping a tally of that, by the way. Ghosts, versus real history.

Ben: Fine. Keep the tally. Now, the practical part. Each chapter is one stop. We tell you where you are, what you're looking at, and the story. Then we point you toward the next one.

Anna: And if I want to stand there and stare at a spooky wall?

Ben: Press pause. Stare as long as you like. The tour waits.

Anna: What do I need?

Ben: Comfortable shoes, because of the cobbles. A phone with some battery, because it's dark and you'll want the map. And a little care, because the canals here often have no railings.

Anna: So don't walk into the water because of a ghost story.

Ben: Please don't. Not a good ending to the evening.

Anna: And the champagne?

Ben: Twice on offer. We'll tell you when. Roughly in the middle, and again at the end.

Anna: That's what I'm here for.

Ben: One more thing. Most of these buildings are closed at night. So this is a walk of outsides. And we'll flag the places where you should be quiet, or take care.

Anna: Understood. Where do we start?

Ben: By the harbor. A short walk from the main station, along a street called Prins Hendrikkade. Look for a round brick tower with a cafe at its foot, on a corner by the water. Press play when you're standing there.

## Stop 1: The Schreierstoren, the Weeping Tower

Anna: Okay. Where are we?

Ben: On the harbor side, in front of a short, round, brick tower. It's called the Schreierstoren.

Anna: The Weeping Tower.

Ben: That's the name everyone gives it in English.

Anna: And it's weeping because?

Ben: Ah. Here's the legend. Say it as the tour guides say it. Sailors left from here for months, or years, and their wives came to the tower to wave, and cry.

Anna: Oh, that's lovely. And sad.

Ben: It is. It's the story you'll hear at the door.

Anna: So, record, or legend?

Ben: You tell me.

Anna: Legend. Too neat.

Ben: Correct. Legend. Point one for you.

Anna: Ha. Wait, a point for me? So what's the record?

Ben: The record is this. The tower was built in 1487, as a defense tower, part of the medieval city wall. In its older Dutch form, the name was Schreyhoeckstoren. Schrey meant sharp, hoeck meant corner, toren meant tower.

Anna: The Sharp Corner Tower.

Ben: Yes. Because the wall made a sharp angle here. Over time the name shortened. And the Dutch word for weeping sounds very close to the old word for sharp.

Anna: So somebody heard "sharp" and wrote "weeping."

Ben: That's the standard explanation. A confusion of words, and then a story grew to fit it.

Anna: That's almost worse than the legend. It's a typo with a tragedy attached.

Ben: There's a bit more to it. There's an old tablet on the tower, dated 1569, that tells of a woman driven out of her mind by grief. People think it helped the weeping story along.

Anna: So the wives are a legend. The tablet is real, though.

Ben: The tablet exists, yes. What it proves is a matter for historians. We'll say it fed the story.

Anna: Okay. Anything real about the sailors?

Ben: Plenty. Look for the small plaque by the door. It marks Henry Hudson. In 1609 he sailed from Amsterdam, on a ship called the Halve Maen, for the Dutch East India Company. He was looking for a route to Asia. He found the river that now carries his name, and the harbor where New York now stands.

Anna: Wait. From here?

Ben: The tower is where the sailing is marked. Whether he cast off at exactly this spot is a different question. The plaque says it for the tower.

Anna: Careful. You're hedging.

Ben: I'm being exact. It's the same thing.

Anna: Oh, here's a fun one. This tower is the only thing on the harbor with a Chicago connection.

Ben: Not quite the only. But yes, a brick from this tower is built into the Tribune Tower in Chicago.

Anna: A piece of the Weeping Tower, in a skyscraper.

Ben: Memorials like that were collected from all over the world.

Anna: Okay, one more. What's inside now?

Ben: A cafe and a nautical bookshop. And the tower is a national monument. It's no longer a place of weeping. Mostly coffee.

Anna: Score so far. Ghost stories, nought. Real history, one.

Ben: Don't write the whole tally yet.

Anna: Before we leave. Is there a spooky detail?

Ben: At night, the water in front of the tower is black, and the old harbor wall is right there. That's a feeling, not a fact. Take it as atmosphere.

Anna: Pause here, everyone. Look at the water. Imagine somebody waving. And remember it's probably not what happened.

Ben: Well put.

Anna: Now, where?

Ben: Turn your back to the harbor, walk inland and a bit south, away from the water, for about ten minutes. You're heading to a big open square. In the middle of it is a brick building that looks like a small castle. That's the next stop.

Anna: A castle that isn't a castle.

Ben: A gate. We'll get there.

## Stop 2: Nieuwmarkt and De Waag

Anna: Okay. A big square, and a castle in the middle. This is what, a castle?

Ben: It's called De Waag. It means the Weigh House. And no, it's not a castle. It was built as a city gate.

Anna: A gate. Like a door in the wall.

Ben: Yes. Its first name was Saint Anthony's Gate, the Sint Antoniespoort. A stone on the building gives the date for the first stone as 28 April 1488. One historian argues it might be older, around 1425, so the date may need correcting.

Anna: So we're not sure.

Ben: Sort of. That's the record. Slightly fuzzy.

Anna: Why's it not a gate now?

Ben: The city grew. By the late 16th century the wall had moved outward. The gate was no longer on the edge. In 1614, a stretch of canal beside it was filled in to make this square. That's why it's called the Nieuwmarkt. The New Market.

Anna: And the gate became a weigh house.

Ben: In 1617. Goods were weighed here, including anchors and artillery from the nearby shipyards.

Anna: Okay, but you promised spooky.

Ben: I did. Look at the top floors. Guilds had their halls up there. Blacksmiths, painters, masons, and the surgeons.

Anna: Surgeons. Oh no.

Ben: The surgeons' guild had a lecture hall in the building. There's an inscription at their entrance. Theatrum Anatomicum.

Anna: Anatomy theatre.

Ben: Yes. Where bodies were dissected, in front of an audience.

Anna: In winter, I'm guessing. For the smell.

Ben: In winter, yes. According to the city's heritage site, the dissections were of the bodies of executed criminals. And the guild kept using the top floor for that until 1869.

Anna: Okay. Can we say something about the painting?

Ben: Yes. Rembrandt's Anatomy Lesson of Doctor Nicolaes Tulp. It was painted in 1632. The surgeons' guild commissioned it, and each man in it paid to be included.

Anna: Pay to be in the picture. Very modern.

Ben: Quite. Now, we'll slow down a bit. The corpse in the painting was a real person. His name was Aris Kindt. He had been convicted of armed robbery and hanged earlier that day. The dissection was on 31 January 1632.

Anna: Right.

Ben: The painting now hangs in the Mauritshuis, in The Hague. Not here. It was made for the guild.

Anna: That's the record.

Ben: That's the record.

Anna: And the square itself? You said the city site had something to say.

Ben: It did. It says the Nieuwmarkt was known for corporal punishment and executions, though that source gives no dates. And in the first half of the 19th century, punishments were carried out in front of this building. A guillotine was used here.

Anna: Okay. That's a hard thing to stand on.

Ben: It is. That's not a ghost story. That's documented. People suffered here.

Anna: So where's the legend?

Ben: We have to be honest. We didn't find a well-sourced ghost legend attached to the Waag. There are plenty of internet claims about hauntings. We can't back them up.

Anna: So we don't repeat them.

Ben: We don't repeat them. You can stand here after dark and feel what you feel. But we won't tell you a ghost walks the roof.

Anna: Fine. A clean stamp. Record, record, record.

Ben: Today the Waag has a cafe and restaurant on the ground floor, and a technology and society foundation above. It became a national monument in 1970.

Anna: A restaurant in an old execution square.

Ben: Yes. Cities do that.

Anna: Tally. Ghost stories, still nought.

Ben: Real history, two.

Anna: Don't gloat.

Ben: I'm deadpan. It's different.

Anna: Okay. Next?

Ben: Walk south-east, just a few minutes. A tall tower with a thin lead-covered spire rises over the rooftops. Walk toward it. That's the Zuiderkerk.

## Stop 3: The Zuiderkerk, the South Church

Anna: Oh, that's pretty. It looks like a wedding cake.

Ben: Good eye. That's Renaissance style, in brick and stone. The architect was Hendrick de Keyser. It was built from 1603 to 1611. The tower came later, finished in 1614.

Anna: And it's called the South Church because it's in the south.

Ben: Of the city, as it was then. Yes. It was the first new Protestant church built in Amsterdam.

Anna: Wait, is that in the record?

Ben: It's a common claim in guides. Our fact sources for this walk don't state it, so take it as a guide's claim. The dates and the architect, we can state.

Anna: Good. I like a Ben who admits it.

Ben: Now, De Keyser himself. He died in 1621, and he's buried in this church.

Anna: The architect, buried in his own building.

Ben: Yes. That's the record.

Anna: It's a nice thing to have.

Ben: Now we come to the sad part. This part has no jokes.

Anna: Okay.

Ben: Three of Rembrandt's children are buried here. Rembrandt lived very close, on the Jodenbreestraat, just a short distance from this church.

Anna: Three children.

Ben: Yes. We know that from the church's own history. We won't guess anything more about them.

Anna: And there's more.

Ben: In the winter of 1944 to 1945, the Hunger Winter, the church was used as a temporary morgue. People were dying faster than they could be buried.

Anna: That's very recent.

Ben: It is. Some of the people alive in this city today remember it, or their parents do.

Anna: That's a different kind of haunted.

Ben: Yes. Not a ghost story. A memory.

Anna: How long did it work as a church?

Ben: Services ended in 1929. The building closed in 1970. After renovation from 1976 to 1979, it opened as a municipal information center in 1988. It still has that role, with exhibitions.

Anna: And the tower has bells.

Ben: A carillon, installed in 1656 by the Hemony brothers, famous bell makers. If you're lucky, you might hear it. It's not guaranteed.

Anna: Is there a legend?

Ben: Again, we looked. Nothing well-sourced that we can pass on. We'd rather say that than make something up.

Anna: Okay. Pause for a moment, everyone. Look up at the tower against the night sky.

Ben: Good moment for it.

Anna: And then?

Ben: Then we head back toward the old center, west. You're going to the Oude Kerk. The Old Church. Walk back past the Waag, then west through the lanes. Keep the map open, because the streets bend. The church tower stands in a small square, with buildings tight all around it.

Anna: And what's the area like?

Ben: Lively. It's the red light district. We'll talk about that when we're there.

## Stop 4: The Oude Kerk, the Old Church

Anna: Okay. We're in a small square, with a big church in the middle. It's surrounded by buildings. It feels almost hidden.

Ben: That's the Oude Kerk. It means the Old Church. It's the oldest building in Amsterdam still standing, by most accounts, though we'll say "by most accounts."

Anna: Why am I not surprised?

Ben: A wooden chapel stood here by about 1213. The stone church was consecrated in 1306, to Saint Nicholas.

Anna: Patron saint of sailors.

Ben: Right. Fits a harbor town.

Anna: And now the surroundings. It's busy tonight.

Ben: It is. The church stands in the old quarter called De Wallen. This is the red light district. There are windows behind the church. Please be respectful, and do not photograph the workers.

Anna: Understood. They're working.

Ben: Yes. And a small statue near the church, called Belle, carries a message of support for sex workers.

Anna: Okay. Now the spooky part.

Ben: Here's the thing. This one doesn't need a legend. The record is spooky enough.

Anna: Go on.

Ben: The floor of the church is made of gravestones. About 2,500 of them. Under them, roughly 60,000 Amsterdam citizens were buried. Burials inside the church continued until 1865.

Anna: Sixty thousand.

Ben: Under the floor. You walk on them.

Anna: That's more than a lot of towns.

Ben: Yes. And it's all documented.

Anna: Okay, I'm properly spooked. And no ghost needed. Stamp it. Record.

Ben: Record. Now the famous names. Rembrandt's wife Saskia van Uylenburgh was buried here in 1642. And the composer and organist Jan Pieterszoon Sweelinck is buried here too. He lived from 1562 to 1621.

Anna: Saskia. Oh.

Ben: There's a lovely detail. On one morning in March, the sun briefly lights her tomb. 9 March, at about 8:39 in the morning.

Anna: That's beautiful. Record?

Ben: That's what the church's own information says. We'll call it record, with the caution that we can't check the sunlight ourselves.

Anna: Fine. What happened in the 16th century?

Ben: In 1566, the Beeldenstorm. It means the Statue Storm. Protestant crowds destroyed religious images across the Low Countries. Here, most of the church's art and fittings were destroyed. An altarpiece by Jan van Scorel was lost. Only the ceiling paintings survived.

Anna: And then?

Ben: In 1578 the city switched sides and the church became Protestant, Calvinist. It still is. It has four organs, and one of them, built in 1724 by Christian Vater, is considered among the finest Baroque organs in Europe.

Anna: Wait, how does this connect to the miracle?

Ben: Good question. Each March, Catholics in Amsterdam walk a silent procession called the Stille Omgang, in memory of a miracle said to have happened in 1345. It's a tradition, and we'll hear more of it at the next stop.

Anna: Okay. Tally. Ghost stories, nought.

Ben: Real history, three.

Anna: But this one's the spookiest.

Ben: By far. Sixty thousand people, under your shoes.

Anna: Is it open now?

Ben: Probably not, this late. It does host art exhibitions today, and it's an active church. Check the hours another day.

Anna: Okay. This is the champagne moment, isn't it?

Ben: It is. You're in a lively quarter. There are bars and cafes near here. Take a half hour. Sit down. Warm up.

Anna: Raise a glass to the sixty thousand?

Ben: A quiet toast would be fine.

Anna: A very quiet toast. Pause the tour, everyone. We'll wait.

Ben: When you're ready, the next stop is the Begijnhof. It's in the middle of the old center, near a square called the Spui. From here you go south through the center, toward the Spui. A narrow passage off the pedestrian street, the Kalverstraat, leads into the courtyard.

Anna: Pedestrian street, narrow passage. Hidden door.

Ben: Hidden is the word. And please keep your voice down once you're in. People live there.

## Stop 5: The Begijnhof

Anna: Oh. Oh, that's quiet.

Ben: It is. You've stepped through a small passage and left the city behind. This is the Begijnhof. A hidden courtyard of small houses around a green lawn.

Anna: It feels like a different century.

Ben: In a way, yes. Please talk softly, if you talk at all. These are people's homes.

Anna: Right. Whispering then.

Ben: Whispering is fine. Now, who lived here? Beguines. Lay Catholic women who lived together, devoted to prayer and charity, but who were not nuns.

Anna: When did it start?

Ben: It's unclear exactly. A beguine house is mentioned in a document from 1346, and a courtyard in 1389. So we say, by the late 14th century at the latest.

Anna: Okay. And the miracle?

Ben: That's the story behind the Stille Omgang. Here's the tradition. In March 1345, a gravely ill man was given the last rites. He vomited up the communion host. The priest wrapped it and put it in the fire. The next morning, the host was found unburnt in the ashes.

Anna: That sounds like a legend.

Ben: It is. It's tradition. The earliest sources of the story are a matter for historians, and the details we gave are the version people tell. Even the retellings say "allegedly."

Anna: So, record, or legend?

Ben: Legend, in its details. What is documented is that the devotion to it grew, that pilgrims came, and that it shaped the city's religious life. A procession, the Silent Procession, still commemorates it every March.

Anna: Okay. Legend. Point to me?

Ben: Point to you.

Anna: And the houses?

Ben: Look for number 34. It's called Het Houten Huys, the Wooden House. It dates from about 1420, and it's the oldest wooden house in Amsterdam. One of only two wooden houses still standing in the center.

Anna: Because of fires.

Ben: Yes. Wooden houses were banned after big fires. This one survived.

Anna: And there's a church.

Ben: Two. In 1578, when the city turned Protestant, the beguines' chapel was closed for about 30 years, then given to English Presbyterians. It's known as the English Church.

Anna: And the other?

Ben: Across from it, a hidden Catholic church. In 1671 the architect Philip Vingboons converted two houses into a church that could not be recognized from the outside. Catholics could worship, quietly. Today it's called the Miracle Church, from 1908.

Anna: A secret church. That's the record?

Ben: That's the record.

Anna: Okay, so now the ghost story. You promised.

Ben: Yes. This one is a legend. It's about a woman named Cornelia Arens. She was a beguine, and she died on 14 October 1654.

Anna: That date is certain?

Ben: It's the date given for her death. The legend is about her burial. The story says she wanted to be buried in the church. The church was Protestant by then. So she was buried in the gutter outside.

Anna: In the gutter. Outside the door.

Ben: That's the legend, and the details vary in the retelling. Today a red stone slab lies in the gutter outside the chapel, often with flowers on it.

Anna: Ah. So is she the ghost?

Ben: We don't say that. We say it's a legend about a burial. We can't prove the coffin story. We can't prove the stone marks her grave. We just tell you what's told.

Anna: Legend, legend.

Ben: Legend. Stamped.

Anna: And the last beguine?

Ben: Not her. The last beguine was Sister Antonia, whose name was Agatha Kaptein. She died on 23 May 1971.

Anna: That's recent.

Ben: Yes. And that's the record.

Anna: The residents today?

Ben: Women, but no longer nuns. It's a quiet place, so we'll leave it quiet.

Anna: Tally. Ghosts, one, if you count the legend.

Ben: I count it as a legend. Real history, four. Legends, two.

Anna: I'm changing the tally. Record versus legend. Not ghosts.

Ben: Better.

Anna: Okay. Last stop. Where?

Ben: Back out through the passage. Go north-west, along the Singel canal, then across to the Keizersgracht. Number 123. It's a tall, elegant house on the canal, with six stone heads along its front.

Anna: Six heads. Of course.

Ben: And one of the oldest murder legends in town.

## Stop 6: The House with the Heads, Keizersgracht 123

Anna: Okay. We're on the canal. And look at that.

Ben: Look up. Six stone heads, set into the facade, just under the roof line.

Anna: They're staring.

Ben: They are. This is the Huis met de Hoofden. The House with the Heads. Keizersgracht 123.

Anna: So, what are they?

Ben: The record first. The house was built in 1622, for a wealthy merchant and art lover named Nicolaas Sohier. The facade is attributed to Hendrick de Keyser. But he died in 1621, so it was probably finished by his son, Pieter de Keyser.

Anna: Wait. The same De Keyser from the Zuiderkerk.

Ben: The same. Good callback.

Anna: I notice things.

Ben: The six heads are Roman gods. Each has a clue. Apollo has a laurel wreath, for the arts. Ceres has grain, for agriculture. Mercury has a winged helmet, for trade. Minerva stands for wisdom. Bacchus has grapes, for wine. And Diana has a half moon, for the hunt.

Anna: So it's an art collector's house, decorated with gods.

Ben: Yes. That's the record.

Anna: And the legend?

Ben: Here it is. It's the story told on the canal after dark. Six burglars broke into the house. A maid, alone, caught them, and cut off their heads. The six heads on the wall are the thieves.

Anna: Oh, that's grim. And a bit satisfying.

Ben: Careful. It's a legend. The record is the gods.

Anna: Legend. Stamp it.

Ben: Stamped. We found no source that supports it. It's the kind of story a house with six faces collects.

Anna: And since it's Halloween, can I say one thing? The gods are looking at us.

Ben: You may. That's atmosphere. Not fact.

Anna: Who lived here?

Ben: After Sohier, a man named Louis de Geer bought it in 1634, and his family held it for four generations. Later owners included Anthoni Grill, who rented it from 1752 to 1775. In 1811, Cornelis Sebille Roos settled here. The city restored it in 1907.

Anna: And now?

Ben: It houses the Embassy of the Free Mind, a museum and cultural platform. It's connected to a library of hermetic philosophy, the Bibliotheca Philosophica Hermetica. The buyer announced in 2006 was Joost Ritman.

Anna: A library of mystery. In a house of heads.

Ben: It does fit.

Anna: Okay, final tally?

Ben: Legends: the weeping wives, the miracle's details, Cornelia's gutter burial, and the six thieves. Everything else in the walk is record.

Anna: Four legends, and a heap of real history. And the real history was spookier.

Ben: Often is.
