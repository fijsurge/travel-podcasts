## Chapter 1: Before Luxembourg

Anna: Let's start with a puzzle, Ben. When was Luxembourg born?

Ben: Depends who you ask. Some people say 1815, when the Grand Duchy was created at the Congress of Vienna. Others say 1839, when its modern borders were drawn in London. Others point to 1867, when the great powers guaranteed its independence.

Anna: But you have a different answer.

Ben: The story we're telling really starts in the year nine sixty-three, with an exchange of land beside a river. And before that exchange, there are thousands of years we shouldn't skip.

Anna: Who's our first character?

Ben: He has no name. Archaeologists call him Loschbour man, after the rock shelter near the village of Heffingen where his skeleton was found in 1935. He lived about eight thousand years ago, when this land was covered in dense forest and people survived by hunting, fishing, and gathering.

Anna: And his DNA made headlines.

Ben: Decades after his discovery, when scientists sequenced it, the result suggested that this early European had dark skin and blue eyes, a combination that overturned a lot of assumptions about what the first Europeans looked like.

Anna: Who came after him?

Ben: Farmers, who arrived from the southeast and cleared fields along the river valleys. Then the age of metal, first bronze and later iron. By the last centuries before Christ, the land of Luxembourg belonged to a Celtic people called the Treveri.

Anna: And they left a monument.

Ben: A fortified hilltop town called the Titelberg, in the far southwest of today's country, close to the French border. It sat on a high plateau, with ramparts around it, and it was a center for metalworking and trade. Imagine a town of craftsmen and warriors, with a view across the countryside, and one of the richest cavalry traditions in Gaul.

Anna: Then Rome arrives.

Ben: The first written reference to this territory comes from Julius Caesar himself, in his account of the Gallic Wars. Around the middle of the first century before Christ, Caesar fought the Treveri, and after years of rebellion and repression, the region was swallowed into the Roman Empire.

Anna: And the Romans built a city nearby.

Ben: On the Moselle river, just across what's now the German border: Augusta Treverorum, today's Trier. Within a few generations it became one of the great cities of the western empire. At one point it was the home of emperors, and its Roman gates and baths still stand.

Anna: So what was Luxembourg's role?

Ben: Trier's backyard. Farmland and forest, crossed by Roman roads and dotted with country estates. One of those roads ran from the city of Reims, in what's now France, to Trier. Along it, near today's village of Dalheim, grew a small Roman town with a theater.

Anna: And villas.

Ben: At Echternach and at Diekirch, wealthy landowners built grand villas. At Diekirch, a Roman mosaic from one of those houses survives, laid out in colored stone. Life was settled, Latin-speaking, and for a few centuries, secure.

Anna: But the empire cracked.

Ben: In the third and fourth centuries, raiders pushed across the Rhine, and Roman power along the frontier began to wear away. By the end of the fifth century, the region had passed to the Franks, a Germanic people whose king, Clovis, was building a new kingdom out of the ruins of Roman Gaul. Luxembourg's land became part of the Frankish heartland, in the eastern half that was later called Austrasia.

Anna: And Christianity came with them.

Ben: With a handful of remarkable missionaries. The most important for our story is a monk named Willibrord, born in Northumbria, in England, and trained in Ireland. He crossed the sea to preach to the pagan Frisians, and in the year six ninety-eight he founded a monastery at Echternach, on land given to him by a noblewoman from the Trier area. Willibrord died in the year seven thirty-nine and was buried there.

Anna: Echternach became famous, didn't it?

Ben: One of the great intellectual centers of medieval Europe. Its monks copied and illuminated manuscripts so beautiful that they're treasured today in museums in Germany.

Anna: And there's the dancing procession.

Ben: Every year, on the Tuesday after Pentecost, thousands of people dance through the streets of Echternach, hopping in time to a simple polka-like tune, in honor of Saint Willibrord. UNESCO now recognizes it as part of humanity's living cultural heritage.

Anna: Then we reach Charlemagne.

Ben: In the late eighth and early ninth centuries, the Frankish kings became emperors, and the Echternach region sat comfortably inside their realm. But when Charlemagne's empire was split among his grandsons by the Treaty of Verdun in eight forty-three, the land that would become Luxembourg fell into the middle kingdom, named after the grandson Lothar.

Anna: And that didn't last.

Ben: Over the following decades it broke up, and the region we're talking about became a borderland, a long-contested strip between the western Frankish kingdom, which would become France, and the eastern Frankish kingdom, which would become Germany.

Anna: And then a new emperor.

Ben: In the year nine sixty-two, in Rome, a German king named Otto was crowned emperor. His realm would later be known as the Holy Roman Empire, a loose, shifting collection of kingdoms, duchies, counties, and church lands that would last for more than eight hundred years. And just one year later, a count named Siegfried made a small land deal that no one at the time could have thought important.

Anna: Who was Siegfried?

Ben: He belonged to a noble family from the Moselle and Ardennes region, and according to tradition he had Carolingian blood. What he wanted was a stronghold. On the edge of lands he controlled, there was a rocky promontory rising above the river Alzette, with the ruins of an old fortification.

Anna: Which belonged to someone else.

Ben: To the abbey of Saint Maximin in Trier. Siegfried offered other lands in exchange, and the monks agreed.

Anna: And the charter survives.

Ben: Dated nine sixty-three. It gives the name of the place: Lucilinburhuc, which means roughly little castle. In time, the name would shorten and soften into Luxembourg.

Anna: And the rock itself?

Ben: It would later be known as the Bock. Siegfried built a real fortress on it, and from there his family would spread out across the surrounding land. The place was so well chosen that armies would fight over it for the next nine hundred years.

Anna: And there's a legend, of course.

Ben: Of course. It says that Siegfried married a mermaid named Melusina, who agreed to live with him on the condition that he never look at her on a certain day. He broke the promise, and she vanished beneath the waters of the Alzette, leaving him alone.

Anna: And locals say she can still be seen.

Ben: Once every seven years, spinning at the bottom of the river.

Anna: But the real story is better than the legend.

Ben: I think so. Because from this small rocky outcrop, Siegfried's descendants would rise to the heights of European power. They would sit on the throne of Bohemia and wear the imperial crown.

Anna: In the next chapter, we meet the House of Luxembourg.

Ben: And it's quite a family.

## Chapter 2: The House of Luxembourg, 963 to 1443

Anna: So for its first two centuries, the county was a modest affair.

Ben: A rocky fortress, a handful of villages, and a dynasty that married well and waited. The counts of Luxembourg slowly gathered land, and in 1136 a stroke of inheritance gave the county to Henry the Blind, count of Namur, who joined the two territories together.

Anna: And his daughter was remarkable.

Ben: Ermesinde. She inherited the county in 1196 and ruled for more than fifty years, through two marriages and a great deal of politics. She's remembered for granting charters of freedom to the towns of her county.

Anna: Including the capital.

Ben: In 1244, she gave one to the city of Luxembourg. It freed its townspeople from many of the old obligations of serfdom and let them organize themselves as a community, with a council of their own. In a Europe where most people lived under their lord's thumb, that was a bold step. To this day, Luxembourg treats Ermesinde as one of its founding figures.

Anna: Now the small county leaps onto the continental stage.

Ben: In 1288, the count of Luxembourg was killed at the Battle of Worringen, in a war over the succession to the neighboring duchy of Limburg. His heir was a boy of about thirteen, also named Henry. We know him as Henry the Seventh.

Anna: And he grew up admired.

Ben: Chivalrous, pious, and quietly ambitious. And in 1308, after the German king was murdered, the electors of the empire looked around for someone who was capable but not too powerful. They chose the count of Luxembourg.

Anna: That's astonishing. A lord from a small county on the western edge.

Ben: Suddenly king of the Germans. And Henry seized the chance. He arranged for his teenage son John to marry Elisabeth, the heiress of the kingdom of Bohemia, which is roughly today's Czech Republic. John became King of Bohemia in 1310. In one stroke, the House of Luxembourg had acquired one of the richest crowns in central Europe.

Anna: And Henry himself?

Ben: He marched over the Alps into Italy, where he was crowned emperor in Rome in 1312. He dreamed of restoring imperial glory. The poet Dante, who lived through those years and pinned his hopes on Henry, wrote that a seat was waiting for him in Paradise. But Henry died of a fever in 1313, near the city of Siena, and the dream died with him.

Anna: And his son John had a very different life.

Ben: Known to history as John the Blind, he was one of the great adventurers of the Middle Ages. He was born in Luxembourg and raised partly at the French court. As King of Bohemia, he was almost never home. He fought in Italy, in Poland, and in Lithuania, alongside the crusading Teutonic Knights.

Anna: He loved tournaments.

Ben: And he loved France. His daughter Bonne married the man who would become King John the Second of France, and she became the grandmother of a future French king.

Anna: When did he go blind?

Ben: By around 1340 he had lost his sight completely. It didn't slow him down. In 1340 he founded a fair in Luxembourg City that still exists today, the Schueberfouer. It takes place every year in late August and early September, with rides, food stalls, and traditional parades, and it's the biggest funfair in the country.

Anna: Then the war that defined his end.

Ben: In 1346, England and France were fighting what we now call the Hundred Years' War, and John, loyal to the French, rode to the battlefield of Crécy. Blind, he asked his knights to lead him into the fight. According to the old account, they tied their horses together by the reins, so that he wouldn't be separated, and charged straight into the English archers.

Anna: And the next morning.

Ben: John and his companions were found dead on the field.

Anna: And the story about the feathers.

Ben: Tradition says that the English prince, the one we call the Black Prince, who was just sixteen, took the three ostrich feathers from John's helmet as a trophy, and made them his own emblem. Historians argue about whether that's really true. But in Luxembourg, John is remembered as the great national hero. His body now rests in the crypt of Notre-Dame cathedral in the capital, and to Luxembourgers he's a symbol of bravery, loyalty, and a certain doomed gallantry.

Anna: Now the greatest of the Luxembourgers.

Ben: John's eldest son. He was born in Prague and baptized with the name Wenceslas, but he took the name Charles at the French court, in honor of his godfather and uncle. As Charles the Fourth, he became King of Bohemia after his father's death, and in 1355 he was crowned Holy Roman Emperor in Rome.

Anna: He was a different kind of emperor.

Ben: A scholar, a builder, and a patient politician. In 1356 he issued a famous decree called the Golden Bull, which fixed how emperors would be elected by seven electors, a system that more or less lasted until the empire itself ended. He founded Charles University in Prague in 1348, the first university in central Europe.

Anna: And the bridge.

Ben: He started building the stone bridge across the Vltava that still bears his name. He turned Prague into one of the great cities of Europe. And he didn't forget the little county where his family began. In 1354, Charles raised Luxembourg to the rank of duchy, and installed his half-brother Wenceslas as its first duke.

Anna: So by the late fourteenth century, this is a great dynasty.

Ben: Its sons ruled Bohemia, and for a time Hungary, and sat on the imperial throne. In all, four Luxembourgers held the title of ruler of the Holy Roman Empire: Henry the Seventh, Charles the Fourth, Charles's son Wenceslas the Fourth, and Charles's other son Sigismund. Only three of them, Henry, Charles, and Sigismund, were actually crowned emperor in Rome.

Anna: And Sigismund was the last.

Ben: A shrewd and restless man. He was King of Hungary from 1387, and he became King of the Romans in 1411. He called the great Council of Constance, which met from 1414 to 1418 and ended a split in the Catholic Church, and which also burned the Czech reformer Jan Hus, an act that triggered the Hussite wars in Bohemia.

Anna: And then the line ended.

Ben: Sigismund was crowned emperor in 1433, and he died in 1437. He had no surviving son. With his death, the male line of the House of Luxembourg came to an end.

Anna: What happened to the little duchy in all this?

Ben: Its glamorous rulers were scattered across central Europe. They spent most of their time in Prague, Buda, or Rome, and they rarely visited the place that had made them. To them, Luxembourg was a source of money, and they treated it that way. They mortgaged it. They pledged it as security for loans, and passed it around among cousins and creditors.

Anna: Who ended up holding it?

Ben: One of them was a woman named Elisabeth of Görlitz, a niece of Sigismund. She was widowed, remarried, and constantly short of money. In the end, she sold her rights to the most powerful figure in the region: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy.

Anna: And that's how it passed to Burgundy.

Ben: In 1443, his forces took possession of the city, reportedly by a surprise assault with the help of local nobles who were fed up with the unstable rule of the pledge-holders. And just like that, the old era came to an end. The county that had given Europe emperors and kings was now just one more piece in the growing Burgundian collection of lands.

Anna: Before we move on, there's a thread worth remembering. The castles.

Ben: In the countryside of Luxembourg, medieval lords built castles that still stand. The most spectacular is Vianden, on a hill above a river in the north, one of the largest feudal residences in this part of Europe. In the early fifteenth century, Vianden passed by inheritance to the family of Nassau, which would later become the Dutch royal house.

Anna: Keep that name in mind.

Ben: Four hundred years later, it'll be central to our story.

## Chapter 3: The Gibraltar of the North, 1443 to 1815

Anna: If the first part of the story was about a dynasty, this part is about a rock.

Ben: For the next three and a half centuries, Luxembourg's main job in European politics was to be a fortress. And it was a good one. A great deal of the time, the Luxembourgers themselves weren't the ones deciding who owned it.

Anna: We left it with Philip the Good of Burgundy in 1443.

Ben: And for a while Luxembourg was one piece in a glittering Burgundian empire that included Flanders, Brabant, and Holland. Burgundy was the richest, most fashionable court in Europe. But it didn't last. In 1477, Philip's grandson Charles the Bold was killed in battle outside the city of Nancy, leaving a single daughter, Mary.

Anna: Who married Maximilian of Habsburg.

Ben: And through that marriage the Burgundian lands, Luxembourg included, passed to the Habsburg family. That was the beginning of an era in which Luxembourg belonged to the most powerful dynasty in the world. Maximilian and Mary's grandson was born in Ghent in the year 1500 and grew up to be Charles the Fifth, who ruled Spain, much of Italy, the Netherlands, Germany, and the new Spanish lands in the Americas.

Anna: And Luxembourg was a tiny corner of all that.

Ben: But it sat right on the road between his Netherlands and his Italian and Spanish possessions, so it mattered.

Anna: What happened when Charles retired?

Ben: His empire was divided. His son Philip the Second took Spain and the Netherlands, and Luxembourg went with them. That put Luxembourg in the front row for one of the great conflicts of the age. The northern provinces of the Netherlands rose in revolt, turned Protestant, and eventually broke away to become the Dutch Republic.

Anna: But not the south.

Ben: The southern provinces, including Luxembourg, stayed under Spain and stayed Catholic. Luxembourg's people had barely been touched by the Reformation, and they remained loyal.

Anna: And the seventeenth century was brutal.

Ben: During the Thirty Years' War, armies marched back and forth across the Luxembourg countryside, looting and burning. Famine and plague followed the soldiers. In some parts of the country, the population fell by something like half. Meanwhile Spain was declining, and France under the Sun King, Louis the Fourteenth, was rising.

Anna: And France began nibbling.

Ben: In 1659, the peace treaty between Spain and France handed over a belt of towns along Luxembourg's southern border, including Thionville. It was the first of several times that Luxembourg would shrink.

Anna: Then Louis came for the fortress itself.

Ben: In 1684. French forces besieged the city for about a month and took it. And here the history of the place changes. Louis brought the great military engineer Marquis de Vauban, the greatest builder of fortresses of his age, to rebuild the defenses.

Anna: What did Vauban do?

Ben: He dug ditches, raised massive walls, and tunneled deep into the rock. Within a few years, the capital of Luxembourg was one of the most formidable fortresses in Europe, with a nickname that would stick: the Gibraltar of the North.

Anna: But the French didn't keep it.

Ben: In 1697, after a long war, Louis gave Luxembourg back to Spain in the Treaty of Ryswick. France held it again briefly in the early eighteenth century, and when the great war over the Spanish throne was settled in 1713 and 1714, Luxembourg was handed to the Austrian branch of the Habsburgs, together with what is now Belgium. Those lands became the Austrian Netherlands, and Luxembourg was a part of them.

Anna: How was the Austrian period?

Ben: About eight decades, sometimes remembered as a relatively calm and prosperous time. Historians still debate whether it was really a golden age, but the evidence of peace is clear enough. The population recovered. Ironworks along the rivers produced cast iron, and farming improved.

Anna: And the fortress got bigger.

Ben: The Austrians kept strengthening it, and in the seventeen-thirties they built a new fort on the plateau across the valley, called Fort Thüngen. Its restored towers, nicknamed the Three Acorns, still stand today, and now they house a museum.

Anna: But the real wonder is hidden.

Ben: Over the generations, engineers dug a vast network of tunnels, called casemates, into the sandstone cliff of the Bock. They provided shelter for soldiers, space for cannon, and storage for supplies. At the height of the fortress, there were many miles of tunnels. Today, roughly seventeen kilometers of them survive, about ten and a half miles, and visitors can still walk through them and look out over the valley through the gun slits.

Anna: The fortress changed hands so often.

Ben: Its history reads like a revolving door. If you were an ambitious king, Luxembourg was a prize. If you lived in Luxembourg, it was a burden. The city was stuffed with soldiers, the townspeople paid for the upkeep of the walls, and every time a war broke out between France and its neighbors, armies tramped across your fields.

Anna: And the quiet ended in the seventeen-eighties.

Ben: Emperor Joseph the Second, who ruled from Vienna, was a reformer of the enlightened kind. He tried to modernize his lands all at once, closing monasteries, limiting the church's power, and reorganizing the courts. Many of his subjects in the Austrian Netherlands, including Luxembourg, were conservative and devout, and they were furious. In 1789, the same year the French Revolution began, a revolt broke out in the Netherlands. It was suppressed within a couple of years.

Anna: But the French Revolution wasn't so easily suppressed.

Ben: In 1794, French revolutionary armies swept through the Austrian Netherlands. The Luxembourg fortress held out. For about seven months, from the autumn of 1794, French troops surrounded it while Austrian defenders waited for relief that never came.

Anna: And it fell.

Ben: In June 1795, starving and cut off, the garrison surrendered. The Gibraltar of the North had fallen, and not to a great assault but to hunger.

Anna: What happened to the duchy?

Ben: In the autumn of 1795, France formally annexed the territory. The old duchy of Luxembourg ceased to exist, and in its place the French created a new administrative unit called the Department of the Forests, named for the heavily wooded Ardennes hills. It was ruled from the city of Luxembourg.

Anna: And the French years brought change.

Ben: Profound change. The old feudal rights were abolished. The church lost much of its land. A new system of laws, the Napoleonic Code, was introduced, and traces of it survive in Luxembourg's legal system to this day.

Anna: But there was a price.

Ben: A heavy one. The French demanded soldiers. Young men from Luxembourg were drafted into Napoleon's armies, and many never came home. In 1798, the conscription law sparked a revolt in the countryside of the north. Peasants armed with clubs, farm tools, and old hunting guns rose up against the French.

Anna: The Peasants' War.

Ben: Or more literally, the War of the Clubs. It was crushed within weeks, and the leaders were punished harshly. But the memory of it has never faded. For generations, it was a powerful reminder of how deeply the ordinary people of Luxembourg resented foreign rule.

Anna: And then 1814.

Ben: After Napoleon's disastrous retreat from Russia and his defeat at Leipzig, the French withdrew. For the first time in centuries, the future of Luxembourg was up for negotiation among the great powers of Europe.

Anna: In the next chapter, they make a decision that creates something no one had ever seen before.

Ben: A grand duchy.

## Chapter 4: A Grand Duchy Is Born, 1815 to 1867

Anna: Picture the scene in Vienna in the winter of 1814 and 1815.

Ben: Napoleon has been defeated and exiled. The diplomats of Europe, kings, princes, and ministers in glittering uniforms, gather for the Congress of Vienna to redraw the map. Their main goal is simple: make sure France can never again conquer its neighbors. And one of the ways to do that is to build a strong state on France's northern border.

Anna: So they combine the Netherlands and Belgium.

Ben: Into a single United Kingdom of the Netherlands, ruled by William of Orange-Nassau, who became King William the First. William's family had lost its ancestral German lands, so the great powers looked for ways to compensate him. One of them was Luxembourg.

Anna: And they made it a state in its own right.

Ben: They raised it to the rank of grand duchy, which was a way of saying it was more than an ordinary duchy, a proper sovereign state with a seat in the new German Confederation. William would be both King of the Netherlands and Grand Duke of Luxembourg, a so-called personal union, in which the same man wore two crowns over two countries that were otherwise separate.

Anna: And that's 1815.

Ben: The Grand Duchy of Luxembourg came into being. It remains the only grand duchy in the world.

Anna: But it was smaller than the old duchy.

Ben: Prussia, the rising power on the Rhine, demanded land, and Luxembourg lost its entire eastern border region, the territory east of the rivers Moselle, Sûre, and Our. That was about a quarter of its area, and it was the second time in the modern era that the country had been carved up.

Anna: And the fortress?

Ben: It became a fortress of the German Confederation, and a Prussian garrison moved in. The Grand Duke ruled the country. The Prussians held the rock.

Anna: Then 1830, and the great shock.

Ben: In Brussels, riots broke out after an opera performance, and within weeks the southern provinces of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands had risen up in a revolution. They were Catholic and mostly French-speaking, and they resented rule by a Protestant, Dutch-speaking king. They declared independence as the new Kingdom of Belgium.

Anna: And Luxembourg?

Ben: Most of Luxembourg joined them. Luxembourgers took part in the uprising, sent representatives to the Belgian national congress, and for several years almost the whole country was governed from Brussels. The only exception was the capital, where the Prussian garrison held the fortress firmly in the name of the German Confederation.

Anna: So it was unresolved for a long time.

Ben: Nearly a decade. Belgium claimed all of it. The Dutch king claimed all of it. The great powers met in London and eventually proposed a compromise. In 1839, after King William finally accepted defeat, a treaty was signed in London that carved the country in two.

Anna: Who got what?

Ben: The western part of Luxembourg, the French-speaking part, went to Belgium. It included the towns of Arlon and Bastogne, a name we'll meet again in the Second World War. That was roughly sixty-five percent of the country's territory, and about half its population, something like one hundred and seventy-five thousand people. What remained was the eastern, German-speaking part, essentially the Luxembourg of today.

Anna: Historians shake their heads about how it was done.

Ben: The line was drawn in London, using maps that were out of date and inaccurate, by diplomats who had never visited the places they were dividing. To compensate the German Confederation for the loss, part of the Dutch province of Limburg was attached to it. And the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg, which had been a middling European territory, became a small, landlocked, mostly poor country with about one hundred and seventy thousand inhabitants.

Anna: But there's a twist.

Ben: The treaty of 1839 is often described as the moment Luxembourg's independence began, because it fixed the borders. For the first time, there was a clear, internationally recognized line around a state called Luxembourg, the same line you can cross on a bridge or a country road today. It was a tragic loss, but it was also a beginning.

Anna: How were the decades that followed?

Ben: Hard. Luxembourg was a farming country with little industry, and in the eighteen-forties a potato blight and poor harvests pushed many families into hunger. In 1842, the Grand Duchy joined the German customs union, the Zollverein, which tied its economy to the German states and opened up markets for its goods. But it also meant that Luxembourg's economic fate was linked to its neighbor across the Moselle.

Anna: And many people left.

Ben: Between about 1840 and 1890, tens of thousands of Luxembourgers emigrated, many to the United States, where they settled in the Midwest, especially in Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, and the area around Chicago. For a country of fewer than two hundred thousand people, this was an enormous drain.

Anna: Meanwhile, the country was learning to govern itself.

Ben: In 1841, William the Second, the king who followed William the First, granted Luxembourg its first constitution, creating an assembly of the estates. In 1848, the year revolutions shook Europe, a more liberal constitution followed. The Dutch kings weren't very interested in their grand duchy. William the Third, who ruled from 1849, rarely visited, and day to day the country was run by his brother Prince Henry as his representative.

Anna: And a national consciousness woke up.

Ben: Writers and poets began composing in Luxembourgish, the Moselle Franconian dialect that ordinary people spoke at home and that had no official status. In 1859, the poet Michel Lentz wrote a song that Luxembourgers still sing, called the Feierwon, or the Fire Wagon, about the arrival of the railway.

Anna: And the refrain became a motto.

Ben: It says, in Luxembourgish, Mir wëlle bleiwe wat mir sinn, which means, We want to remain what we are. In the same decade, Lentz wrote the words to the song that would become the national anthem, Ons Heemecht, or Our Homeland, officially adopted in 1895. And in 1872, Michel Rodange published Renert, a long satirical poem in Luxembourgish about a crafty fox, which many people consider the country's national epic.

Anna: Now we come to the crisis that nearly started a war.

Ben: In 1866, Prussia defeated Austria in a short, decisive war, and the German Confederation, which had existed since 1815, was dissolved. Suddenly, no one was sure what Luxembourg's status was. Was it part of Germany? Was it independent? Who owned the fortress, with its Prussian garrison?

Anna: And Napoleon the Third saw an opportunity.

Ben: Prussia had just become dramatically more powerful, and he wanted something in return to keep the balance, and to restore French prestige. He offered to buy Luxembourg from King William the Third of the Netherlands, who was short of money and had little emotional attachment to the place. William agreed in principle.

Anna: And Bismarck?

Ben: Otto von Bismarck, the Prussian chancellor, wasn't going to let France take a strategic fortress on the German frontier. Bismarck made sure that news of the sale leaked, and German public opinion exploded. Luxembourg is German soil, politicians and newspapers cried. For a few weeks in the spring of 1867, Europe stood at the edge of war.

Anna: How did it end?

Ben: Diplomacy won out. The great powers met in London in May 1867, and they agreed on a settlement that satisfied everyone and no one. The second Treaty of London, signed on the eleventh of May, declared that Luxembourg was an independent state, under the sovereignty of the House of Orange-Nassau, and that it would be perpetually neutral, under the collective guarantee of the great powers.

Anna: And the fortress?

Ben: The Prussian garrison would leave. And the fortress, the Gibraltar of the North, would be dismantled, so that no one could use it as a weapon against their neighbors.

## Chapter 5: Neutrality, Iron and a New Dynasty, 1867 to 1914

Anna: The treaty of 1867 promised the fortress would be taken apart, and it kept its word.

Ben: Over the next sixteen years, work crews tore down walls, filled in ditches, and blew up forts and bastions. It was one of the biggest demolition projects in Europe. And when it was done, a city that had been a cramped military stronghold for centuries was suddenly free to grow.

Anna: So what did it do with the space?

Ben: Parks, boulevards, and building plots. New districts spread across the plateau, and the deep valleys that had been obstacles became green spaces. In 1903, the city opened the Adolphe Bridge, which sweeps across the Pétrusse valley in two graceful stone arches. At the time, it was one of the largest stone arch bridges in the world.

Anna: And not everything was destroyed.

Ben: Parts of the casemates survived, and so did the old town on its cliffs. In 1994, UNESCO recognized the old quarters and the surviving fortifications as a World Heritage site. The Gibraltar of the North had become, in the end, a beautiful ruin.

Anna: But neutrality was a gamble.

Ben: A real one. The great powers had guaranteed it together, which in practice meant that if anyone broke it, no single power was obliged to punish them. And the country had almost no army, only a small corps of volunteers and gendarmes. Luxembourg's safety depended on the goodwill of its neighbors.

Anna: While the diplomats worried, something was happening underground.

Ben: In the south of Luxembourg, running down into the French region of Lorraine, there is a belt of iron ore called minette, a reddish-brown stone. Local blacksmiths had known about it for centuries, but it was nearly useless, because it contained a lot of phosphorus, which made the iron brittle.

Anna: Until 1878.

Ben: That year, an Englishman named Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist developed a way to remove the phosphorus. Suddenly the poor ore of Luxembourg and Lorraine could be made into excellent steel.

Anna: An industrial revolution in miniature.

Ben: Blast furnaces went up along the Alzette and across the southwest corner of the country. Villages like Esch, Differdange, Dudelange and Rumelange swelled into industrial towns. The night sky over the south turned orange and red dust settled on everything. Within a generation, the country that had been exporting farmers to America was importing workers.

Anna: From where?

Ben: First from Germany, then from Italy. Italian laborers began arriving in large numbers in the eighteen-nineties and settled in the steel towns. They were the first of many waves. Luxembourg had a long tradition of sending its sons and daughters abroad. Now, for the first time, it began to receive people.

Anna: And the companies consolidated.

Ben: In 1911, the main steel producers merged into a single giant called ARBED, a name built from the French words for the united steelworks of Burbach, Eich and Dudelange. For most of the twentieth century, ARBED was practically a state within the state. Per head of population, few places in the world made more steel.

Anna: And who ran the country during all this?

Ben: A careful lawyer named Paul Eyschen, head of the government from 1888 until 1915, an extraordinary twenty-seven years. He guided Luxembourg through industrialization and the pressures of its neighbors.

Anna: Now for the strangest episode, the change of dynasty.

Ben: In 1890, King William the Third of the Netherlands died. He had been King of the Netherlands and Grand Duke of Luxembourg for forty-one years. But he had no surviving sons, only a ten-year-old daughter, Wilhelmina.

Anna: Who became Queen of the Netherlands.

Ben: But in Luxembourg, an old family law, the Nassau family pact, said only men could inherit. So the Grand Duchy and the Netherlands, united for seventy-five years, separated.

Anna: And the crown went to a cousin.

Ben: A distant one, named Adolphe, the former Duke of Nassau. His own duchy in Germany had been swallowed by Prussia in 1866, and he'd been living in exile. Now, at the age of seventy-three, he became Grand Duke of Luxembourg. For the first time, the country had a ruler who lived there, whose family belonged to it, and who wasn't the king of someone else. It was the beginning of the modern Luxembourg monarchy.

Anna: But that family had a succession problem too.

Ben: Adolphe reigned until 1905. His son William the Fourth followed, and he had no sons, only daughters. So parliament changed the rules in 1907 to allow women to inherit. When William died in 1912, his eldest daughter, Marie-Adélaïde, became Grand Duchess at eighteen.

Anna: The first woman to rule Luxembourg in her own right was a teenager.

Ben: And in the same years, this tiny country produced a few remarkable people who made their names abroad. In 1879, a boy named Edward Steichen was born in the village of Bivange. His family emigrated to the United States when he was a toddler, and he grew up to be one of the greatest photographers of the twentieth century.

Anna: And he organized a famous exhibition.

Ben: The Family of Man, in 1955. It toured the world, and today it is permanently displayed in a castle in the northern town of Clervaux.

Anna: Who else?

Ben: The physicist Gabriel Lippmann won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1908 for a method of reproducing colors in photographs. And Hugo Gernsback, born in Luxembourg City in 1884, moved to the United States in 1904 and published some of the first magazines devoted to what he called scientifiction. We call it science fiction. The Hugo Awards are named after him.

Anna: So by 1914...

Ben: Luxembourg was prosperous, stable, proud, and increasingly sure of its identity. It had a language its writers loved, a dynasty that was its own, and a fast-growing economy. But it still sat between France, Belgium and Germany, and the guarantee of the great powers was only as strong as their promises.

Anna: And in the summer of 1914, a crisis in the Balkans pushed Europe toward catastrophe.

Ben: The neutrality of Luxembourg was about to be tested.

## Chapter 6: Two World Wars, 1914 to 1945

Anna: On the second of August, 1914, German soldiers crossed the border into Luxembourg.

Ben: By road and by rail. They seized the capital's railway station and the key bridges within hours. It was a plain violation of the treaty of 1867. Germany claimed it was only protecting the railways, which were vital to its plan to invade France through Belgium.

Anna: And Luxembourg could do nothing.

Ben: The government protested, but it had no army worth the name. A popular story says that the young Grand Duchess Marie-Adélaïde drove to the Adolphe Bridge and tried to block it with her car. Whether or not that's exactly true, it captures the helplessness of the moment. A country that had trusted a piece of paper for fifty years was now occupied.

Anna: How strange was the occupation?

Ben: Strange. Germany let the Luxembourg government keep operating and promised not to harm the country. But the economy was tied into the German war machine, food grew scarce, and thousands of young men slipped away to join the French army, fighting under the French flag against the occupiers of their homeland.

Anna: And the Grand Duchess was in an impossible position.

Ben: She was very young, very Catholic, and very conservative. She stayed in the country and kept up official contacts with the occupiers, including meeting the German Crown Prince. When the war ended in November 1918, those meetings came back to haunt her. France and Belgium especially accused her of being pro-German. Crowds demonstrated against her, and the French and the Americans refused to deal with her.

Anna: So she stepped down.

Ben: In January 1919, she abdicated in favor of her younger sister Charlotte, who was twenty-two. Marie-Adélaïde left the country, entered a convent in Italy, and died in 1924, aged only twenty-nine.

Anna: But the monarchy itself was now in question.

Ben: In September 1919, Luxembourgers voted in a double referendum. The first question asked whether they wanted to keep the dynasty, and about seventy-eight percent said yes. The second asked what kind of economic union they wanted with a larger neighbor, and about seventy-three percent chose France.

Anna: But France said no.

Ben: So Luxembourg turned to Belgium. In 1921, the two formed an economic union, with a shared customs area and a Luxembourg franc tied to the Belgian franc. It lasted until both countries adopted the euro.

Anna: And 1919 brought a political revolution too.

Ben: A revised constitution introduced universal suffrage, including women. That year, women voted in Luxembourg for the first time, and a woman was elected to parliament.

Anna: What were the next two decades like?

Ben: Quiet transformation. Charlotte, a poised and popular young monarch, married Prince Felix of Bourbon-Parma in 1919, and their son Jean was born in 1921. The family became beloved symbols of national unity. The steel industry prospered, then suffered in the Depression. And in 1929, a new law made the country unusually attractive for holding companies, the first small step toward its future as a financial center.

Anna: And radio.

Ben: In 1933, Radio Luxembourg began broadcasting from the country with a powerful transmitter and programs in several languages. It was a business as much as a broadcaster, selling advertising across the borders, and in time it became one of the most famous radio stations in Europe.

Anna: But the shadow of Germany was growing again.

Ben: And on the tenth of May, 1940, it fell. German forces invaded at dawn as part of their attack on France and the Low Countries. The country was overrun in a single day. Grand Duchess Charlotte and her government escaped across the border into France, then to Portugal, then to London. Charlotte spent much of the war in exile in Britain and Canada, and her BBC radio addresses were heard in secret by people in the occupied country. They gave courage to many.

Anna: And this occupation was worse than the first.

Ben: Far worse. Hitler's Germany didn't treat Luxembourg as a neutral occupied country. It treated it as German. Gustav Simon, the Nazi governor of the neighboring Koblenz-Trier district, was given authority over Luxembourg in the summer of 1940, and his goal was to make the country fully German.

Anna: What did that look like?

Ben: Thorough and petty at once. The French language was banned. People were told to change their names, street signs were repainted, and even a polite merci had to become danke. Political parties and trade unions were abolished, and Luxembourgers were declared to be Germans of the Moselle region who would sooner or later be expected to join the German war effort.

Anna: Then the census.

Ben: In October 1941, the Nazi authorities arranged a census with three questions: your nationality, your mother tongue, and your ethnicity. They expected everyone to answer German, which would prove Luxembourg was a German land. But resistance organizers spread the word quietly, and the answer they urged was Luxembourgish, three times. More than nine in ten people wrote Luxembourgish on every line. The Germans were furious and canceled the census.

Anna: And the Jewish community?

Ben: It numbered a few thousand before the war, and it was persecuted from the first days of the occupation. Many managed to flee, but more than a thousand were deported to ghettos and camps in the East, and only a small number survived.

Anna: And then the summer of 1942.

Ben: On the thirtieth of August, Simon announced that young Luxembourgers would be drafted into the German army. Within hours a spontaneous general strike began. It started in the northern town of Wiltz and spread to the steel towns, the mines, the schools, the government offices. Workers walked off their jobs and trains stopped.

Anna: How did the Nazis respond?

Ben: Simon declared a state of emergency, and the Gestapo moved in. A special court sentenced about twenty strikers to death, and they were shot within days. The strike was crushed, but it remains one of the proudest moments in Luxembourg's national memory, an act of mass defiance in the middle of a brutal occupation.

Anna: And the draft went ahead anyway.

Ben: Around twelve thousand Luxembourgers were forced to serve in the German army. About thirty-five hundred deserted or went into hiding, and their families were deported to labor camps in Germany and Silesia. Somewhere between twenty-five hundred and twenty-eight hundred conscripts were killed or went missing at the front. It divided families and left a scar that took generations to heal.

Anna: But many Luxembourgers fought on the other side too.

Ben: A small Luxembourg unit served in the Allied armies in Britain. And Charlotte's son, Prince Jean, joined the British Army's Irish Guards and took part in the advance after the Normandy landings.

Anna: Liberation came in September 1944.

Ben: American troops entered Luxembourg City on the tenth, to scenes of wild celebration. But the ordeal wasn't over. In December, Hitler launched his last great offensive in the Ardennes forests, the Battle of the Bulge, and the fighting swept across northern Luxembourg. Wiltz, Clervaux and Vianden were battered. Wiltz, the cradle of the 1942 strike, was nearly destroyed and earned the title of martyr town.

Anna: And Patton was there.

Ben: General Patton's Third Army made its headquarters in Luxembourg City, and from there launched the counterattack that helped save the besieged town of Bastogne, just across the border in Belgium. When the battle ended in January 1945, Luxembourg was free again, but exhausted and scarred.

Anna: Patton himself is buried there.

Ben: He died in December 1945 after a car accident in Germany, and he was buried, at his own wish, among his soldiers, in the American military cemetery at Hamm, just outside the capital. More than five thousand American servicemen rest there today.

Anna: So in barely a quarter of a century, Luxembourg had been invaded twice by its giant neighbor.

Ben: The old strategy of neutrality, of staying quiet and hoping to be left alone, had failed twice, catastrophically. The country needed a new way to survive.

Anna: And it found one. Next time, we meet the Luxembourgers who decided the answer was not to hide from Europe, but to help build it.

## Chapter 7: Rebuilding and Founding Europe, 1945 to 1980

Anna: When the war ended, Luxembourg drew a conclusion that would shape the rest of its history.

Ben: A small country cannot protect itself by standing alone. Neutrality had been tried and had failed twice. So the Grand Duchy threw itself into international cooperation with an enthusiasm few larger countries could match.

Anna: It started before the war even ended.

Ben: In September 1944, with the exiled governments of Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg still in London, they signed an agreement to create a customs union. The name was a combination of their initials: Benelux. Fourteen years later, they turned it into a full economic union.

Anna: Then a flurry of firsts.

Ben: In 1945, Luxembourg became a founding member of the United Nations. In 1948, it rewrote its constitution to remove the clause that made it perpetually neutral. And in 1949, it was one of the twelve founding members of NATO. For a country with a tiny army, that was a bold declaration. Luxembourg was firmly part of the Western camp.

Anna: But the most important step had Luxembourg connections of a different kind.

Ben: Robert Schuman was born in 1886 in Clausen, a neighborhood of Luxembourg City, to a Luxembourg mother and a father from the French region of Lorraine. He grew up speaking German, French and Luxembourgish, studied in Germany, and eventually became a French politician. After the Second World War he served as France's foreign minister.

Anna: And on the ninth of May, 1950...

Ben: Schuman proposed that France and Germany, who had fought three wars in less than a century, should place their coal and steel production under a single common authority, open to other European countries. If the raw materials of war were jointly controlled, a new war between the two would become unthinkable. That is the Schuman Declaration, and May ninth is celebrated today as Europe Day.

Anna: And the result was...

Ben: The European Coal and Steel Community, set up in 1951 by six countries: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. Luxembourg was a natural participant. It made more steel per head than almost anyone, ARBED was one of the giants of the sector, and its leaders had learned that tying themselves to bigger neighbors in shared institutions was the best way to stay free.

Anna: And there was another key figure.

Ben: Joseph Bech, Luxembourg's foreign minister for more than thirty years, from 1926 to 1958. In 1955, at a conference in the Sicilian town of Messina, he helped push forward a plan for a wider common market. That led to the Treaties of Rome in 1957, which created the European Economic Community, the direct ancestor of today's European Union.

Anna: Small government, skilled diplomats.

Ben: They made themselves useful as mediators between France and Germany. And Luxembourg was rewarded. The High Authority of the Coal and Steel Community was based there from 1952. So was the Court of Justice, which still sits in Luxembourg today. In 1968, the European Investment Bank set up its headquarters there, and in 1977 the European Court of Auditors followed. Parts of the European Parliament's administration are there too, along with Eurostat, the EU's statistics office.

Anna: In a continent of far larger capitals...

Ben: The little city on the Alzette had quietly become one of the capitals of Europe.

Anna: And it lent its name to a famous diplomatic fix.

Ben: In 1965, France's president Charles de Gaulle was furious about plans to give the European institutions more power, and he boycotted meetings for months. That was the empty chair crisis. It was settled in January 1966 at a meeting in Luxembourg. The deal, known as the Luxembourg Compromise, effectively let a country block a decision if its vital national interests were at stake. For decades afterwards it shaped how Europe made decisions.

Anna: Back home, the country was rebuilding.

Ben: The north, devastated by the Battle of the Bulge, was rebuilt town by town. Steel boomed in the postwar decades, as Europe needed girders, rails and machinery. Wages rose, new housing went up, and the standard of living climbed steadily.

Anna: And the throne changed hands.

Ben: Grand Duchess Charlotte, who had reigned for forty-five years and become the symbol of the country's survival, abdicated on the twelfth of November, 1964, in favor of her son Jean. Jean, who had fought alongside the Allies, was forty-three, and he would reign for thirty-six years.

Anna: And a quieter change.

Ben: The steel mills needed workers, and Luxembourg's own population was too small to supply them. Starting in the nineteen-sixties, thousands of people arrived from Portugal, escaping poverty and a dictatorship at home. They worked in construction, in the mills, in hotels and restaurants. They became the largest foreign community in the country, and today people of Portuguese origin make up about one in seven residents.

Anna: So Luxembourg was multicultural long before that was fashionable.

Ben: And then there was a financial first. In 1963, an Italian motorway company raised money through a new kind of bond, sold outside its home country and denominated in a foreign currency. It was the first so-called Eurobond, and it was listed on the Luxembourg stock exchange.

Anna: Why Luxembourg?

Ben: Simple. It was friendly, flexible, discreet, and lightly regulated. Over the next decade, banks from Germany, the United States and elsewhere arrived to set up shop. It was the seed of a new economy.

Anna: And someone saw where it was going.

Ben: Pierre Werner, prime minister and finance minister for much of the sixties and seventies. In 1970, European leaders asked a committee chaired by Werner to design a path toward a single European currency. The Werner Report mapped out a step-by-step plan for monetary union by 1980. It was shelved after the monetary chaos of the early seventies and the oil crisis, but its ideas came back twenty years later. The euro, launched in 1999, owes something to the plan sketched by a Luxembourg politician in 1970.

Anna: Then the oil crisis hit.

Ben: The crisis of 1973 and the global steel crisis that followed hit Luxembourg hard. Steel demand collapsed and the southern mills were in danger. In 1977, rather than let mass unemployment tear society apart, the government brought unions and employers together in a standing body called the Tripartite. They negotiated early retirements, retraining and state support, and avoided the bitter strikes and layoffs that wrecked other industrial regions. This approach of consensus became known as the Luxembourg model.

Anna: Now a lighter note.

Ben: In these same years, Luxembourg quietly became a pop music superpower. Radio Luxembourg, broadcasting in English by night on medium wave, was one of the few ways British teenagers could hear the latest records. Many remember listening under the blankets with a transistor radio.

Anna: And Eurovision.

Ben: Luxembourg won five times: in 1961, 1965, 1972, 1973, and 1983. One winner was the French singer France Gall, who won in 1965 with a song written by Serge Gainsbourg. A country of a few hundred thousand people won more often than many far larger ones, helped by its commercial radio and its talent for hiring great singers from abroad.

Anna: So by 1980...

Ben: The Luxembourg that had been invaded and half-destroyed was a comfortable, cosmopolitan little state with a seat at Europe's table. But the steel that had made it rich was in decline.

Anna: And the answer to what would replace it...

Ben: Was money.

## Chapter 8: From Steel to Finance, and Luxembourg Today, 1980 to 2026

Anna: In the early nineteen-eighties, the steel industry that had built modern Luxembourg was shrinking.

Ben: Blast furnaces closed and thousands of jobs vanished. In many European industrial regions, that kind of decline led to decades of misery. Luxembourg took a different path, because a new industry was already growing in its shadow: banking and finance.

Anna: The seeds went back to the first Eurobond.

Ben: Over the following decades the Grand Duchy built itself into a major international financial center. Banks from around the world set up offices, attracted by its stability, its multilingual workforce, its flexible laws, and its famously strong banking secrecy. In 1988, Luxembourg was the first country to write a 1985 European directive on cross-border investment funds into national law, which gave it a head start in that business.

Anna: And today?

Ben: It is the largest center for investment funds in Europe, second in the world only to the United States, and by common estimates the financial sector accounts for roughly a quarter of the whole economy.

Anna: What happened to the steel company?

Ben: In 2002, ARBED merged with Spanish and French rivals to form Arcelor, which in 2006 was taken over by the steel group of the Indian-born businessman Lakshmi Mittal. The result was ArcelorMittal, one of the largest steelmakers in the world, still headquartered in Luxembourg City.

Anna: And the old steel country found new uses for its industrial heritage.

Ben: At Belval, in the far south near Esch, one of the biggest steelworks was shut down. Its enormous blast furnaces weren't demolished. They were preserved as monuments, and around them rose a whole new district, with offices, a concert hall called the Rockhal, and the campus of the University of Luxembourg, which was founded only in 2003. Where workers once tapped molten iron, students now study for exams.

Anna: And Europe.

Ben: In June 1985, on a passenger boat anchored on the Moselle near the Luxembourg village of Schengen, five European countries signed an agreement to gradually abolish border checks among them. Schengen is a tiny wine-growing village where the borders of Luxembourg, France and Germany meet. The Schengen Area, which now lets millions of people cross borders without passport control, was born on that boat.

Anna: Then the euro.

Ben: In the early nineties, as leaders negotiated the Maastricht Treaty, which set the path to a single currency, Luxembourg's finance minister was a young politician named Jean-Claude Juncker. A crafty, informal negotiator, he helped broker the compromises that made the deal possible. He became prime minister in 1995 and held the job for eighteen years, longer than any other head of government in the European Union at the time. He later chaired the eurozone finance ministers through the debt crisis, and in 2014 he was chosen as President of the European Commission.

Anna: He wasn't the first Luxembourger in that job.

Ben: Gaston Thorn led the Commission from 1981 to 1985, and Jacques Santer from 1995 to 1999. Three presidents from one of the smallest countries in the union is an extraordinary record. It reflects what Luxembourg has always been good at: making deals among bigger partners, and making itself indispensable.

Anna: What about the language?

Ben: In 1984, Luxembourgish, the Moselle Franconian dialect spoken at home for centuries, was formally recognized as the national language. French and German remained the languages of law and administration. Children grow up with three languages in school, and many speak English, Portuguese or Italian at home as well. In few countries is multilingualism such a normal part of daily life.

Anna: And the country grew rich and crowded.

Ben: Luxembourg has about two thirds of a million residents, and nearly half hold a foreign passport. On top of that, around two hundred thousand people cross the border every day from France, Belgium and Germany to work. Per head, it has one of the highest standards of living in the world, but also some of the highest housing costs in Europe, and legendary traffic jams on the roads into the capital. In 2020, the government made all public transport free for everyone, the first country in the world to do so.

Anna: And it looked to the sky.

Ben: A Luxembourg-based company, SES, launched the Astra satellites in the nineteen-eighties, one of Europe's great satellite television success stories. In 2017, the government passed a law on space resources, and in 2018 it created its own space agency, hoping to become a hub for the new space economy.

Anna: Not everything has been smooth.

Ben: In 2008, Grand Duke Henri, who had come to the throne in 2000 when his father Jean abdicated, announced that his conscience would not allow him to sign a bill legalizing euthanasia. The government responded by changing the constitution, so that the Grand Duke simply promulgates laws rather than approving them. A quiet change that further reduced the monarch's role.

Anna: And LuxLeaks.

Ben: In 2014, a leak of confidential documents revealed that Luxembourg had signed tax deals with hundreds of multinational companies, allowing them to pay very low taxes. The outcry across Europe was fierce, and many officials elsewhere said the Grand Duchy had been undermining its neighbors. The government defended its legal framework but came under heavy pressure, and in 2015 it joined an international system for the automatic exchange of bank account information, effectively ending the era of strict banking secrecy for foreign account holders. The debate over what is fair in international taxation continues.

Anna: And the politics?

Ben: The Christian Social party dominated for most of the twentieth century. From 2013 to 2023, a three-party coalition of liberals, Greens and social democrats governed under Prime Minister Xavier Bettel. In the election of October 2023, the Greens lost heavily and the coalition lost its majority. A new government took office in November 2023, led by Luc Frieden of the Christian Social party, in coalition with Bettel's Democratic Party. Frieden is prime minister, and Bettel is deputy prime minister and foreign minister.

Anna: And one more big moment, the most recent chapter of the oldest story.

Ben: On the twenty-third of June, 2025, Luxembourg's National Day, Grand Duke Henri announced that he would step down after twenty-five years on the throne. On the third of October, 2025, in a ceremony at the Grand Ducal Palace, he formally abdicated in favor of his eldest son, Guillaume.

Anna: Who was forty-three.

Ben: He swore an oath to the constitution before parliament, and greeted crowds from a balcony of the palace with his Belgian-born wife, Stéphanie, and their two young sons beside him. He is the seventh grand duke of the modern line that began with Adolphe in 1890. Voluntary abdication has become something of a tradition in this family, since Henri himself took the throne when his father stepped aside in 2000.

Anna: So let's step back and look at the whole journey.

Ben: It began in nine sixty-three with a count and a rock. It passed through a golden age of emperors who sat on the thrones of Prague, Budapest and Rome. It became a fortress that foreign armies fought over for centuries, passed from Burgundy to Spain to France to Austria and back to France again.

Anna: Then it was created as a grand duchy in 1815.

Ben: Carved in half in 1839, saved from a war in 1867, occupied by Germany twice, and each time it recovered. It turned from farms into steel, from steel into finance, and from a nation guarding itself into one of the founding pillars of a united Europe.

Anna: And through all of it, one thing stayed consistent.

Ben: The people of this small land, who have spoken three languages and lived under a dozen flags, never stopped insisting on being themselves. The line from that old song, written in the eighteen-fifties, still works as a national motto: Mir wëlle bleiwe wat mir sinn.

Anna: We want to remain what we are.

Ben: That is the story of Luxembourg.

Anna: Thank you for listening, and safe travels.
