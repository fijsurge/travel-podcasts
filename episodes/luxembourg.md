## Welcome

Welcome. Over the next hour or so, we are going to tell the story of one of the strangest and most successful countries in Europe: the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg.

It is small. You could drive across it in about an hour. Its population is smaller than that of many a single city. And yet it has been fought over by emperors and kings for more than a thousand years. It has been a fortress, a province, a battlefield, a steel empire, a banking center, and one of the founding members of the European Union. It is the last grand duchy in the world.

How did a tiny territory on the edge of the great powers end up with all of that history? The answer is a story of rock, iron, borders, and stubbornness. It is a story of a people who, in the words of their own national motto, want to remain what they are.

We will move in order, from the Stone Age to the present day. Settle in, and let's begin with a swap of land beside a river.

## Chapter 1: Before Luxembourg

Let's begin with a puzzle: when was Luxembourg born? Some people say 1815, when the Grand Duchy was created at the Congress of Vienna. Others say 1839, when its modern borders were drawn in London. Others point to 1867, when the great powers guaranteed its independence. But the story we are telling really starts in the year 963, with an exchange of land beside a river. And before that exchange, there are thousands of years we should not skip.

Our first character has no name. Archaeologists call him Loschbour man, after the rock shelter near the village of Heffingen where his skeleton was found in 1935. He lived about eight thousand years ago, when this land was covered in dense forest and people survived by hunting, fishing and gathering. When scientists sequenced his DNA, decades after his discovery, the result made headlines. It suggested that this early European had dark skin and blue eyes, a combination that overturned a lot of assumptions about what the first Europeans looked like.

After Loschbour man came farmers, who arrived from the southeast and cleared fields along the river valleys. Then came the age of metal, first bronze and later iron. By the last centuries before Christ, the land of Luxembourg belonged to a Celtic people called the Treveri.

The Treveri left a remarkable monument: a fortified hilltop town called the Titelberg, in the far southwest of today's country, close to the French border. It sat on a high plateau, with ramparts around it, and it was a center for metalworking and trade. Imagine a town of craftsmen and warriors, with a view across the countryside, and one of the richest cavalry traditions in Gaul.

Then Rome arrived. The first written reference to this territory comes from Julius Caesar himself, in his account of the Gallic Wars. Around the middle of the first century before Christ, Caesar fought the Treveri, and after years of rebellion and repression, the region was swallowed into the Roman Empire.

The Romans founded a city on the Moselle river, just across what is now the German border: Augusta Treverorum, today's Trier. Within a few generations it became one of the great cities of the western empire. At one point it was the home of emperors, and its Roman gates and baths still stand. The land that would become Luxembourg was Trier's backyard. It was farmland and forest, crossed by Roman roads and dotted with country estates.

One of those roads ran from the city of Reims, in what is now France, to Trier. Along it, near today's village of Dalheim, grew a small Roman town with a theater. At Echternach and at Diekirch, wealthy landowners built grand villas. At Diekirch, a Roman mosaic from one of those houses survives, laid out in colored stone. Life was settled, Latin-speaking and, for a few centuries, secure.

Then the empire cracked. In the third and fourth centuries, raiders pushed across the Rhine, and Roman power along the frontier began to wear away. By the end of the fifth century, the region had passed to the Franks, a Germanic people whose king, Clovis, was building a new kingdom out of the ruins of Roman Gaul. Luxembourg's land became part of the Frankish heartland, in the eastern half that was later called Austrasia.

Christianity came with the Franks, and with a handful of remarkable missionaries. The most important for our story is a monk named Willibrord, born in Northumbria, in England, and trained in Ireland. He crossed the sea to preach to the pagan Frisians, and in the year 698 he founded a monastery at Echternach, on land given to him by a noblewoman from the Trier area. Willibrord died in 739 and was buried there.

Echternach became one of the great intellectual centers of medieval Europe. Its monks copied and illuminated manuscripts so beautiful that they are treasured today in museums in Germany. And the town still marks its founder's memory with a custom that has lasted for centuries. Every year, on the Tuesday after Pentecost, thousands of people dance through the streets of Echternach in a procession, hopping in time to a simple polka-like tune, in honor of Saint Willibrord. UNESCO now recognizes it as part of humanity's living cultural heritage.

Now we move to Charlemagne. In the late 700s and early 800s, the Frankish kings became emperors, and the Echternach region sat comfortably inside their realm. But when Charlemagne's empire was split among his grandsons by the Treaty of Verdun in 843, the land that would become Luxembourg fell into the middle kingdom, named after the grandson Lothar. That middle kingdom did not last. Over the following decades, it broke up, and the region we are talking about became a borderland, a long-contested strip between the western Frankish kingdom, which would become France, and the eastern Frankish kingdom, which would become Germany.

Then, in the year 962, in Rome, a German king named Otto was crowned emperor. His realm would later be known as the Holy Roman Empire. It was a loose, shifting collection of kingdoms, duchies, counties and church lands that would last for more than eight hundred years. And just one year later, a count named Siegfried made a small land deal that no one at the time could have thought important.

Siegfried belonged to a noble family from the Moselle and Ardennes region, and according to tradition he had Carolingian blood. What he wanted was a stronghold. On the edge of lands he controlled, there was a rocky promontory rising above the river Alzette, with the ruins of an old fortification. That fortification belonged to the abbey of Saint Maximin in Trier. Siegfried offered other lands in exchange, and the monks agreed.

The charter that records the swap, dated 963, gives the name of the place: Lucilinburhuc. It means roughly little castle. In time, the name would shorten and soften into Luxembourg.

The rock itself would later be known as the Bock. Siegfried built a real fortress on it, and from there his family would spread out across the surrounding land. The place was so well chosen that, as we will see, armies would fight over it for the next nine hundred years.

A legend adds a touch of magic. It says that Siegfried married a mermaid named Melusina, who agreed to live with him on the condition that he never look at her on a certain day. He broke the promise, and she vanished beneath the waters of the Alzette, leaving him alone. Locals say she can still be seen once every seven years, spinning at the bottom of the river.

But the real story is better than the legend. Because from this small rocky outcrop, Siegfried's descendants would rise to the heights of European power. They would sit on the throne of Bohemia and wear the imperial crown. In the next chapter, we meet the House of Luxembourg.

## Chapter 2: The House of Luxembourg, 963 to 1443

For its first two centuries, Siegfried's county was a modest affair. It was a rocky fortress, a handful of villages, and a dynasty that married well and waited. The counts of Luxembourg slowly gathered land, and in 1136 a stroke of inheritance gave the county to Henry the Blind, count of Namur, who joined the two territories together.

His daughter became one of the most remarkable rulers in the story. Her name was Ermesinde. She inherited the county in 1196 and ruled for more than fifty years, through two marriages and a great deal of politics. Ermesinde is remembered for granting charters of freedom to the towns of her county. In 1244, she gave one to the city of Luxembourg, and it freed its townspeople from many of the old obligations of serfdom and let them organize themselves as a community, with a council of their own. In a Europe where most people lived under their lord's thumb, that was a bold step. To this day, Luxembourg treats Ermesinde as one of its founding figures.

Now we reach the part of the story where this small county suddenly leaps onto the continental stage.

In 1288, the count of Luxembourg was killed at the Battle of Worringen, in a war over the succession to the neighboring duchy of Limburg. His heir was a boy of about thirteen, also named Henry. We know him as Henry the Seventh. As a young man, he was widely admired. He was said to be chivalrous, pious, and quietly ambitious. And in 1308, after the German king was murdered, the electors of the empire looked around for someone who was capable but not too powerful. They chose the count of Luxembourg.

That is an astonishing moment. A lord from a small county on the western edge of the empire suddenly found himself king of the Germans. And Henry seized the chance. He arranged for his teenage son John to marry Elisabeth, the heiress of the kingdom of Bohemia, which is roughly today's Czech Republic. John became King of Bohemia in 1310. In one stroke, the House of Luxembourg had acquired one of the richest crowns in central Europe.

Henry himself marched over the Alps into Italy, where he was crowned emperor in Rome in 1312. He dreamed of restoring imperial glory. The poet Dante, who lived through those years and pinned his hopes on Henry, wrote that a seat was waiting for him in Paradise. But Henry died of a fever in 1313, near the city of Siena, and the dream died with him.

His son John had a very different life. Known to history as John the Blind, he was one of the great adventurers of the Middle Ages. He was born in Luxembourg and raised partly at the French court. As King of Bohemia, he was almost never home. He fought in Italy, in Poland, and in Lithuania, alongside the crusading Teutonic Knights. He loved tournaments, and he loved France. His daughter Bonne married the man who would become King John the Second of France, and she became the grandmother of a future French king.

By around 1340, John had lost his sight completely. It did not slow him down. In 1340 he founded a fair in Luxembourg City that still exists today, the Schueberfouer. It takes place every year in late August and early September, with rides, food stalls, and traditional parades, and it is the biggest funfair in the country.

Then came the war that would define his end. In 1346, England and France were fighting what we now call the Hundred Years' War, and John, loyal to the French, rode to the battlefield of Crécy. Blind, he asked his knights to lead him into the fight. According to the old account, they tied their horses together by the reins, so that he would not be separated, and charged straight into the English archers. By the next morning, John and his companions were found dead on the field.

Tradition says that the English prince, the one we call the Black Prince, who was just sixteen, took the three ostrich feathers from John's helmet as a trophy, and made them his own emblem. Historians argue about whether that is really true. But in Luxembourg, John is remembered as the great national hero. His body now rests in the crypt of Notre-Dame cathedral in the capital, and to Luxembourgers he is a symbol of bravery, loyalty, and a certain doomed gallantry.

We come now to the greatest of the Luxembourgers. John's eldest son was born in Prague and baptized with the name Wenceslas, but he took the name Charles at the French court, in honor of his godfather and uncle. As Charles the Fourth, he became King of Bohemia after his father's death, and in 1355 he was crowned Holy Roman Emperor in Rome.

Charles was a very different kind of emperor from his father and grandfather. He was a scholar, a builder, and a patient politician. In 1356 he issued a famous decree called the Golden Bull, which fixed how emperors would be elected by seven electors, a system that more or less lasted until the empire itself ended. He founded Charles University in Prague in 1348, the first university in central Europe. He started building the stone bridge across the Vltava that still bears his name. He turned Prague into one of the great cities of Europe.

And he did not forget the little county where his family began. In 1354, Charles raised Luxembourg to the rank of duchy, and installed his half-brother Wenceslas as its first duke.

So by the late fourteenth century, the House of Luxembourg was one of the great dynasties of Europe. Its sons ruled Bohemia, and for a time Hungary, and sat on the imperial throne. In all, four Luxembourgers held the title of ruler of the Holy Roman Empire: Henry the Seventh, Charles the Fourth, Charles's son Wenceslas the Fourth, and Charles's other son Sigismund. Only three of them, Henry, Charles and Sigismund, were actually crowned emperor in Rome.

Sigismund was the last. A shrewd and restless man, he was King of Hungary from 1387, and he became King of the Romans in 1411. He called the great Council of Constance, which met from 1414 to 1418 and ended a split in the Catholic Church, and which also burned the Czech reformer Jan Hus, an act that triggered the Hussite wars in Bohemia. Sigismund was crowned emperor in 1433, and he died in 1437. He had no surviving son. With his death, the male line of the House of Luxembourg came to an end.

Notice what happened to the little duchy of Luxembourg through all this. Its glamorous rulers were scattered across central Europe. They spent most of their time in Prague, Buda or Rome, and they rarely visited the place that had made them. To them, Luxembourg was a source of money, and they treated it that way. They mortgaged it. They pledged it as security for loans, and passed it around among cousins and creditors.

One of the people who ended up holding it was a woman named Elisabeth of Görlitz, a niece of Sigismund. She was widowed, remarried, and constantly short of money. In the end, she sold her rights to the most powerful figure in the region: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy. In 1443, his forces took possession of the city, reportedly by a surprise assault with the help of local nobles who were fed up with the unstable rule of the pledge-holders.

And just like that, the old era came to an end. The county that had given Europe emperors and kings was now just one more piece in the growing Burgundian collection of lands.

Before we move on, one more thread worth remembering. In the countryside of Luxembourg, medieval lords built castles that still stand. The most spectacular is Vianden, on a hill above a river in the north, one of the largest feudal residences in this part of Europe. In the early fifteenth century, Vianden passed by inheritance to the family of Nassau, which would later become the Dutch royal house. Keep that name in mind. Four hundred years later, it will be central to our story.

Next, we enter the age of the fortress.

## Chapter 3: The Gibraltar of the North, 1443 to 1815

If the first part of our story was about a dynasty, this part is about a rock. For the next three and a half centuries, Luxembourg's main job in European politics was to be a fortress. And it was a good one. A great deal of the time, the Luxembourgers themselves were not the ones deciding who owned it.

Philip the Good of Burgundy took the duchy in 1443, and for a while Luxembourg was one piece in a glittering Burgundian empire that included Flanders, Brabant and Holland. Burgundy was the richest, most fashionable court in Europe. But it did not last. In 1477, Philip's grandson Charles the Bold was killed in battle outside the city of Nancy, leaving a single daughter, Mary. She married Maximilian of Habsburg, and through that marriage the Burgundian lands, Luxembourg included, passed to the Habsburg family.

This was the beginning of an era in which Luxembourg belonged to the most powerful dynasty in the world. Maximilian and Mary's grandson was born in Ghent in the year 1500 and grew up to be Charles the Fifth, who ruled Spain, much of Italy, the Netherlands, Germany, and the new Spanish lands in the Americas. Luxembourg was a tiny corner of his empire. But it sat right on the road between his Netherlands and his Italian and Spanish possessions, so it mattered.

When Charles retired in the middle of the sixteenth century, his empire was divided. His son Philip the Second took Spain and the Netherlands, and Luxembourg went with them. That put Luxembourg in the front row for one of the great conflicts of the age. The northern provinces of the Netherlands rose in revolt, turned Protestant, and eventually broke away to become the Dutch Republic. But the southern provinces, including Luxembourg, stayed under Spain and stayed Catholic. Luxembourg's people had barely been touched by the Reformation, and they remained loyal.

The seventeenth century was brutal. During the Thirty Years' War, armies marched back and forth across the Luxembourg countryside, looting and burning. Famine and plague followed the soldiers. In some parts of the country, the population fell by something like half. Meanwhile Spain was declining, and France under the Sun King, Louis the Fourteenth, was rising.

France began nibbling. In 1659, the peace treaty between Spain and France handed over a belt of towns along Luxembourg's southern border, including Thionville. This was the first of several times that Luxembourg would shrink.

Then, in 1684, Louis the Fourteenth came for the fortress itself. French forces besieged the city for about a month and took it. And here the history of the place changes. Louis brought the great military engineer Marquis de Vauban, the greatest builder of fortresses of his age, to rebuild the defenses. Vauban dug ditches, raised massive walls, and tunneled deep into the rock. Within a few years, the capital of Luxembourg was one of the most formidable fortresses in Europe, with a nickname that would stick: the Gibraltar of the North.

The French did not keep it. In 1697, after a long war, Louis gave Luxembourg back to Spain in the Treaty of Ryswick. But France held it again briefly in the early eighteenth century, and when the great war over the Spanish throne was settled in 1713 and 1714, Luxembourg was handed to the Austrian branch of the Habsburgs, together with what is now Belgium. Those lands became the Austrian Netherlands, and Luxembourg was a part of them.

The Austrian period, which ran for about eight decades, is sometimes remembered as a relatively calm and prosperous time. Historians still debate whether it was really a golden age, but the evidence of peace is clear enough. The population recovered. Ironworks along the rivers produced cast iron, and farming improved. The Austrians kept strengthening the fortress, and in the 1730s they built a new fort on the plateau across the valley, called Fort Thüngen. Its restored towers, nicknamed the Three Acorns, still stand today, and now they house a museum.

The real wonder of the fortress, however, was hidden. Over the generations, engineers dug a vast network of tunnels, called casemates, into the sandstone cliff of the Bock. They provided shelter for soldiers, space for cannon, and storage for supplies. At the height of the fortress, there were many miles of tunnels. Today, roughly seventeen kilometers of them survive, about ten and a half miles, and visitors can still walk through them and look out over the valley through the gun slits.

The fortress changed hands so often in this period that its history reads like a revolving door. If you were an ambitious king, Luxembourg was a prize. If you lived in Luxembourg, it was a burden. The city was stuffed with soldiers, the townspeople paid for the upkeep of the walls, and every time a war broke out between France and its neighbors, armies tramped across your fields.

The quiet eventually ended in the 1780s. Emperor Joseph the Second, who ruled from Vienna, was a reformer of the enlightened kind. He tried to modernize his lands all at once, closing monasteries, limiting the church's power, and reorganizing the courts. Many of his subjects in the Austrian Netherlands, including Luxembourg, were conservative and devout, and they were furious. In 1789, the same year the French Revolution began, a revolt broke out in the Netherlands. It was suppressed within a couple of years.

But the French Revolution was not so easily suppressed. In 1794, French revolutionary armies swept through the Austrian Netherlands. The Luxembourg fortress held out. For about seven months, from the autumn of 1794, French troops surrounded it while Austrian defenders waited for relief that never came. Finally, in June 1795, starving and cut off, the garrison surrendered. Luxembourg, the Gibraltar of the North, had fallen, and not to a great assault but to hunger.

In the autumn of 1795, France formally annexed the territory. The old duchy of Luxembourg ceased to exist, and in its place the French created a new administrative unit called the Department of the Forests, named for the heavily wooded Ardennes hills. It was ruled from the city of Luxembourg.

The French years brought profound change. The old feudal rights were abolished. The church lost much of its land. A new system of laws, the Napoleonic Code, was introduced, and traces of it survive in Luxembourg's legal system to this day. But there was also a heavy price. The French demanded soldiers. Young men from Luxembourg were drafted into Napoleon's armies, and many never came home.

In 1798, the conscription law sparked a revolt in the countryside of the north. Peasants armed with clubs, farm tools, and old hunting guns rose up against the French. The revolt is still remembered in Luxembourg by a name that means the Peasants' War, or more literally, the War of the Clubs. It was crushed within weeks, and the leaders were punished harshly. But the memory of it has never faded. For generations, it was a powerful reminder of how deeply the ordinary people of Luxembourg resented foreign rule.

Then, in 1814, after Napoleon's disastrous retreat from Russia and his defeat at Leipzig, the French withdrew. For the first time in centuries, the future of Luxembourg was up for negotiation among the great powers of Europe. In the next chapter, they make a decision that creates something no one had ever seen before: a grand duchy.

## Chapter 4: A Grand Duchy Is Born, 1815 to 1867

Picture the scene in Vienna in the winter of 1814 and 1815. Napoleon had been defeated and exiled. The diplomats of Europe, kings, princes, and ministers in glittering uniforms, gathered for the Congress of Vienna to redraw the map. Their main goal was simple: make sure France could never again conquer its neighbors. And one of the ways to do that was to build a strong state on France's northern border.

So they combined what is now the Netherlands and what is now Belgium into a single United Kingdom of the Netherlands, ruled by William of Orange-Nassau, who became King William the First. William's family had lost its ancestral German lands, so the great powers looked for ways to compensate him. One of them was Luxembourg.

The diplomats made it a state in its own right. They raised it to the rank of grand duchy, which was a way of saying it was more than an ordinary duchy and a proper sovereign state with a seat in the new German Confederation. William would be both King of the Netherlands and Grand Duke of Luxembourg, a so-called personal union, in which the same man wore two crowns over two countries that were otherwise separate. And so, in 1815, the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg came into being. It remains the only grand duchy in the world.

But the new state was smaller than the old duchy. Prussia, the rising power on the Rhine, demanded land, and Luxembourg lost its entire eastern border region, the territory east of the rivers Moselle, Sûre and Our. This was about a quarter of its area, and it was the second time in the modern era that the country had been carved up.

As part of the deal, the fortress of Luxembourg became a fortress of the German Confederation, and a Prussian garrison moved in. The Grand Duke ruled the country. The Prussians held the rock.

Then came 1830, and the great shock. In Brussels, riots broke out after an opera performance, and within weeks the southern provinces of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands had risen up in a revolution. They were Catholic and mostly French-speaking, and they resented rule by a Protestant, Dutch-speaking king. They declared independence as the new Kingdom of Belgium.

Most of Luxembourg joined them. Luxembourgers took part in the uprising, sent representatives to the Belgian national congress, and for several years almost the whole country was governed from Brussels. The only exception was the capital, where the Prussian garrison held the fortress firmly in the name of the German Confederation.

For nearly a decade, the fate of Luxembourg was unresolved. Belgium claimed all of it. The Dutch king claimed all of it. The great powers met in London and eventually proposed a compromise. In 1839, after King William finally accepted defeat, a treaty was signed in London that carved the country in two.

The western part of Luxembourg, the French-speaking part, went to Belgium. It included the towns of Arlon and Bastogne, a name we will meet again in the Second World War. This was roughly sixty-five percent of the country's territory, and about half its population, something like one hundred and seventy-five thousand people. What remained was the eastern, German-speaking part, essentially the Luxembourg of today.

Historians still shake their heads at how it was done. The line was drawn in London, using maps that were out of date and inaccurate, by diplomats who had never visited the places they were dividing. To compensate the German Confederation for the loss, part of the Dutch province of Limburg was attached to it. And the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg, which had been a middling European territory, became a small, landlocked, mostly poor country with about one hundred and seventy thousand inhabitants.

But here is the twist. The treaty of 1839 is often described as the moment Luxembourg's independence began. Because it fixed the borders. For the first time, there was a clear, internationally recognized line around a state called Luxembourg, which is the same line you can cross on a bridge or a country road today. It was a tragic loss, but it was also a beginning.

The decades that followed were hard. Luxembourg was a farming country with little industry, and in the 1840s a potato blight and poor harvests pushed many families into hunger. In 1842, the Grand Duchy joined the German customs union, the Zollverein, which tied its economy to the German states and opened up markets for its goods. But it also meant that Luxembourg's economic fate was linked to its neighbor across the Moselle.

Many Luxembourgers simply left. Between about 1840 and 1890, tens of thousands of them emigrated, many to the United States, where they settled in the Midwest, especially in Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, and the area around Chicago. For a country of fewer than two hundred thousand people, this was an enormous drain.

Meanwhile, the Grand Duchy was slowly learning to govern itself. In 1841, William the Second, the king who followed William the First, granted Luxembourg its first constitution, creating an assembly of the estates. In 1848, the year revolutions shook Europe, a more liberal constitution followed. The Dutch kings were not very interested in their grand duchy. William the Third, who ruled from 1849, rarely visited, and day to day the country was run by his brother Prince Henry as his representative.

And a national consciousness was waking up. Writers and poets began composing in Luxembourgish, the Moselle Franconian dialect that ordinary people spoke at home and that had no official status. In 1859, the poet Michel Lentz wrote a song that Luxembourgers still sing, called the Feierwon, or the Fire Wagon, about the arrival of the railway. Its refrain contains a line that became a kind of national motto. It says, in Luxembourgish, Mir wëlle bleiwe wat mir sinn, which means, We want to remain what we are. In the same decade, Lentz wrote the words to the song that would become the national anthem, Ons Heemecht, or Our Homeland, officially adopted in 1895. And in 1872, Michel Rodange published Renert, a long satirical poem in Luxembourgish about a crafty fox, which many people consider the country's national epic.

Now we come to the crisis that nearly started a war.

In 1866, Prussia defeated Austria in a short, decisive war, and the German Confederation, which had existed since 1815, was dissolved. Suddenly, no one was sure what Luxembourg's status was. Was it part of Germany? Was it independent? Who owned the fortress, with its Prussian garrison?

Napoleon the Third of France saw an opportunity. Prussia had just become dramatically more powerful, and he wanted something in return to keep the balance, and to restore French prestige. He offered to buy Luxembourg from King William the Third of the Netherlands, who was short of money and had little emotional attachment to the place. William agreed in principle.

But Otto von Bismarck, the Prussian chancellor, was not going to let France take a strategic fortress on the German frontier. Bismarck made sure that news of the sale leaked, and German public opinion exploded. Luxembourg is German soil, politicians and newspapers cried. For a few weeks in the spring of 1867, Europe stood at the edge of war.

In the end, diplomacy won out. The great powers met in London in May 1867, and they agreed on a settlement that satisfied everyone and no one. The second Treaty of London, signed on the eleventh of May, declared that Luxembourg was an independent state, under the sovereignty of the House of Orange-Nassau, and that it would be perpetually neutral, under the collective guarantee of the great powers. The Prussian garrison would leave. And the fortress, the Gibraltar of the North, would be dismantled, so that no one could use it as a weapon against their neighbors.

After nine hundred years of being a prize, Luxembourg was to be a country at peace. The question was whether its great neighbors would respect it. We will find out in the chapters ahead.

## Chapter 5: Neutrality, Iron and a New Dynasty, 1867 to 1914

The treaty of 1867 promised that the fortress of Luxembourg would be taken apart, and it kept its word. Over the next sixteen years, work crews tore down the walls, filled in ditches, and blew up forts and bastions. It was one of the biggest demolition projects in Europe, and when it was done, the city that had been a cramped military stronghold for centuries was suddenly free to grow.

The city took the opportunity. The land where the defenses had stood became parks, boulevards, and building plots. New districts spread across the plateau. The deep valleys that had been military obstacles became green spaces. And in 1903, the city opened a magnificent stone bridge, the Adolphe Bridge, which sweeps across the Pétrusse valley in two graceful arches. At the time, it was one of the largest stone arch bridges in the world.

Not everything was destroyed. Parts of the casemates survived, and the old town on its cliffs remained. In 1994, UNESCO recognized the old quarters and the surviving fortifications of Luxembourg City as a World Heritage site. The Gibraltar of the North had become, in the end, a beautiful ruin.

With the fortress gone, Luxembourg was now a neutral country. That sounds peaceful, but it was also a gamble. The great powers had guaranteed its neutrality together, which in practice meant that if anyone broke it, there was no single power obliged to punish them. And the country had almost no army to defend itself, only a small corps of volunteers and gendarmes. Luxembourg's safety depended entirely on the goodwill of its neighbors.

But while the diplomats worried, something else was happening underground, something that would change the country more than any treaty.

In the south of Luxembourg, running down into the neighboring French region of Lorraine, lies a belt of iron ore. It is called minette, a reddish-brown stone. For centuries, local blacksmiths had known it was there, but it was almost useless, because it contained a lot of phosphorus, which made the iron brittle and impossible to turn into good steel. Then, in 1878, two Englishmen, Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist, developed a method for removing phosphorus from iron. Suddenly, the poor ore of Luxembourg and Lorraine could be made into excellent steel.

What followed was an industrial revolution in miniature. Blast furnaces went up along the Alzette and in the southwest corner of the country. Villages like Esch, Differdange, Dudelange and Rumelange swelled into industrial towns. Mines opened up in the red hills. The sky above the south turned orange at night and the dust settled red on everything. Within a generation, the country that had been exporting its farmers to America was importing workers.

They came from Germany, and then from Italy. Italian laborers began arriving in large numbers in the 1890s, and they settled in the steel towns, and they would be the first of many waves of immigrants who would reshape the country. Luxembourg had a tradition of sending its sons and daughters abroad. Now, for the first time, it began to receive people from elsewhere.

In 1911, the main steel producers merged into a single giant company called ARBED, a name made from the French words for the united steelworks of Burbach, Eich and Dudelange. For most of the twentieth century, ARBED was practically a state within the state. By the eve of the First World War, steel was the backbone of the economy, and per head of population, few places in the world made more of it. The shy farming country had become an industrial power.

The politics of these years were stable, even dull, and the person who embodied that stability was a man named Paul Eyschen, who was head of the government from 1888 until 1915, an extraordinary twenty-seven years. He was a careful, pragmatic lawyer who guided the country through industrialization and the swirling pressures of its neighbors.

Now we reach one of the most unusual episodes in the whole story: the change of dynasty.

In 1890, King William the Third of the Netherlands died. He had been both King of the Netherlands and Grand Duke of Luxembourg for forty-one years. But he had no surviving sons, only a ten-year-old daughter, Wilhelmina. She became Queen of the Netherlands. But in Luxembourg, an old family law, the Nassau family pact, said that only men could inherit. So the Grand Duchy and the Netherlands, united for seventy-five years, separated.

The crown of Luxembourg passed to a distant cousin, a man named Adolphe, the former Duke of Nassau. His own duchy in Germany had been swallowed up by Prussia in 1866, and he had been living in exile. Now, in 1890, at the age of seventy-three, he found himself the Grand Duke of Luxembourg. For the first time, the country had a ruler who lived there, whose family belonged to it, and who was not the king of someone else. It was the beginning of the modern Luxembourg monarchy.

Adolphe reigned until 1905. His son William the Fourth followed, but he too had a problem: he had no sons, only daughters. So parliament changed the succession rules in 1907 to allow women to inherit the throne. When William died in 1912, his eldest daughter, Marie-Adélaïde, became Grand Duchess at the age of eighteen. The first woman to rule Luxembourg in her own right was a teenager.

In the same years, the little country was producing a few remarkable people who made their mark abroad. In 1879, a boy named Edward Steichen was born in the village of Bivange. His family emigrated to the United States when he was a toddler, and he grew up to become one of the greatest photographers of the twentieth century. In 1955, he organized a legendary exhibition called The Family of Man, which toured the world, and which is permanently displayed today in a castle in the northern Luxembourg town of Clervaux.

Another Luxembourger, the physicist Gabriel Lippmann, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1908 for a method of reproducing colors in photographs. And a man named Hugo Gernsback, born in Luxembourg City in 1884, moved to the United States in 1904, where he published some of the first magazines devoted to what he called scientifiction, now known as science fiction. The Hugo Awards, one of the most famous prizes in the genre, are named after him.

All these stories, of mines and bridges, of princesses and physicists, show a small country that had figured out how to punch above its weight. By 1914, Luxembourg was prosperous, stable, proud, and increasingly sure of its own identity. It had a language that its writers loved, a dynasty that was its own, and an economy that was growing fast.

But the country still had the same geography, wedged between France, Belgium and Germany. And the guarantee of the great powers was only as strong as their promises. In the summer of 1914, a crisis in the Balkans pushed Europe toward catastrophe, and the neutrality of Luxembourg was about to be tested.

## Chapter 6: Two World Wars, 1914 to 1945

On the second of August, 1914, German soldiers crossed the border into Luxembourg. They came by road and by rail, and they took the capital's railway station and the key bridges within hours. This was a plain violation of the treaty of 1867, which guaranteed Luxembourg's perpetual neutrality. Germany said it was only protecting the railways, which were vital to its plan to invade France through Belgium. Luxembourg's government protested, but it had no army worth the name and no way to resist.

A popular story says that the young Grand Duchess Marie-Adélaïde drove to the Adolphe Bridge and tried to block it with her car. Whether that is exactly true or not, it captures the helplessness of the moment. A country that had trusted a piece of paper for fifty years was now occupied.

The occupation was strange. Germany allowed the Luxembourg government to continue operating, and promised not to harm the country. But the economy was tied into the German war machine, food grew scarce, and many Luxembourgers felt trapped. Thousands of young men slipped away to join the French army, fighting under the French flag against the occupiers of their homeland.

The Grand Duchess, who was very young, very Catholic, and very conservative, was in an impossible position. She remained in the country and kept up official contacts with the occupying authorities, including meeting with the German Crown Prince. When the war ended in November 1918, those meetings came back to haunt her. The victorious Allies, France and Belgium especially, accused her of being pro-German. Her reputation collapsed. Crowds demonstrated against her, and the French and the Americans refused to deal with her.

In January 1919, Marie-Adélaïde abdicated in favor of her younger sister, Charlotte, who was twenty-two. Marie-Adélaïde left the country, entered a convent in Italy, and died in 1924, aged only twenty-nine.

But the monarchy itself was now in question. In September 1919, Luxembourgers voted in a double referendum. The first question asked whether they wanted to keep the dynasty, and about seventy-eight percent said yes. The second question asked what kind of economic union they wanted with a larger neighbor, and about seventy-three percent chose France. But France turned the offer down, and so Luxembourg turned to Belgium. In 1921, the two countries formed an economic union, which shared a customs area and tied the Luxembourg franc to the Belgian franc. That union lasted until both countries adopted the euro.

The year 1919 also brought a political revolution. A revision of the constitution introduced universal suffrage, which for the first time included women. In that year's elections, women voted in Luxembourg for the first time, and a woman was elected to parliament.

The next two decades were a time of quiet transformation. Charlotte, a poised and popular young monarch, married Prince Felix of Bourbon-Parma in 1919. Their son Jean was born in 1921, and the family became beloved symbols of national unity. The steel industry prospered, then suffered in the Depression. And in 1929, Luxembourg passed a law that made it unusually attractive for holding companies, which was the first small step towards the country's future as a financial center.

In 1933, a powerful commercial radio station, Radio Luxembourg, began broadcasting from the country. It used a strong transmitter, and its programs were in several languages. It was a business as much as a broadcaster, selling advertising across the borders, and in time it would become one of the most famous radio stations in Europe, bringing popular music into homes across Britain and the continent.

But the shadow of Germany was growing again. And on the tenth of May, 1940, it fell.

German forces invaded Luxembourg at dawn as part of their attack on France and the Low Countries. The country was overrun in a single day. Grand Duchess Charlotte and her government had already decided to leave, and they escaped across the border into France. From there they went to Portugal, then to London. Charlotte spent much of the war in exile, in Britain and Canada, and became a voice of the country. Her radio addresses over the BBC, broadcast to Luxembourg, were heard in secret by people in the occupied country, and they gave courage to many.

What happened next was more brutal than in the First World War. Hitler's Germany did not treat Luxembourg as a neutral occupied country. It treated Luxembourg as German. The man in charge, Gustav Simon, was the Nazi regional governor of the neighboring Koblenz-Trier district, and he was given authority over Luxembourg in the summer of 1940. His goal was to make the country fully German.

The campaign of Germanization was thorough and petty at once. The French language was banned. People were told to change their names, street signs were repainted, and even French words in everyday speech were forbidden, so that a polite merci had to become danke. Political parties and trade unions were abolished. Luxembourgers were declared to be Germans of the Moselle region, and sooner or later were expected to join the German war effort.

Then, in October 1941, the Nazi authorities arranged a census. It had three questions: what is your nationality, what is your mother tongue, and what is your ethnicity? The Germans expected everyone to answer German, which would prove that Luxembourg was a German land. But resistance organizers spread the word quietly, and the answer they urged was Luxembourgish, three times. And so more than nine in ten people wrote Luxembourgish on every line. The Germans were furious, and they canceled the census.

The Jewish community of Luxembourg, a few thousand people before the war, was persecuted from the first days of the occupation. Many managed to flee, but more than a thousand were deported to ghettos and camps in the East, and only a small number survived.

The crisis came in the summer of 1942. On the thirtieth of August, Simon announced that young Luxembourgers would be drafted into the German army. Within hours, a spontaneous general strike began. It started in the town of Wiltz, in the north, and spread to the steel towns, to the mines, to schools, to government offices. Workers walked off their jobs. Trains stopped. Simon declared a state of emergency, and the Gestapo moved in. A special court sentenced about twenty strikers to death, and they were shot within days.

The strike was crushed, but it is one of the proudest moments in Luxembourg's national memory, because it was an act of mass defiance in the middle of a brutal occupation. And the draft went ahead anyway. Around twelve thousand Luxembourgers were forced to serve in the German army. About thirty-five hundred of them deserted or went into hiding, and their families were deported to labor camps in Germany and Silesia. Somewhere between twenty-five hundred and twenty-eight hundred of the conscripts were killed or went missing at the front.

And so the war divided families, and it left a scar that took generations to heal. Many Luxembourgers fought on the Allied side too. In Britain, a small Luxembourg unit served in the Allied armies. And Charlotte's son, Prince Jean, joined the British Army's Irish Guards and took part in the Allied advance after the Normandy landings.

Liberation came in September 1944. American troops entered Luxembourg City on the tenth, to scenes of wild celebration. But the country's ordeal was not over. In December, Hitler launched his last great offensive in the Ardennes forests, the Battle of the Bulge, and the fighting swept straight across northern Luxembourg. Towns like Wiltz, Clervaux and Vianden were battered, and Wiltz, which had been the cradle of the 1942 strike, was nearly destroyed, earning the title of martyr town. Meanwhile General Patton's Third Army made its headquarters in Luxembourg City, and from there launched the counterattack that helped save the surrounding region, including the besieged town of Bastogne, just across the border in Belgium.

When the battle ended in January 1945, Luxembourg was free again, but exhausted and scarred. Patton himself died in December 1945 after a car accident in Germany, and he was buried, at his own wish, among his soldiers, in the American military cemetery at Hamm, just outside the capital, where more than five thousand American servicemen rest today.

In barely a quarter of a century, Luxembourg had been invaded twice by its giant neighbor. The old strategy of neutrality, of staying quiet and hoping to be left alone, had failed twice, catastrophically. The country now had to find a new way to survive. And it did. In the next chapter, we meet the Luxembourgers who decided that the answer was not to hide from Europe, but to help build it.

## Chapter 7: Rebuilding and Founding Europe, 1945 to 1980

When the war ended, Luxembourg drew a conclusion that would shape the rest of its history: a small country cannot protect itself by standing alone. Neutrality had been tried, and it had failed twice. So the Grand Duchy did something that surprised many observers. It threw itself into international cooperation, with an enthusiasm that few larger countries could match.

The process had begun even before the war ended. In September 1944, while the exiled governments of Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg were still sitting in London, they signed an agreement to create a customs union among the three countries. The name for it was a combination of their initials: Benelux. Fourteen years later, the three countries turned it into a full economic union.

In 1945, Luxembourg became a founding member of the United Nations. In 1948, it formally rewrote its constitution to remove the clause that made it a perpetually neutral country. And in 1949, it was one of the twelve founding members of NATO, the Western military alliance. For a country with a tiny army, this was a bold declaration. Luxembourg was now firmly part of the Western camp.

But the most important step was still to come, and the key figures in it had strong Luxembourg connections.

One was a man named Robert Schuman. He was born in 1886 in Clausen, a neighborhood in Luxembourg City, to a mother from Luxembourg and a father from the French region of Lorraine. He grew up speaking German and French and Luxembourgish, studied in Germany, and eventually became a French politician. After the Second World War, he served as France's foreign minister.

On the ninth of May, 1950, Schuman made an announcement that changed the history of Europe. He proposed that France and Germany, who had fought three wars in less than a century, should place their coal and steel production under a single common authority, open to other European countries. The idea was that if the raw materials of war were jointly controlled, a new war between the two nations would become unthinkable. This is known as the Schuman Declaration, and May ninth is celebrated today as Europe Day.

The result was the European Coal and Steel Community, set up by six countries in 1951: France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. And Luxembourg was a natural participant. It was one of Europe's biggest producers of steel per head of population. Its steel company ARBED was one of the giants of the sector. And its leaders had learned that tying themselves to bigger neighbors in a set of shared institutions was the best way to stay free.

Another leading figure was Joseph Bech, Luxembourg's foreign minister for more than thirty years, from 1926 to 1958. In 1955, at a conference in the Sicilian town of Messina, Bech helped push forward a plan for a wider European common market, and that led to the Treaties of Rome in 1957, which created the European Economic Community, the direct ancestor of today's European Union. Luxembourg's government was small, but its diplomats were skilled, and they made themselves useful as mediators between France and Germany.

The country was rewarded. When the institutions of the new Europe were being placed, Luxembourg became one of their homes. The High Authority of the Coal and Steel Community was based there from 1952. So was the Court of Justice of the European Communities, which still sits in Luxembourg today as the Court of Justice of the European Union. In 1968, the European Investment Bank, the EU's lending arm, set up its headquarters there. In 1977, the European Court of Auditors followed. Parts of the European Parliament's administration are in Luxembourg too, along with Eurostat, the EU's statistics office, and its publications office. In a continent of far larger capitals, the little city on the Alzette had become, quietly, one of the capitals of Europe.

It also lent its name to a famous diplomatic fix. In 1965, France's president Charles de Gaulle was furious about plans to give the European institutions more power, and he boycotted meetings for months, in what is known as the empty chair crisis. The crisis was finally settled in January 1966 at a meeting in Luxembourg. The deal is still known as the Luxembourg Compromise, and it effectively let a country block a decision if its vital national interests were at stake. For decades afterwards, it shaped how Europe made decisions.

Meanwhile, back home, the country was rebuilding. The north, devastated by the Battle of the Bulge, was rebuilt town by town. Steel boomed in the postwar decades, as Europe needed girders, rails and machinery for reconstruction, and for years it was the largest single part of the economy. Wages rose, new housing went up, and the standard of living climbed steadily.

On the throne, there was a change of generation. Grand Duchess Charlotte, who had reigned for forty-five years and who had become the symbol of the country's survival, abdicated on the twelfth of November, 1964, in favor of her son, Jean. Jean, who had fought alongside the Allies in the war, was forty-three, and he would reign for thirty-six years.

There was another, quieter change. The steel mills needed workers, and Luxembourg's own population was too small to supply them. Starting in the 1960s, thousands of people arrived from Portugal, escaping poverty and a dictatorship at home. They worked in construction, in the steel mills, in hotels and restaurants. Over the decades, they became the largest foreign community in the country, and today people of Portuguese origin make up about one in seven residents. The Luxembourg of the postwar years was becoming a multicultural society, long before that was fashionable.

And then there was a financial first. In 1963, an Italian motorway company raised money through a new kind of bond, sold outside its home country and denominated in a foreign currency. It was the first so-called Eurobond, and it was listed on the Luxembourg stock exchange. The reason Luxembourg got the business was simple: it was friendly, flexible, discreet, and had light regulation. Over the next decade, banks from Germany, the United States, and elsewhere arrived to set up shop in the Grand Duchy. This was the seed of a new economy.

One of the people who understood where all this was going was Pierre Werner, who was prime minister and finance minister for much of the 1960s and 1970s. In 1970, European leaders asked a committee chaired by Werner to design a path toward a single European currency. The result, the Werner Report, mapped out a step-by-step plan for economic and monetary union by 1980. It was shelved after the monetary chaos of the early seventies and the oil crisis. But its ideas came back twenty years later, and the euro, launched in 1999, owes something to the plan sketched by a Luxembourg politician in 1970.

The oil crisis of 1973 and the global steel crisis that followed hit Luxembourg hard. Steel demand collapsed, and the mills in the south were suddenly in danger. In 1977, rather than let mass unemployment tear society apart, the government brought the unions and the employers together in a standing body, the Tripartite. They negotiated early retirements, retraining, and state support, avoiding the bitter strikes and layoffs that wrecked other industrial regions in Europe. This approach of consensus and dialogue became known as the Luxembourg model, and it shaped the country's politics for decades.

Finally, a lighter note. In these same years, Luxembourg quietly became a superpower of pop music. Radio Luxembourg, broadcasting in English by night on medium wave, was one of the few ways British teenagers could hear the latest records, and many remember listening under their blankets with a transistor radio. And the Eurovision Song Contest, Luxembourg won five times, in 1961, 1965, 1972, 1973, and 1983. Among the winners was the French singer France Gall, who won in 1965 with a song written by Serge Gainsbourg. A country with a few hundred thousand people won more times than many with far larger populations, helped by its commercial radio and its talent for hiring great singers from abroad.

By 1980, the Luxembourg that had been invaded and half-destroyed was a comfortable, cosmopolitan little state, with a seat at Europe's table. But the steel that had made it rich was in decline. The question was what would replace it. And the answer, as we will see, was money.

## Chapter 8: From Steel to Finance, and Luxembourg Today, 1980 to 2026

In the early 1980s, the steel industry that had built modern Luxembourg was shrinking. Blast furnaces closed, and thousands of jobs vanished. In many European industrial regions, this kind of decline led to decades of misery. Luxembourg took a different path, and the reason was that a new industry was already growing in its shadow: banking and finance.

The seeds had been planted in the 1960s with the first Eurobond. Over the following decades, the Grand Duchy built itself into a major international financial center. Banks from around the world set up offices, attracted by its stability, its multilingual workforce, its flexible laws, and its famously strong banking secrecy. In 1988, Luxembourg was the first country to write a 1985 European directive on cross-border investment funds into national law, which gave it a head start in that business. Today, it is the largest center for investment funds in Europe, second in the world only to the United States, and the financial sector accounts for roughly a quarter of the whole economy by common estimates.

The old steel company went through its own transformation. In 2002, ARBED merged with Spanish and French rivals to form a new company called Arcelor, which in 2006 was taken over by the steel group of the Indian-born businessman Lakshmi Mittal. The result was ArcelorMittal, one of the largest steelmakers in the world, which is still headquartered in Luxembourg City.

And the old steel country found surprising new uses for its industrial heritage. At Belval, in the far south near Esch, one of the biggest steelworks was shut down. Its enormous blast furnaces were not demolished. They were preserved as monuments, and around them rose a whole new district, with offices, a concert hall called the Rockhal, and the campus of the University of Luxembourg, which was founded only in 2003. Where workers once tapped molten iron, students now study for exams.

At the same time, the country kept doing what it had done since the 1950s, which was to help build Europe. In June 1985, on a passenger boat anchored on the Moselle river near the Luxembourg village of Schengen, five European countries signed an agreement to gradually abolish border checks among them. Schengen is a tiny wine-growing village, where the borders of Luxembourg, France and Germany meet. Its name is now known across Europe. The Schengen Area, which allows millions of people to cross borders without passport control, was born on that boat.

Then came the greatest step toward integration: the euro. In the early 1990s, as European leaders negotiated the Maastricht Treaty, which set the path to a single currency, Luxembourg's finance minister was a young politician named Jean-Claude Juncker. He was a crafty, informal negotiator who helped broker the compromises that made the deal possible. Juncker became prime minister in 1995 and held the job for eighteen years, longer than any other head of government in the European Union at the time. He later chaired the group of eurozone finance ministers through the debt crisis, and in 2014, he was chosen as President of the European Commission.

He was not the first Luxembourger in that role. Gaston Thorn led the Commission from 1981 to 1985, and Jacques Santer led it from 1995 to 1999. Three presidents of the Commission, from one of the smallest countries in the union, is an extraordinary record. It reflects what Luxembourg had always been good at: making deals among bigger partners, and making itself indispensable in the process.

Back at home, the language question was settled by law. In 1984, Luxembourgish, the Moselle Franconian dialect that had been spoken at home for centuries, was formally recognized as the national language. French and German remained the languages of law and administration. Today, children in Luxembourg grow up with three languages in school, and many speak English, Portuguese, or Italian at home as well. In few countries in the world is multilingualism such a normal part of daily life.

The country grew rich, and it grew crowded. Today, Luxembourg has about two thirds of a million residents, and nearly half of them hold a foreign passport. On top of that, around two hundred thousand people cross the border every day from France, Belgium and Germany to work. Per head, the Grand Duchy has one of the highest standards of living in the world. But it also has some of the highest housing costs in Europe, and the traffic jams on the roads into the capital are legendary. In 2020, the government made all public transport free for everyone, the first country in the world to do so, partly as a way of easing the pressure.

The country has also looked to the sky. A Luxembourg-based company, SES, launched the Astra satellites in the 1980s, which became one of the great satellite television success stories in Europe. In 2017, the government passed a law on space resources, and in 2018, it created its own space agency, hoping to make Luxembourg a hub for the new space economy.

The modern era has not been free of controversy. In 2008, Grand Duke Henri, who had come to the throne in 2000 on the abdication of his father Jean, announced that his conscience would not allow him to sign a bill legalizing euthanasia. The government responded by changing the constitution, so that the Grand Duke would simply promulgate laws, rather than having to approve them. It was a quiet constitutional change that further reduced the monarch's role in making laws.

And in 2014, a leak of confidential documents, known as LuxLeaks, revealed that Luxembourg had signed tax deals with hundreds of multinational companies, allowing them to pay very low taxes. The outcry across Europe was fierce, and many officials in other countries said the Grand Duchy had been undermining its neighbors. Luxembourg's government defended its legal framework but came under heavy pressure, and in 2015 it joined an international system for the automatic exchange of bank account information with other countries, effectively ending the era of strict banking secrecy for foreign account holders. The debate over what is fair in international taxation continues.

In politics, the stable Christian Social party dominated for most of the twentieth century. From 2013 to 2023, a three-party coalition of liberals, Greens and social democrats governed under Prime Minister Xavier Bettel. Then, in the election of October 2023, the Greens lost heavily, and the coalition lost its majority. A new government took office in November 2023, led by Luc Frieden of the Christian Social party, in coalition with Bettel's Democratic Party. Frieden is prime minister, and Bettel serves as deputy prime minister and foreign minister.

And there has been one more big moment, the most recent chapter of the oldest story. On the twenty-third of June, 2025, Luxembourg's National Day, Grand Duke Henri announced that he would step down after twenty-five years on the throne. On the third of October, 2025, in a ceremony at the Grand Ducal Palace, he formally abdicated in favor of his eldest son, Guillaume. Guillaume, who was forty-three, swore an oath to the constitution before the country's parliament, and greeted crowds from a balcony of the palace, with his Belgian-born wife, Stéphanie, and their two young sons beside him. He is the seventh grand duke of the modern line that began with Adolphe in 1890. Voluntary abdication has become something of a tradition in this family, since Henri himself took the throne when his father stepped aside in 2000.

So let's step back and look at the whole journey.

It began in 963 with a count and a rock. It passed through a golden age of emperors, who sat on the thrones of Prague, Budapest and Rome. It became a fortress that foreign armies fought over for centuries, passed from Burgundy to Spain to France to Austria and back to France again. It was created as a grand duchy in 1815, carved in half in 1839, saved from a war in 1867, occupied by Germany twice, and each time it recovered. It turned from farms into steel, from steel into finance, and from a nation guarding itself into one of the founding pillars of a united Europe.

Through all of it, one thing has remained consistent. The people of this small land, who have spoken three languages and lived under a dozen flags, have never stopped insisting on being themselves. The line from that old song, written in the 1850s, still works as a national motto: Mir wëlle bleiwe wat mir sinn. We want to remain what we are.

That is the story of Luxembourg. Thank you for listening, and safe travels.
