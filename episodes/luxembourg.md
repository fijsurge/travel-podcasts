Ben: Per head, it has one of the highest standards of living in the world, but also some of the highest housing costs in Europe, and legendary traffic jams on the roads into the capital. [sigh]

Anna: That's the cost of being popular.

Ben: And in 2020, the government made all public transport free for everyone, the first country in the world to do so.

Anna: Okay, that's a good solution. And it looked to the sky.

Ben: A Luxembourg-based company, SES, launched the Astra satellites in the nineteen-eighties, one of Europe's great satellite television success stories. In 2017, the government passed a law on space resources, and in 2018 it created its own space agency, hoping to become a hub for the new space economy.

Anna: From fortress to satellites. Not everything has been smooth, though.

Ben: No. In 2008, Grand Duke Henri, who had come to the throne in 2000 when his father Jean abdicated, announced that his conscience would not allow him to sign a bill legalizing euthanasia. The government responded by changing the constitution, so that the Grand Duke simply promulgates laws rather than approving them. A quiet change that further reduced the monarch's role.

Anna: And LuxLeaks.

Ben: In 2014, a leak of confidential documents revealed that Luxembourg had signed tax deals with hundreds of multinational companies, allowing them to pay very low taxes. The outcry across Europe was fierce, and many officials elsewhere said the Grand Duchy had been undermining its neighbors.

Anna: How did Luxembourg respond?

Ben: The government defended its legal framework, but came under heavy pressure. In 2015, it joined an international system for the automatic exchange of bank account information, effectively ending the era of strict banking secrecy for foreign account holders. The debate over what is fair in international taxation continues.

Anna: And the politics today?

Ben: The Christian Social party dominated for most of the twentieth century. From 2013 to 2023, a three-party coalition of liberals, Greens and social democrats governed under Prime Minister Xavier Bettel. In the election of October 2023, the Greens lost heavily and the coalition lost its majority.

Anna: So who's in charge now?

Ben: A new government took office in November 2023, led by Luc Frieden of the Christian Social party, in coalition with Bettel's Democratic Party. Frieden is prime minister, and Bettel is deputy prime minister and foreign minister.

Anna: And one more big moment. The most recent chapter of the oldest story.

Ben: On the twenty-third of June, 2025, Luxembourg's National Day, Grand Duke Henri announced that he would step down after twenty-five years on the throne. On the third of October, 2025, in a ceremony at the Grand Ducal Palace, he formally abdicated in favor of his eldest son, Guillaume.

Anna: Who was forty-three.

Anna: Seventh, in a line going back to 1890.

Ben: Voluntary abdication has become something of a tradition in this family, since Henri himself took the throne when his father stepped aside in 2000.

Anna: All right. Let's step back and look at the whole journey.

Ben: It began in nine sixty-three with a count and a rock. It passed through a golden age of emperors who sat on the thrones of Prague, Budapest and Rome. Anna: Then it was created as a grand duchy in 1815.

Ben: Carved in half in 1839, saved from a war in 1867, occupied by Germany twice, and each time it recovered. It turned from farms into steel, from steel into finance, and from a nation guarding itself into one of the founding pillars of a united Europe.
