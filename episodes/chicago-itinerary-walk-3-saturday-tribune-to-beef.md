## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A short walk with two stops, designed for Saturday, December 5. The first stop is a quick stroll from the hotel. The second is about eighteen minutes on foot, with an Italian beef lunch as the destination. Stop positions are approximate, so keep a map open. Allow a little extra time at the stand, since it can have a line. The stones are best seen at ground level and up to about head height.

## Welcome

Anna: Hello, and welcome to the first walk of Saturday. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. Two stops. One building made of the world, and one sandwich.

Anna: A sandwich is a stop.

Ben: A sandwich is the stop.

Anna: I'm in. And I've got a bet. The sandwich will be on fire with something called giardiniera.

Ben: It will be hot, if you ask for hot. We'll get there.

## Stop 1: Tribune Tower

Anna: Okay. Michigan Avenue, over the bridge, and there's a Gothic castle.

Ben: That's Tribune Tower. Finished in 1925. It's thirty-six floors and four hundred and sixty-three feet tall, and it began with a contest.

Anna: Of course it did.

Ben: In 1922 the Chicago Tribune turned seventy-five and announced an international competition to design what it called the most beautiful office building in the world.

Anna: Modest.

Ben: The prize was a hundred thousand dollars, with fifty thousand to the winner. More than two hundred and sixty entries arrived from twenty-three countries.

Anna: Who won?

Ben: A pair of New York architects, Raymond Hood and John Mead Howells. The style is Gothic Revival. The crown is based on a tower from Rouen Cathedral in France, a thirteenth-century tower, and the lower floors use Art Deco-style piers.

Anna: So a medieval church on top of an office block.

Ben: Basically. Now come down to the sidewalk, because this is the part you came for. Look at the base of the walls. Some stones are different from the rest.

Anna: Oh, I see them. There's a little label.

Ben: More than a hundred and forty of those. They're fragments from famous places around the world, set into the walls.

Anna: Which ones?

Ben: A piece of the Great Wall of China. A piece of the Parthenon in Athens. A piece from the Taj Mahal. A piece of the Great Pyramid at Giza. A fragment of Notre-Dame, one from the Alamo, one from the Colosseum.

Anna: And the Berlin Wall.

Ben: That too. Walk along and find it. The labels say where each piece came from.

Anna: Who collected them?

Ben: Colonel Robert McCormick, the Tribune's co-publisher, spent decades collecting. Tribune correspondents brought back the earliest pieces at his request, and readers contributed specimens too.

Anna: So the newspaper's reporters were also carrying rocks.

Ben: In their luggage. Yes.

Anna: One more. There's a moon rock in there?

Ben: There's a commemorative stone for the Apollo missions. And one guide says it's actually ordinary earth stone, honoring the moon missions, not a real lunar rock.

Anna: A pretend moon rock. A decoy.

Ben: A tribute. It's fine.

Anna: Okay, that's kind of delightful. [chuckle]

Ben: Take your time, and pause here. You can circle the building. When you're ready, the second stop is about eighteen minutes' walk northwest, through the River North gallery district.

## Stop 2: Mr. Beef on Orleans

Anna: And we are at the sandwich.

Ben: You're at the corner of Orleans and Erie. Mr. Beef opened in 1979 as a stand, founded by Joe Zucchero, and his family still runs it.

Anna: And the story is that it's the Bear restaurant.

Ben: It inspired the television show The Bear, and its storefront is the one in the show. One account says the sales went up from about three hundred sandwiches a day to more than eight hundred.

Anna: I'll go along with the crowd. Tell me about Italian beef.

Ben: It starts in the early twentieth century. Italian immigrant families were often sold cheaper, tougher cuts of meat, like the roast or the ends of the cow.

Anna: And they made it work.

Ben: They cooked it for a long time to tenderize it, sliced it very thin to stretch the supply, and served it on fresh bread. It was common at wedding gatherings. Historians largely believe a shop called Al's was the first to sell it, starting in the 1930s.

Anna: So a sandwich that comes from making do.

Ben: It's a hard-times sandwich, yes. Thin beef, sweet peppers or the pickled vegetable relish called giardiniera, in an Italian loaf, with the meat's juices on top.

Anna: What's the order?

Ben: One Chicago historian orders his lightly dipped, to avoid soggy bread, with both hot and mild peppers and a little mustard. That's his order, not a rule.

Anna: Light dip. Hot and mild. I'm remembering that.

Ben: The menu also has hamburgers, hot dogs, fries and pizza puffs. And the restaurant has been on television, including on The Tonight Show.

Anna: So I order, I eat, I stand in line.

Ben: In that order, probably the other way around.

Anna: I'll bet you a dollar I get beef on my sleeve. [laugh]

Ben: I'll take that bet.
