## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A short walk with four stops, designed for Friday, December 4. It comes in two pieces. The first two stops fit the lunch walk to Miller's Pub on Wabash Avenue, about eight minutes south of the hotel. The last two stops are for the evening, on the way to dinner on the north bank of the river and back to the hotel's Wacker Drive entrance. Pause between the pieces. Stop positions are approximate, so keep a map open.

## Welcome

Anna: Hello, and welcome to another short walk. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. It's Friday in Chicago. Four stops, in two pieces, because the work day sits in the middle.

Anna: Lunch walk, then dinner walk.

Ben: Exactly. And a theme. Both pieces are about the infrastructure that holds Chicago up.

Anna: Infrastructure. Ben, it's Friday.

Ben: Anna, it's a train, a pub, a road, and a bridge. That's four fun things.

Anna: Fine. I'll bet that you can't make a road interesting.

Ben: We'll get there.

## Stop 1: The trains over Wabash Avenue

Anna: Okay. I'm walking south and there's a giant steel thing over my head.

Ben: That's the elevated track for the L, Chicago's elevated train. Stand under it for a moment, and listen when a train goes over.

Anna: Loud.

Ben: Yes. It always has been. Chicago's first L train ran on June 6, 1892, from Thirty-ninth Street and State Street to Congress Parkway and Wabash Avenue.

Anna: Why put trains in the air at all?

Ben: Because the streets were choked. Horse-drawn streetcars, and then cable cars, couldn't carry the crowds as the city grew, and the cars added to the congestion.

Anna: So they went up.

Ben: A businessman named Charles Yerkes built elevated tracks above downtown streets to connect the separate lines. That's the Loop. The first full circuit of it was finished in 1897.

Anna: Why's it called the Loop?

Ben: The name is said to come from the cable cars that once looped around several downtown blocks. Then it stuck to the whole downtown.

Anna: So when people say "the Loop," they mean the area, and the tracks, and the cable cars?

Ben: All three. Now look at the steel. It was designed by the bridge designer John Alexander Low Waddell, and it's riveted steel plate. One architecture guide says it resembles the Eiffel Tower and the first Ferris wheel, which were built just before.

Anna: An elevated railroad that looks like the Eiffel Tower.

Ben: A little. And it's still working after more than a century. Since 1993 the lines have color names, but the old structure is the same.

Anna: Okay, that's cool. A bit. Don't tell Ben I said cool.

Ben: I'm Ben.

Anna: Right. Don't tell yourself.

Ben: Keep walking south on Wabash. The pub is just ahead, beside the tracks. About a four-minute walk.

## Stop 2: Miller's Pub

Anna: Neon sign. "Now serving Tom and Jerry." Do they mean the cartoon?

Ben: No. It's a holiday drink. A hot, dairy-rich, eggnog-style punch with plenty of liquor, with origins more than two centuries old. This place serves it from a big batter bowl behind the bar, in brown ceramic mugs.

Anna: At lunch?

Ben: It's Chicago and it's winter. I'll let you decide.

Anna: Okay, tell me about the pub.

Ben: It's been here since 1935. It's called Miller's, but the owners aren't Millers.

Anna: What?

Ben: Three brothers, Pete, Nick and Jimmy Gallios, bought it from the original Millers in 1950, and a fourth brother joined in 1953. They kept the name because they couldn't afford a new sign.

Anna: That's the best business decision I've heard all day.

Ben: The original site was on nearby Adams Street. It's a parking garage now, and the current building used to hold two other Gallios restaurants.

Anna: So they moved a pub twenty feet and called it history.

Ben: Not quite. But close. The walls are covered in photos of the brothers and of celebrity visitors, especially sports figures. Performers from the Palmer House hotel's Empire Room often ended their nights here.

Anna: Famous people eating ribs at midnight.

Ben: Canadian baby back ribs, served by the slab or the slab and a half. And the pub is typically open very late, until four in the morning.

Anna: At that hour I want pizza.

Ben: Different walk. This lunch stop ends the first piece. Pause the walk, and the second piece is the evening.

## Stop 3: Wacker Drive

Anna: Okay, it's evening. Where are we?

Ben: Outside the hotel, on the south side of the river, on Wacker Drive. Head west, along the river, and let me prove that a road can be interesting.

Anna: The bet.

Ben: The bet. Wacker Drive is named for Charles H. Wacker, an early twentieth-century businessman and city planner. The first section was completed in 1926 at a cost of eight million dollars. It replaced two older streets, South Water Street and River Street.

Anna: Okay. Two streets became one. Not thrilling yet.

Ben: It's a double-decker road. There's an upper level, where you're standing, and a lower level for service vehicles, delivery trucks, waste collection and utilities.

Anna: So that's why Chicago has Lower Wacker.

Ben: Yes. If you've seen a movie car chase under Chicago, that's the same lower level. Some sections have a third level too, used for towing, impound, utilities and railroad maintenance.

Anna: A road with a basement and a sub-basement.

Ben: And the road follows the south bank of the main branch of the Chicago River through the Loop. In the early 2000s the stretch from Michigan Avenue to Lake Street was redesigned with walkways along the river to be more pedestrian-friendly.

Anna: So we're walking on a redesign.

Ben: We are.

Anna: Fine. A double-decker road is mildly interesting. Mildly. Don't push it.

Ben: Noted. Keep going west to LaSalle Street, and the bridge.

## Stop 4: The LaSalle Street Bridge

Anna: Okay. A bridge again.

Ben: A different one. This is the LaSalle Street Bridge. It opened on December 20, 1928, and it carries LaSalle Street over the main stem of the river. It connects the Loop with the Near North Side.

Anna: Was it new, then, like the Michigan Avenue one?

Ben: Close. Widening LaSalle Street and improving the Loop's access to the north side was proposed as early as 1902, and the design was approved in 1916. The designer was Donald Becker, and a company called Strobel Steel built it, for two and a half million dollars.

Anna: Two and a half million dollars. In 1928.

Ben: It's a single-deck, double-leaf bascule bridge. That means two halves of the roadway lift upward so boats can pass. The same idea as the one you crossed yesterday, but only one deck.

Anna: And does it have a name?

Ben: It does. In 1999 the Chicago City Council renamed it the Marshall Suloway Bridge, after a former commissioner of the Department of Public Works.

Anna: Does anyone call it that?

Ben: Mostly, people still say LaSalle Street Bridge.

Anna: Naturally.

Ben: Cross to the north bank. The steakhouse is right there, in a glass-walled dining room on the river. Pause, enjoy dinner, and then come back.

Anna: And the walk back?

Ben: Same bridge, same river, but look at it differently. You'll be walking east along Wacker Drive to the hotel's Wacker entrance, about twelve minutes. And Bar Pendry is right inside, with its fireplace.

Anna: A fireplace. Finally, a promise.

Ben: And you don't have to go outside again afterward. The elevator goes up to your room.

## Wrap-up

Anna: So that's Friday. A train over the street, a pub with the wrong name, a road with a basement, and a bridge with two names.

Ben: Did I make the road interesting?

Anna: A road with a basement. That's a good one. Fine. I lose the bet. [laugh]

Ben: Tomorrow, Saturday, and the last day. More architecture, and a very famous sandwich.

Anna: Now you're talking.
