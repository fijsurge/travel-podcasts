## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

An overview for a one-day visit to Antwerp, about twelve minutes, for people who want the spooky side. It keeps two things apart on purpose: legends, which are stories people tell, and documented history, which is what the sources support. The legends are labelled as legends. Where the sources were thin, the script says less rather than guessing. For the general briefing, play "Antwerp in 15 Minutes".

## Welcome

Anna: Evening. You're nearly in Antwerp. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. You have one day, and tonight we have about twelve minutes.

Anna: And tonight's theme is the spooky stuff. Ghosts. Giants. Dark history.

Ben: With one rule. We keep legend and history in separate boxes.

Anna: Boring.

Ben: Useful. When you stand in front of a statue tomorrow, you'll know which kind of story you're hearing.

Anna: Fine. I'll say it out loud. A legend is a story. History is the paperwork.

Ben: Close enough.

Anna: Five chapters. A giant, a shapeshifter, a stone fortress, a riot, and a massacre.

Ben: The first three are legends. The last two are real.

Anna: Cheerful. Let's go.

## Chapter 1: The giant and the hand

Anna: Start me with the big one. Literally.

Ben: Okay. This is a legend, not history. Long ago, a giant called Druon Antigoon lived by the river Scheldt.

Anna: Of course he did.

Ben: He guarded a crossing and charged a toll. Anyone who couldn't pay lost a hand. He cut it off and threw it into the river.

Anna: That's a very specific business model.

Ben: Then a young Roman soldier called Silvius Brabo arrives. He beats the giant, cuts off the giant's own hand, and throws it into the river.

Anna: Poetic justice. With a knife.

Ben: And the story says that's how the city got its name. The Dutch name is Antwerpen. The tale reads it as "hand werpen," which means hand throwing.

Anna: Oh, that's good. So the whole city is named after a thrown hand.

Ben: Not quite. That's the folk explanation. The sources I checked say some specialists dispute it. Another idea connects the name to a word for a jetty or landing place by the water.

Anna: A jetty. Much less fun.

Ben: Much less fun.

Anna: So where do I see the hand?

Ben: The main square, the Grote Markt, in front of the town hall. There's a fountain by the sculptor Jef Lambeaux, put up in 1887. Brabo stands on top, about to throw. At the bottom, the hand is already in the air.

Anna: And I'm not allowed to pretend it's real.

Ben: You can pretend. Just remember it's a legend.

Anna: Noted. Bet number one. Somebody tonight mentions another hand.

Ben: We'll get there. Next, a tall one.

## Chapter 2: The shapeshifter at the castle

Anna: Tall? Another giant?

Ben: A different one. Lange Wapper. Another legend, from Antwerp's folklore.

Anna: Give me the short version.

Ben: He's a shapeshifter. In the version I found, he starts as a baby, raised by an Antwerp family, and grows into a giant who can change size and shape.

Anna: Is he nice?

Ben: Mixed. He helps people at first. Later he's a trickster. He frightens drunks at night, and plays tricks on children.

Anna: So, a night-time prankster.

Ben: And a nuisance. One detail people like: he disliked the Virgin Mary. So the story goes that residents put little statues of her on their houses to keep him away.

Anna: Wait. That's why there are Madonnas on the corners of the buildings?

Ben: Not quite. Antwerp does have many street-corner Madonnas, but I can't tell you from my sources that Wapper is the reason. That's the story. Treat it as a story.

Anna: Fair. And how does it end?

Ben: Two endings. He leaves the city for good. Or he drowns in the Scheldt.

Anna: Two endings is cheating.

Ben: It's folklore. It does that.

Anna: Where do I find him?

Ben: By the river, in front of Het Steen, the old fortress. There's a bronze statue of him by the sculptor Albert Poels, from 1963. You can't miss it. He's a giant.

Anna: I'll keep my eyes up.

Ben: And Het Steen is next, because it has its own dark side.

## Chapter 3: The stone fortress

Anna: Het Steen. Is that the castle?

Ben: What survives is the gateway to a bigger castle. It was built between 1200 and 1225. The rest was torn down in the 1800s.

Anna: And the dark part?

Ben: Now we're back in history. It was used as a prison. From 1303 to 1827.

Anna: That's more than five hundred years.

Ben: Five hundred and twenty-four, if you count it. I don't have good sources on who was held there or how they were treated, so I'm not going to make that up.

Anna: Good. No invented dungeon.

Ben: There's also an odd carved relief over the archway. It's a figure called Semini. The story is that women once prayed to him for children, and later church people disapproved. Citizens were even called children of Semini.

Anna: Is that real?

Ben: The tradition is old. The details, including where the figure came from, are murky. Call it a local tradition and leave it there.

Anna: So three layers at one gate. A real prison, a tangled tradition, and a giant out front.

Ben: Right. Take a photo, and then walk up to the cathedral.

Anna: Teaser?

Ben: In the cathedral, there's a night in 1566 when the church looked like hell. That's from a witness.

## Chapter 4: The night the saints fell

Anna: Okay. Real history now. No jokes?

Ben: No jokes. Not for this one, and not for the next.

Anna: Understood.

Ben: In the summer of 1566, a wave of attacks on Catholic churches swept the Low Countries. It's called the Beeldenstorm, which means the storm of images. People smashed statues, altars and church fittings.

Anna: Why?

Ben: It was part of the religious conflict of the Reformation. Some Protestants considered religious images idolatry.

Anna: And it reached Antwerp.

Ben: On 20 August 1566. The Cathedral of Our Lady was badly damaged inside. An English merchant, Richard Clough, was there. He wrote that the church looked like a hell, with above 10,000 torches burning.

Anna: That's a line you don't forget.

Ben: It's one reason this night is remembered. Statues fell. Organs were wrecked. Books were burned.

Anna: And the building today? It looks perfect.

Ben: It survived, and was repaired, and it survived more. In 1794, French revolutionary troops plundered it and did serious damage. Around 1798 the French authorities even planned to demolish it. It was spared.

Anna: And the cathedral itself has one more odd feature.

Ben: The towers. Two were planned, equal in height. Only the north tower was finished, rising 123 meters. A fire in 1533 delayed the second, and it was never completed.

Anna: So it's lopsided on purpose?

Ben: On purpose is too strong. It's lopsided because of fire, money and time.

Anna: And the well?

Ben: Next to the cathedral on the Handschoenmarkt there's an ornate iron well cover, from the 1500s. It is traditionally attributed to Quinten Matsys, the artist. Only traditionally. I couldn't confirm it from a firm source.

Anna: Tradition again.

Ben: It's that kind of city.

## Chapter 5: The Spanish Fury

Anna: And the other real one.

Ben: This is the darkest chapter of the day, and we'll tell it plainly.

Anna: Go on.

Ben: In 1576, Spain was fighting in the Low Countries and its soldiers had not been paid. Troops mutinied. In November, mutinous Spanish troops came to Antwerp.

Anna: And then?

Ben: They attacked the city. It's known as the Spanish Fury. They killed people and burned buildings. The town hall was damaged by fire.

Anna: How many died?

Ben: Estimates differ widely. Some accounts say about 6,000 people. Others go much higher, up to 17,000 men, women and children. Around 800 to 1,000 buildings were burned, depending on the source.

Anna: That's a huge range.

Ben: It is. Nobody counted carefully in the middle of a massacre. The honest answer is that we don't know the exact number. We do know it was a catastrophe.

Anna: And what came after?

Ben: It shocked the region. Within days a treaty called the Pacification of Ghent was signed, calling for the expulsion of Spanish troops.

Anna: So it changed the politics.

Ben: Yes. And it still shapes how people remember the city.

Anna: Where do I feel it tomorrow?

Ben: On the Grote Markt, in front of the town hall. It's the same square as the fountain with the thrown hand. A legend on one side, a real tragedy on the other.

Anna: Hm. That's a strange square.

Ben: It is.

## Wrap-up

Anna: Okay. Let's put it in boxes.

Ben: Legends. Brabo and the giant's hand. Lange Wapper. The tales around Semini. The idea that a thrown hand named the city.

Anna: And history.

Ben: The prison at Het Steen, from 1303 to 1827. The Beeldenstorm on 20 August 1566. The French plundering in 1794. And the Spanish Fury, in November 1576.

Anna: And my bet. Somebody mentioned a hand. Twice.

Ben: Technically it was the same hand.

Anna: I'll take it.

Ben: Have a good day tomorrow. Look up at the towers, and read the plaques.

Anna: And be kind to the giants.
