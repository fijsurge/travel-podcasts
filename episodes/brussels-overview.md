## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

An overview for the morning of a Brussels day, about fifteen minutes. It is written to be heard, with no tables. It leans on the same checked facts as the longer Brussels episode. For the full story, play "The Story of Brussels".

## Welcome

Anna: Morning. You're in Brussels. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. You've got a day, and we've got fifteen minutes.

Anna: Think of it as the briefing before the mission.

Ben: Three things to know about the city. Five things worth seeing. Three stories for your pocket. And one plan for when it rains.

Anna: It will rain.

Ben: It's Brussels. It might rain while we're talking.

Anna: Okay. Start me off. What is this place?

## The big picture

Ben: Two things at once. A small, very old Belgian city that grew up on a muddy river. And the main home of the European Union.

Anna: And those two don't look much alike.

Ben: Not at all. In one walk, you go from a medieval guild square to a glass office tower full of EU officials.

Anna: Let's do the mud first.

Ben: The river is called the Senne. And the name of the city comes from an old word meaning a home in the marsh.

Anna: Charming.

Ben: Honest, at least. People settled along that river, merchants on the road between Flanders and Cologne stopped here, and a market town grew.

Anna: How old are we talking?

Ben: The date people usually give is around the year nine seventy-nine. A Frankish nobleman called Charles moved the relics of a saint named Gudula to a chapel on an island in the river. Brussels celebrated its thousandth birthday in 1979.

Anna: Is that exactly true?

Ben: Historians say the story is probably tidier than the reality.

Anna: But the party was already booked.

Ben: The date stuck. Then came centuries of other people's empires. Burgundy, Spain, Austria, France, the Netherlands.

Anna: Brussels has had a lot of landlords.

Ben: And in 1830, a revolution. Belgium becomes its own country, with Brussels as the capital.

Anna: And the second thing? The EU.

Ben: After the Second World War, Belgium was one of the six countries that founded what became the European Union. Brussels ended up with the European Commission and the Council, and the Parliament does much of its work here too.

Anna: Which is why there are so many flags.

Ben: And so many languages. Which is the third thing.

Anna: Go on.

Ben: Brussels is officially bilingual, French and Dutch. Every street sign has both. In daily life, most people speak French, but you'll hear Dutch, English, and dozens of other languages. Nearly four in ten residents hold a foreign passport.

Anna: So don't be surprised if someone switches language mid-sentence.

Ben: Don't be surprised. And don't feel bad about starting in English.

Anna: I'll start in English and smile a lot.

Ben: A solid strategy.

## Five things worth seeing

Anna: Okay. If I only had a day. What are the five?

Ben: One. The Grand Place. A paved square, about sixty-eight meters by a hundred and ten, surrounded by guild houses with gilded decoration, and a Gothic town hall with a tower about ninety-six meters high.

Anna: The heart of the city.

Ben: And here's the surprise. Most of it isn't medieval.

Anna: What?

Ben: In August of 1695, a French army under Marshal Villeroi shelled the city for two days, and most of the square was destroyed. Only the stone shell of the town hall and a few fragments survived.

Anna: But it looks perfect.

Ben: The guilds and owners rebuilt it in about four years. So when you look at those golden facades, you're looking at a very fast, very proud recovery. UNESCO listed it in 1998.

Anna: Four years. My kitchen has taken longer.

Ben: Your kitchen didn't have the guilds competing.

Anna: Fair. Number two.

Ben: The Manneken Pis. A small bronze boy, about fifty-five centimeters tall, relieving himself into a fountain basin on a street corner, a few minutes from the Grand Place.

Anna: And I bet you a coffee that you'll say "that's it?" out loud.

Ben: Everyone does. People are always disappointed by how small he is.

Anna: I win.

Ben: You haven't even been there yet. But he has a wardrobe of about a thousand costumes, which have their own museum nearby.

Anna: A thousand outfits. For a statue.

Ben: And there are two or three competing legends about why he exists. My favorite is the baby duke of Leuven, who supposedly relieved himself on enemy troops from a basket in a tree.

Anna: A tiny duke, in a tree, in a basket.

Ben: Winning a battle.

Anna: Number three.

Ben: The Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert. A glass-roofed shopping arcade, opened in June of 1847, designed by an architect named Jean-Pierre Cluysenaar. It's about two hundred and thirteen meters long and has three sections. The King's, the Queen's, and the Princes' galleries.

Anna: And it's still a shopping arcade?

Ben: With chocolate shops, a theater, a cinema, bookshops, and the Taverne du Passage. It's also where the Lumière brothers' films were shown in public in Brussels, in 1896.

Anna: Number four.

Ben: The Boulevard Anspach and the Bourse. Underneath that wide pedestrian boulevard, the Senne is still flowing, in a tunnel.

Anna: They buried a river.

Ben: Between 1867 and 1871. The river was polluted and flooded, so the city covered it over and built grand boulevards on top, modeled on Paris. The Bourse, the old stock exchange, went up between 1868 and 1873. And since 2015, the boulevards around it have been mostly car-free.

Anna: A river under a pedestrian street. Brussels does like to hide things.

Ben: And five. The Atomium.

Anna: The giant atom.

Ben: A giant steel model of an iron crystal, built for the 1958 World's Fair. You can ride up inside it for a view over the city. It's out on the northern edge, so it's a metro ride away.

Anna: That's a half-day trip.

Ben: It is. So pick one or two of these and enjoy them, rather than rushing through all five.

Anna: Rushing through Brussels. Never works.

Ben: Never.

## Three stories to carry in your pocket

Anna: Facts are great, but I want stories. Three I can tell over a coffee.

Ben: The coffee you won.

Anna: The coffee I won.

Ben: Story one. A night raid.

Anna: Ooh.

Ben: In 1356, the Count of Flanders invades Brussels and takes it. A citizen called Everard t'Serclaes, from one of the great families, leads a night raid with a small band of men. They slip into the city and drive the Flemish garrison out.

Anna: A local hero.

Ben: A very local one. There's a monument to him in the Rue Charles Buls, just off the Grand Place. And the tradition is that you touch the bronze for luck.

Anna: I'll be touching it.

Ben: Everyone does. It's very shiny.

Anna: Story two?

Ben: A night at the opera.

Anna: Two nights, two stories. I like the pattern.

Ben: On the twenty-fifth of August, 1830, there's a performance at the Théâtre de la Monnaie of a popular French opera called The Mute Girl of Portici. It's about a revolt against Spanish rule in seventeenth-century Naples, and it has a stirring duet about love of country.

Anna: And the audience took it personally.

Ben: They poured out into the street, and the unrest turned into a revolution. Within weeks, the southern provinces of the Netherlands had broken away, and Belgium was an independent country with Brussels as its capital.

Anna: From a duet to a country.

Ben: In a few weeks.

Anna: I can't get my neighbors to agree on a bin schedule.

Ben: Story three is smaller. Saint Gudula, the patron saint of the city. The legend says she walked to church before dawn, carrying a lantern. The devil kept blowing it out, and each time, an angel lit it again.

Anna: So the patron saint of a capital is a woman, a lantern, and a very persistent angel.

Ben: Persistent is the word. It tells you something about the city's character.

Anna: Stubbornness, again.

Ben: It comes up a lot.

## Beyond the old center

Anna: And if I have more than a day? Or I'm sick of cobblestones?

Ben: Then go and look at three other Brussels. The first is Art Nouveau.

Anna: Curves and flowers.

Ben: In the eighteen-nineties, a young architect named Victor Horta built a townhouse for a professor called Emile Tassel. Iron columns shaped like plant stems, stairs that curl like vines, and light pouring down from above. In 1893 and 1894. It's widely regarded as the first Art Nouveau building.

Anna: And there's a museum.

Ben: The Horta Museum, in Saint-Gilles, in the house where he lived and worked. Several of his houses are now UNESCO World Heritage. If you want just one museum, I'd pick that.

Anna: What's the second Brussels?

Ben: Comics.

Anna: Tintin!

Ben: A young Brussels cartoonist named Georges Remi, who signed his work Hergé, published the first adventure of a young reporter with a quiff of red hair, in a children's supplement, in January of 1929.

Anna: That quiff is iconic.

Ben: And Belgium's comic strip tradition has made Brussels a world capital of the form. You'll find big comic murals on the sides of buildings around the center, if you keep your eyes up.

Anna: Eyes up. Got it. And the third?

Ben: The European quarter. Take the metro to Schuman station and walk out. You'll face the Berlaymont, the star-shaped headquarters of the European Commission.

Anna: It's not exactly cute.

Ben: It's not charming in the way the Grand Place is. But it tells you what the city is for now.

Anna: A slightly different kind of landmark.

Ben: A different kind, and every bit as much part of the story.

## A few things to know

Anna: Practical stuff. What's the vibe?

Ben: A city of cafés and small museums, and the weather is changeable. Carry a light jacket. The center is quite walkable, with cobbled streets and hills, so wear good shoes.

Anna: And the food everybody talks about?

Ben: Belgian beer culture was recognized by UNESCO in 2016. Chocolate, waffles, and fries are everywhere. And the praline is traditionally credited to a Brussels chocolatier named Neuhaus, who has a shop in the Galeries.

Anna: Tourist traps?

Ben: The streets right around the Grand Place can be crowded and pricey. Walk two or three streets away and you'll usually find something better.

Anna: And if it rains?

Ben: Then go indoors, and Brussels is good at that. The Galeries are covered. There are museums. And a lot of cafés that exist just to give you somewhere to wait the weather out.

Anna: So what's the one thing to take away from all this?

Ben: That Brussels gets knocked down and rebuilt, and keeps its sense of humor. A small boy on a fountain, in a different costume every week, tells you most of it.

Anna: Right. Go have a good day.

Ben: And if you want the whole story, the long episode will be waiting for you tonight.

Anna: And if it rains?

Ben: Then you've got the Galeries, and a very good waffle.
