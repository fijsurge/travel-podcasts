## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

An overview for the morning of a Brussels day, about fifteen minutes. It is written to be heard, with no tables. It leans on the same checked facts as the longer Brussels episode. For the full story, play "The Story of Brussels".

## Welcome

Anna: Morning. You're in Brussels, and you've got a day. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. We're going to give you fifteen minutes of context, so that everything you walk past today means something.

Anna: Think of it as the briefing before the mission.

Ben: Exactly that. Three things to know about the city, five things worth seeing, and one thing to do if it rains.

Anna: Okay. Start me off. What is this place?

## The big picture

Ben: Brussels is two things at once. It's a small, very old Belgian city that grew up on a muddy river. And it's the main home of the European Union.

Anna: And those two things don't always look alike.

Ben: They really don't. In one walk you can go from a medieval guild square to a glass office tower full of EU officials.

Anna: Let's do the mud first.

Ben: The river is called the Senne. The name of the city comes from an old word meaning a home in the marsh. People settled along that river, merchants on the road between Flanders and Cologne stopped here, and a market town grew.

Anna: How old are we talking?

Ben: The date people usually give for the founding is around the year nine seventy-nine, when a Frankish nobleman called Charles moved the relics of a saint named Gudula to a chapel on an island in the river. Brussels celebrated its thousandth birthday in 1979.

Anna: Is that exactly true?

Ben: Historians say the story is probably tidier than the reality. But it's the date that stuck.

Anna: Okay, that's the old city. Then what?

Ben: Then centuries of other people's empires. Burgundy, Spain, Austria, France, and the Netherlands, and then in 1830 a revolution, and Belgium becomes its own country with Brussels as the capital.

Anna: And the second thing? The EU.

Ben: After the Second World War, Belgium was one of the six countries that founded what became the European Union. Brussels ended up with the European Commission and the Council, and the Parliament does much of its work here too. That's why you'll see so many flags and so many languages.

Anna: Languages. That's the third thing.

Ben: The third thing. Brussels is officially bilingual, French and Dutch. Every street sign has both. In daily life, French is spoken by most people, but you'll hear Dutch, English, and dozens of other languages. Nearly four in ten residents hold a foreign passport.

Anna: So don't be surprised if somebody switches language mid-sentence.

Ben: Don't be surprised, and don't feel bad about starting with English.

## Five things worth seeing

Anna: Okay. If I only had a day. What are the five?

Ben: First, the Grand Place. It's a paved square about sixty-eight metres by a hundred and ten, surrounded by guild houses with gilded decoration, and a Gothic town hall with a tower about ninety-six metres high.

Anna: And that's the heart.

Ben: That's the heart. And what's amazing is that most of it isn't medieval. In August of 1695, a French army under Marshal Villeroi shelled the city for two days, and most of the square was destroyed. Only the stone shell of the town hall and a few fragments survived.

Anna: And it looks like that now?

Ben: The guilds and owners rebuilt it in about four years. So when you look at those golden facades, you're looking at a very fast, very proud recovery. UNESCO listed it in 1998.

Anna: Number two.

Ben: The Manneken Pis. A small bronze boy, about fifty-five centimetres tall, relieving himself into a fountain basin on a street corner a few minutes from the Grand Place.

Anna: People are always disappointed by how small he is.

Ben: Everyone. But he has a wardrobe of about a thousand costumes, which have their own museum nearby. And there are two or three competing legends about why he exists, including a baby duke of Leuven who supposedly urinated on enemy troops from a basket in a tree.

Anna: Number three.

Ben: The Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert. A glass-roofed shopping arcade, opened in June of 1847, designed by an architect named Jean-Pierre Cluysenaar. It's about two hundred and thirteen metres long and has three sections, the King's, the Queen's, and the Princes' galleries.

Anna: And it's still a shopping arcade.

Ben: With chocolate shops, a theatre, a cinema, bookshops, and the Taverne du Passage. It's also where the Lumière brothers' films were shown in public in Brussels in 1896.

Anna: Four.

Ben: The Boulevard Anspach and the Bourse. Underneath that wide pedestrian boulevard, the Senne is still flowing, in a tunnel. Between 1867 and 1871, the city buried the river because it was polluted and flooded, and built grand boulevards on top, modeled on Paris. The Bourse, the old stock exchange, was built between 1868 and 1873. Since 2015, the boulevards around it have been mostly car-free.

Anna: And five?

Ben: The Atomium. A giant steel model of an iron crystal, built for the 1958 World's Fair, which you can ride up inside for a view over the city. It's out on the northern edge, so it's a metro ride away.

Anna: That's more of a half-day trip, then.

Ben: It is. Pick one or two of these and enjoy them, rather than rushing through all five.

## Three stories to carry in your pocket

Anna: Facts are great, but I want stories. Give me three I can tell somebody over a coffee.

Ben: Okay. Story one. A night raid.

Anna: Ooh.

Ben: In 1356, the Count of Flanders invades Brussels and takes it. A citizen called Everard t'Serclaes, from one of the great families, leads a night raid with a small band of men, slips into the city, and drives the Flemish garrison out.

Anna: A local hero.

Ben: A very local one. There's a monument to him in the Rue Charles Buls, just off the Grand Place. And the tradition is that you touch the bronze for luck.

Anna: I'll be touching it.

Ben: Everyone does. It's shiny. Story two. A night at the opera.

Anna: Two nights, two stories.

Ben: On the twenty-fifth of August, 1830, there's a performance at the Théâtre de la Monnaie of a popular French opera called The Mute Girl of Portici. It's about a revolt against Spanish rule in seventeenth-century Naples, and it has a stirring duet about love of country.

Anna: And the audience took it personally.

Ben: They poured out into the street, and the unrest turned into a revolution. Within weeks, the southern provinces of the Netherlands had broken away, and Belgium was an independent country with Brussels as its capital.

Anna: From a duet to a country.

Ben: Story three is smaller. It's Saint Gudula, the patron saint of the city. The legend says she walked to church before dawn carrying a lantern, and the devil kept blowing it out, and each time an angel lit it again.

Anna: And that's the patron saint of a whole capital.

Ben: A woman, a lantern, and a stubborn angel. It tells you something about the city's character.

## Beyond the old center

Anna: And if I have more than a day, or I'm bored of cobblestones?

Ben: Then you go and look at three other Brussels. The first is Art Nouveau. In the eighteen-nineties, a young architect named Victor Horta built a townhouse for a professor called Emile Tassel, with iron columns shaped like plant stems, stairs that curl like vines, and light pouring down from above. In 1893 and 1894, widely regarded as the first Art Nouveau building. Several of Horta's houses are now UNESCO World Heritage.

Anna: And the Horta Museum is in his own house.

Ben: In Saint-Gilles, where he lived and worked. If you want one museum, I'd pick that.

Anna: What's the second Brussels?

Ben: Comics. A young Brussels cartoonist named Georges Remi, who signed his work Hergé, published the first adventure of a young reporter with a quiff of red hair in a children's supplement in January of 1929.

Anna: Tintin.

Ben: And Belgium's comic strip tradition has made Brussels a world capital of the form. You'll find big comic murals on the sides of buildings around the center, if you keep your eyes up.

Anna: And the third?

Ben: The European quarter. If you take the metro to Schuman station and walk out, you'll face the Berlaymont, the star-shaped headquarters of the European Commission. It's not charming in the way the Grand Place is, but it tells you what the city is for now.

Anna: A slightly different kind of landmark.

Ben: A different kind, and every bit as much part of the story.

## A few things to know

Anna: Practical stuff. What's the vibe?

Ben: Brussels is a city of cafés and small museums, and the weather is changeable. Carry a light jacket. The center is quite walkable, with cobbled streets and hills, so wear good shoes.

Anna: And the food everybody talks about?

Ben: Belgian beer culture was recognised by UNESCO in 2016. Chocolate, waffles, and fries are everywhere. And the praline is traditionally credited to a Brussels chocolatier named Neuhaus, who has a shop in the Galeries.

Anna: Tourist traps?

Ben: The streets right around the Grand Place can be crowded and pricey. Walk two or three streets away and you'll usually find something better.

Anna: And if it rains?

Ben: Then you go indoors, and Brussels is good at that. The Galeries are covered, there are museums, and a lot of cafés that exist just to give you somewhere to wait the weather out.

Anna: What should I take away from all this?

Ben: That Brussels is a city that gets knocked down and rebuilt, and keeps its sense of humour. A small boy on a fountain in a different costume every week tells you most of it.

Anna: Right. Go have a good day.

Ben: And if you want the whole story, the long episode will be waiting for you tonight.
