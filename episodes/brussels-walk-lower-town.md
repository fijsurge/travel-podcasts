## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A walking tour of the old center, about a kilometer and a half in all, in five stops. Each chapter is one stop, so you can pause between them, and the audio carries on as you walk. Streets and landmarks are described loosely, so keep a map open as well. The stops are close together, and you can do the whole route in a morning. Opening hours, queues and any construction change often, so check them on the day.

## Welcome and how this works

Ben: And I'm Ben. This is a walking tour of the lower town of Brussels, the oldest part of the city, in five stops.

Anna: And the idea is that you're not sitting down.

Ben: You're out on the street, with headphones, or a speaker in your pocket. Each chapter is one stop. We tell you where you are, what you're looking at, and the story behind it. Then we point you toward the next one.

Anna: And if I want to stand there and stare?

Ben: Press pause. Stare as long as you like. The tour waits for you.

Anna: What do I need?

Ben: Comfortable shoes, because it's cobblestones. Something for the weather, because Brussels changes its mind. And a map on your phone, because we describe where things are, but we'd rather you not get lost.

Anna: And I'll make a bet with you, right now. Somebody on this walk will say "it's smaller than I thought."

Ben: Within five minutes. You'll win that one.

Anna: I love an easy bet.

Ben: One more thing. This walk is really about a river.

Anna: Okay, so where do we start?

Ben: With a small boy and a very large crowd. Our first stop is the Manneken Pis. From Brussels-Central station, it's roughly a ten-minute walk, and it's a few minutes from the Grand Place.

## Stop 1: The Manneken Pis

Anna: Okay. If there's a crowd, lots of phones, and something very small in the middle, you've found it.

Ben: He stands at the corner of the Rue de l'Étuve and the Rue du Chêne. A bronze figure of a little boy, about fifty-five centimeters tall, relieving himself into a stone basin.

Anna: Fifty-five centimeters. That's less than two feet.

Ben: And somebody around you just said "it's smaller than I thought."

Anna: I win.

Ben: You win. Now take a look at the setting. He's on a corner, on a wall, behind railings. This isn't a monument in a grand square. It's a street fountain that became a symbol.

Anna: How old is he?

Ben: The first mention of a fountain figure of a boy goes back to the thirteen-hundreds. The bronze statue was made in 1619 by the Brabant sculptor Jérôme Duquesnoy the Elder. And here's a detail that surprises people. The one in front of you is a replica.

Anna: A replica?

Ben: The original is kept in the Brussels City Museum, on the Grand Place. Which is where we're going next.

Anna: Why a replica?

Ben: Because he keeps getting stolen.

Anna: A statue that keeps getting kidnapped.

Ben: Greatest hits. A legend says English soldiers took him in the seventeen-forties, and he was found in a town called Geraardsbergen. The French grenadiers allegedly tried another theft in 1747.

Anna: Allegedly.

Ben: In 1817, a freed convict named Antoine Licas stole him and broke him into eleven pieces. He was repaired and recast. In 1963, students from Antwerp hijacked him for five days.

Anna: Five days. What did they do with him?

Ben: I'd rather not know. And in the nineteen-sixties he disappeared again, and divers recovered him from a canal in 1966.

Anna: A canal.

Ben: Which is why there's a replica on the street, and the original is somewhere safe and dry.

Anna: Okay, but why is a boy on a fountain doing that?

Ben: There are several legends, and nobody can prove any of them. The best known says that Duke Godfrey the Third of Leuven, a two-year-old, was hung in a basket from an oak tree to encourage his troops during a battle in 1142. And he urinated on the enemy.

Anna: A baby duke. Doing military service.

Ben: Another legend says a boy put out a burning fuse that was meant to blow up the city walls. Another says he put out a fire. Take your pick.

Anna: Which one do I tell my friends?

Ben: The baby duke. It's the funniest.

Anna: And then there's the wardrobe.

Ben: This is the part I love. He has about a thousand costumes. The thousandth was presented in 2018. They're changed regularly, for special occasions, for visiting delegations, for anniversaries. A little sign near the statue often tells you who he's dressed as today.

Anna: A boy with a thousand outfits.

Ben: More than I own.

Anna: More than I own, and I like clothes.

Ben: And they're all on display. Since 2017, there's a museum for them called GardeRobe MannekenPis, at number nineteen on the Rue du Chêne, just around the corner.

Anna: GardeRobe. I see what they did there.

Ben: Garde-robe is French for wardrobe. Belgians like their puns.

Anna: What does this tiny statue tell me about Brussels?

Ben: That the city has always had a sense of humor about itself. A capital of Europe whose most famous symbol is a boy doing something naughty. And everyone is fine with it. They dress him up and give him a birthday.

Anna: So pause here. Stay a minute. Watch the crowd.

Ben: They all do exactly the same thing. They look, they laugh, they take a photo, and they leave.

Anna: Okay. When you're ready, we head toward the Grand Place. It's only a few minutes away.

Ben: Follow your map, or the crowds. On the way, you'll pass the Rue Charles Buls. In it, there's a bronze monument to a man named Everard t'Serclaes.

Anna: The one we should touch.

Ben: Yes. I'll tell you why when we get there.

## Stop 2: The Grand Place

Anna: Okay, you've turned a corner and the street opens up. And now you're in it.

Ben: Stand somewhere near the middle and turn all the way around. This is the Grand Place. In Dutch, the Grote Markt. A paved square, about sixty-eight meters by a hundred and ten, and every side is covered in decoration.

Anna: I'm going to use the word "gold" a lot.

Ben: Please do. Gilded statues, gilded cornices, gilded ornaments on top of every building.

Anna: Why is it all so ornate?

Ben: Because of who built it. This was the marketplace of a rich trading city, and the houses around the square belonged to the guilds, the associations of craftsmen and merchants. The bakers, the brewers, the butchers, the tanners, the boatmen, the painters. They competed with each other to have the grandest house on the square.

Anna: A very medieval show-off contest.

Ben: Except it isn't medieval. Not most of it. Here's the thing that surprises people.

Anna: Go on.

Ben: In August of 1695, during the Nine Years' War, a French army of about seventy thousand men under Marshal Villeroi bombarded Brussels. The aim was to draw enemy forces away from a siege at Namur. They shelled the city for about two days. Most of the Grand Place was destroyed. Only the stone shell of the town hall and a few fragments of other buildings survived.

Anna: Wow. So what we're looking at...

Ben: Was rebuilt in about four years. The guilds put their houses back, under rules from the city council and the governor, in an exuberant baroque style. So these golden facades are about three hundred and thirty years old, not seven hundred.

Anna: And they look better than ever.

Ben: That's what Brussels does. It gets knocked down, and then it builds something even better, to spite whoever did it.

Anna: Let's talk about the town hall.

Ben: That's the Gothic building with the tall tower. The one old building left. It was built in stages, between 1401 and 1455, and the tower is about ninety-six meters tall.

Anna: And there's someone on top.

Ben: A golden Saint Michael, about two point seven meters tall, slaying a dragon. He's the patron saint of the city. What you see is a copy from the nineteen-nineties. The original is inside the City Museum.

Anna: Another copy.

Ben: Brussels loves a backup.

Anna: Now look at the tower carefully, I'm told.

Ben: Compare the two halves of the facade. The tower isn't in the center. It's off to one side, and the building isn't symmetrical. There's a famous story that the architect found out and threw himself off the tower in despair.

Anna: Oh no.

Ben: It's almost certainly a legend.

Anna: Thank goodness.

Ben: Let's turn around. Opposite the town hall is the building with the neo-Gothic facade, with lots of pointed arches. It's called the King's House, or the Maison du Roi. In Dutch, the Broodhuis, the Bread House.

Anna: Bread house? Not very royal.

Ben: It was named for the bread market that once stood on the site. It was rebuilt in neo-Gothic style between 1874 and 1896, and since 1887 it's housed the Brussels City Museum, where that little boy is kept in his original form.

Anna: So the real Manneken Pis lives in the Bread House.

Ben: In a museum with a very good view of his own fan club.

Anna: And the guild houses.

Ben: Have names. Each has a name, and often a symbol. Number one is Le Roy d'Espagne, the King of Spain, which was the house of the bakers. Number five is La Louve, the she-wolf, the archers' house. Number eight, L'Étoile, the star, is a small and rather modest one. And number nine is Le Cygne, the swan, which belonged to the butchers.

Anna: The butchers got the swan. Fair enough.

Ben: Now, a thing you might want to plan your trip around. The Ommegang. A costumed pageant held on the square in late June or early July, which re-enacts the Joyous Entry of Charles the Fifth and his son Philip in 1549. UNESCO recognized it in 2019.

Anna: And the flower carpet!

Ben: Every two years in August, around Assumption Day, for three or four days, they cover part of the square with a carpet of nearly a million flowers. Begonias or dahlias. About twenty-four meters by seventy-seven.

Anna: A carpet made of a million flowers.

Ben: Someone has to plant them.

Anna: And the Grand Place has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1998.

Ben: It has. And we promised the t'Serclaes monument.

Anna: Yes! The lucky bronze.

Ben: A short walk away. Remember, in 1356, the Count of Flanders invades and takes Brussels. A citizen named Everard t'Serclaes leads a night raid with a small band of men, slips into the city, and drives the Flemish garrison out.

Anna: A local hero.

Ben: The monument, on the Rue Charles Buls, shows him lying dead. Touch the bronze, the tradition says, and you'll have luck. You can see where millions of hands have polished it.

Anna: Millions of hands. Hope they washed.

Ben: I'd use hand sanitizer afterward. And then take the luck.

Anna: Pause here as long as you like. Look up, look around, go into a café on the edge of the square if you want.

Ben: When you're ready, we're heading north to a very different kind of place. A covered arcade.

Anna: Walk with the Grand Place behind you, toward the little streets with all the restaurants.

Ben: That's the Rue des Bouchers, the butchers' street. Now it's lined with restaurants with menus on the pavement. A word of advice. It's touristy. Look, and keep walking.

## Stop 3: The Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert

Anna: Okay, you've found it. You've stepped from a cobbled street into a glass tunnel.

Ben: Look up. That's a glass roof, held up by a delicate cast-iron frame, arching over a long shopping street. This is the Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert.

Anna: It's gorgeous.

Ben: About two hundred and thirteen meters long. It opened on the twentieth of June 1847, inaugurated by King Leopold the First and his two sons. The architect was Jean-Pierre Cluysenaar, who designed and built it between 1846 and 1847.

Anna: And it's often described as...

Ben: One of the first truly public covered shopping arcades in Europe. Paris had its famous covered passages, but this one was built as a public street, open to everyone. Its motto, in Latin, is Omnibus omnia.

Anna: Everything for everybody.

Ben: That's the idea. A very nineteenth-century dream. A clean, dry, well-lit street where anyone could stroll, shop, and be seen.

Anna: Dry. In Brussels. That's a luxury.

Ben: The rain isn't going to get you in here.

Anna: And it's three galleries.

Ben: Three sections. The Galerie du Roi, the King's Gallery. The Galerie de la Reine, the Queen's Gallery, which is the longer one. And a smaller side passage called the Galerie des Princes, the Princes' Gallery.

Anna: Who's in them now?

Ben: Chocolate shops, leather goods, a bookshop, a theater, and a cinema. The Royal Theater of the Galleries opened in the King's Gallery on the seventh of June 1847, just before the arcade itself. And a cinema, the Cinéma des Galeries, opened in the Queen's Gallery in 1939.

Anna: And chocolate. I'm counting on chocolate.

Ben: You'll find it. One of the best known names here is Neuhaus. Tradition credits a Neuhaus, a Brussels chocolatier, with inventing the praline, the filled chocolate, in the early twentieth century. The shop is right here in the gallery.

Anna: I'm sorry, the praline was invented here?

Ben: According to tradition. As with the Manneken Pis, I'd call it a good story with some truth in it. But the shop is real, and I think it's worth a visit.

Anna: I'll check the truth. Carefully. With samples.

Ben: A scientific method.

Anna: And the cafés?

Ben: The Taverne du Passage, in the Queen's Gallery, was called the Café des Arts until 1892, and it was a meeting place for writers and artists. It's still a restaurant, with a classic Belgian menu.

Anna: And film history.

Ben: On the first of March, 1896, the Lumière brothers' films were shown to the public in Brussels in these galleries, just a few months after their first public screening in Paris.

Anna: So this is where Brussels first saw the movies.

Ben: In a glass-roofed passage. I like that.

Anna: And the galleries are protected.

Ben: Listed as a historic monument in 1986, and they're on UNESCO's tentative list for World Heritage.

Anna: Give me one thing to look for.

Ben: Look up at the roof, then look down at the pavement. The architect designed the whole thing as one piece, from the cast-iron frame to the shop fronts. It's a rare place where a nineteenth-century dream is still working.

Anna: And I'm told there's a little girl around here.

Ben: Yes. Back on the Rue des Bouchers there's a narrow alley called the Impasse de la Fidélité, the dead end of Fidelity. At the end of it is a tiny bronze figure of a squatting girl, about fifty centimeters tall.

Anna: The Manneken Pis's sister!

Ben: Jeanneke Pis, installed in 1987. Behind a little grille, so she's often hard to see.

Anna: A bit of equality.

Ben: In a way.

Anna: Pause here. Have a coffee, buy some chocolate.

Ben: When you're ready, we're heading toward a very grand building and the story of a vanished river.

Anna: Leave the galleries and walk west, toward the wide boulevard.

Ben: You'll see the building long before you reach it. It looks like a small palace.

## Stop 4: The Bourse and the buried river

Anna: You've reached a big building with columns, lions, and lots of statues, on a square with a wide pedestrian street running past.

Ben: This is the Place de la Bourse, and the building is the Bourse, the old stock exchange. Built between 1868 and 1873, on the site of a former butter market. The architect was Léon-Pierre Suys.

Anna: It looks like a palace.

Ben: An eclectic style, a mix of neo-Renaissance and Second Empire. You can see the lions at the entrance, by the Jacquet brothers, and figures along the facade symbolizing Art, Agriculture, Industry, and Science. And inside, the caryatids were carved with the help of a young Auguste Rodin, who worked in Brussels at the time.

Anna: Rodin. Here.

Ben: A young sculptor, working on a commission. Not the Thinker yet.

Anna: Still working on his résumé.

Ben: But you asked about the river.

Anna: I did. I've been very patient.

Ben: You have. Turn and look down the long pedestrian street. That's Boulevard Anspach. And what I want you to imagine is that it's a river.

Anna: A river?

Ben: The Senne. The river that made Brussels. The name of the city means a home in the marsh, remember. For centuries, the Senne flowed through the middle of the city, and by the eighteen-hundreds it was a disaster. An open sewer, choked with refuse. It flooded regularly. And it carried cholera.

Anna: So they buried it.

Ben: The reforming mayor Jules Anspach decided to cover it. In 1865, he chose a design by Léon Suys, the same architect who built the Bourse, to vault the river and build grand boulevards on top. Between 1867 and 1871, the river was covered.

Anna: And the boulevard is named after him.

Ben: Yes. Wide, straight, and lined with imposing buildings, modeled on the boulevards of Paris, which Baron Haussmann was building at the same time. The Bourse was the centerpiece.

Anna: So we're standing on top of a river.

Ben: We are. It's still down there, in a tunnel, flowing under your feet. Most visitors never know.

Anna: We got there.

Ben: We did. Told you.

Anna: Was it a triumph?

Ben: A mixed one. The city got clean water, better health, and a grand new center. But hundreds of old houses and streets in the working-class neighborhoods along the river were torn down, and whole communities were pushed out.

Anna: That's a real cost.

Ben: And it's one reason so many people here care about preserving what's left.

Anna: What's the boulevard like now?

Ben: On the twenty-ninth of June 2015, the central boulevards were closed to traffic between the Place de la Bourse and the Place de Brouckère, creating a big pedestrian zone. It's been a place to walk, sit, and have a drink ever since. There was a lot of argument at first. Now most people seem to like it.

Anna: And inside the Bourse?

Ben: The upper floors have housed a museum called Belgian Beer World since September 2023, and the main hall was opened to the public for the first time then. If you like beer, it's worth knowing about. Belgian beer culture was recognized by UNESCO in 2016.

Anna: And I'd like to say something about the steps.

Ben: Yes. After the attacks of the twenty-second of March, 2016, the steps and the square in front of the Bourse became a place where people came to grieve. They wrote messages in chalk on the pavement and left candles and flowers. It became a kind of living memorial, and people still come here to remember.

Anna: It's good to stand quietly for a moment.

Ben: It is. This is a place where the city gathers, in good times and in bad.

Anna: Pause here. Look at the building, look down the boulevard, and think about the river underneath.

Ben: When you're ready, we have one more stop. A few minutes' walk to the west, and it's where the city began.

Anna: Head toward the Place Saint-Géry.

Ben: It's a short way from the Bourse, in the direction of the old center, among narrow streets and small bars.

## Stop 5: Place Saint-Géry, where Brussels began

Anna: This one isn't grand. It's a pleasant square, with cafés, and an old brick hall in the middle.

Ben: And it might be the most important stop on the tour. Because this is where Brussels started. Or at least, where the story says it did.

Anna: The island.

Ben: Remember the river. Before it was buried, the Senne divided into channels here, forming little islands in the marsh. And the tradition is that around the year five eighty, a holy man named Gaugericus, known in French as Saint Géry, the bishop of Cambrai, built a small chapel on an island in the river.

Anna: Right here?

Ben: Close to where you're standing. The square is named after him. Historians are cautious, because the sources are later and uncertain, but it's a good candidate for the seed of the city.

Anna: And the name of the city?

Ben: The earliest forms look something like Bruocsella. Most scholars read it as a home in the marsh, or a dwelling by the swamp.

Anna: A very honest name.

Ben: It's what a farmer would call a wet bit of ground. And you're standing on that wet ground. It's just been paved over and covered up.

Anna: So that's the story.

Ben: That's the story. Around the year nine seventy-nine, Charles, the exiled Carolingian duke, is said to have built a fortification on an island in the river, and moved the relics of Saint Gudula to the chapel. That's the date usually given for the founding of Brussels.

Anna: With a caution.

Ben: With a caution. Modern historians say the story rests on sources written long afterward, and that the city probably grew gradually, over several generations. But it's the story that stuck, and Brussels celebrated its thousandth birthday in 1979.

Anna: Happy birthday, to a date that might be wrong.

Ben: They had cake anyway.

Anna: And there's an old hall.

Ben: The brick building in the middle of the square is the old market hall, the Halles Saint-Géry, close to where the island once was. In the nineteenth century, it was a covered market. Now it's an exhibition and event space, with a café, and the square around it is full of bars.

Anna: A good place to sit.

Ben: A very good place. And a nice way to end. The city started in a swamp, on an island, with a chapel and a market. It grew into a capital, it rebuilt itself after a bombardment, it buried its river, and now people sit outside in the sun and drink beer on the spot where it began.

Anna: A full circle.

Ben: It is. Take a moment. Order something. Look at the old hall, and then look at the paving stones and imagine the water.

Anna: What's the theme?

Ben: That Brussels is a city of things that got broken and rebuilt, buried and rediscovered. And that it doesn't take itself too seriously.
