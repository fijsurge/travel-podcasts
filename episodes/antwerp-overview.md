## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

An overview for the evening before an Antwerp day, about twelve to fifteen minutes. It is written to be heard, with no tables. The facts come from the pages listed at the end; where a source was thin, the script says less rather than guessing. If you want to walk the old center with us tomorrow, play "Walking Antwerp's Old Town".

## Welcome

Anna: Evening. You're nearly in Antwerp. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. You've got a day ashore tomorrow, and we've got about fifteen minutes tonight.

Anna: Think of it as the briefing before the shore leave.

Ben: Five short chapters. What the city is. Why it was so rich. How it lost the money. Where Rubens fits in. And what to look out for tomorrow.

Anna: And I'm placing a bet before we start. By the end of this, somebody will have mentioned a hand.

Ben: A hand?

Anna: You'll see.

## Chapter 1: What Antwerp is

Anna: Okay. Quick, Ben. One sentence. What is Antwerp?

Ben: A river port in Flanders, on the Scheldt, that has been a big deal for about a thousand years.

Anna: That's two sentences.

Ben: It was one with a comma.

Anna: Fine. Where does the name come from?

Ben: Nobody is certain. But the city has a favorite story, and it involves your hand.

Anna: I knew it. I win already.

Ben: Not yet. Hold on. The legend says a giant called Druoon Antigoon lived by the river. Any ship that wanted to pass had to pay him a toll.

Anna: And if the captain said no?

Ben: The giant cut off his hand.

Anna: Wow. Rude.

Ben: Then a hero called Brabo arrives, beats the giant, cuts off his hand, and throws it into the river.

Anna: Oh no. And that's the name?

Ben: That's the story. The name is said to mean hand throwing. It's a legend, and the real origin is still debated. But you'll see the giant's hand around town tomorrow.

Anna: Where, exactly?

Ben: On top of the main square's fountain, for one. We'll get there.

Anna: And what's the city like today?

Ben: One of the major seaports in the world, and a big city that still feels walkable. The old center is compact, and it's all within a short stroll of the river.

Anna: Good. My shoes will be grateful.

Ben: Wear good ones. The old streets are cobbled.

## Chapter 2: Why it was rich

Anna: So why do people say Antwerp was once the richest city in Europe?

Ben: Let me soften that. Around 1560 it was the second-largest city in Europe north of the Alps. That's the claim the sources support.

Anna: So second place.

Ben: Second place in a very big field. And the reason comes down to geography and luck.

Anna: Luck?

Ben: Around 1500, the channel to the old trading city of Bruges silted up. Ships couldn't get in easily.

Anna: So the merchants moved?

Ben: They moved to Antwerp, where the river was deep and the harbor was open. Foreign trading houses set up here in the late 15th century.

Anna: Like a business park for the whole continent.

Ben: Roughly. And the money came in waves. Pepper from the Portuguese. Silver from the Spanish route to the Americas. Cloth from the north.

Anna: And what did the merchants do with all of it?

Ben: They traded it. And in 1531, Antwerp opened its stock exchange, the first building designed from scratch for the job.

Anna: A building just for trading.

Ben: Just for trading. Before that, merchants met in squares and inns. Antwerp gave it a roof.

Anna: Which means they invented the office.

Ben: That is not quite the word. But I'll allow it.

Anna: I'll take it. So rich city. Beautiful buildings, I assume?

Ben: Very. In 1565 the new town hall opened on the main square. A Renaissance building, designed by Cornelis Floris and Willem van den Broeck. Hold on to that one. You'll stand in front of it tomorrow.

## Chapter 3: How it lost the money

Anna: Okay. There's a "but" coming.

Ben: There is. And it's a heavy one. This part is not funny.

Anna: Understood.

Ben: In 1566 and 1567, religious riots and unrest in the Low Countries brought Spanish troops to the region. Antwerp was a rich trading city in a Catholic king's empire.

Anna: And then?

Ben: In November 1576, Spanish troops sacked the city. It's called the Spanish Fury. About 6,000 people were killed, and about 800 houses were burned. The new town hall was gutted by the fire.

Anna: That's brutal.

Ben: It is. And it wasn't the end. In 1585, after a long siege, Alessandro Farnese, the Duke of Parma, took the city for Spain. Many Protestants were forced to leave.

Anna: And they took their skills with them.

Ben: And their money. Amsterdam became the new trading center.

Anna: And then something worse?

Ben: In 1648, the Treaty of Münster closed the Scheldt to shipping. The river that had made Antwerp rich was shut at the mouth.

Anna: So a port with no ships.

Ben: By 1800 the population had fallen below 40,000. That was the low point.

Anna: How did it recover?

Ben: Slowly. Napoleon eased the restrictions and paid for new docks, starting with the Bonaparte Dock in 1811. The restriction on the river ended in 1863. And in 1859, the old 16th-century walls came down, to make room for broad avenues.

Anna: So the city grew back.

Ben: It did. A new stock exchange in 1872. A grand central station, finished in 1905.

## Chapter 4: Rubens and the art

Anna: Right, let's brighten up. Rubens.

Ben: Peter Paul Rubens. The great painter of the 17th century. Leader of the Antwerp school.

Anna: Wait. If the city was in decline, how did it have a star painter?

Ben: Good question. A city can lose its trade and still keep its talent. Rubens led the Antwerp school of painters, and the city's churches and families kept him busy.

Anna: A bit of a comeback story.

Ben: Of a kind. In 1610 he bought a house in the city, a year after he married Isabella Brant. He rebuilt it in the style of an Italian palace, with a studio, a courtyard, and a Baroque garden.

Anna: He built his own palazzo.

Ben: With a workshop attached. He worked there with students and assistants, and he lived there until he died in 1640.

Anna: And you can go in.

Ben: The city bought the house in 1937, and it opened as a museum in 1946. One warning. At the time of writing, the main artist's residence section is closed for renovation. So check before you go.

Anna: Noted. Where are his paintings?

Ben: Four are in the Cathedral of Our Lady. The Assumption of Mary over the high altar. The Raising of the Cross and the Descent from the Cross on either side. And a Resurrection of Christ in a side chapel.

Anna: Four in one church. That's a good start.

Ben: And his tomb is in a different church, Saint James's.

Anna: And there was also a printer?

Ben: Yes. Christophe Plantin. He moved from France around 1550 and built a printing house. His son-in-law, Jan Moretus, carried it on. The building is now a museum with the two oldest surviving printing presses in the world.

Anna: Oldest in the world. Good fact for the pocket.

Ben: It's a World Heritage Site, since 2005. And that Resurrection by Rubens? It was painted as a memorial for Jan Moretus. So the printer and the painter connect.

Anna: Good. I like a link.

## Chapter 5: What to look for tomorrow

Anna: Okay. The shopping list. What should I look out for?

Ben: Start at the river. There's a small, old fortress called Het Steen. The gateway dates from between 1200 and 1225, and it's Antwerp's oldest building, by some counts.

Anna: Is it a castle?

Ben: What's left of one. It was once a prison, from 1303 to 1827. It's now a visitor center.

Anna: A prison for over 500 years. Ouch.

Ben: Then the Grote Markt, the main square. The town hall. The guild houses. And in the middle, a fountain from 1887 by Jef Lambeaux, with Brabo on top, holding up the giant's hand.

Anna: There it is. The hand. I win.

Ben: You win. Three women stand around the base, holding a boat and a fort, and the giant lies headless below.

Anna: Cheerful.

Ben: Then the cathedral. The north tower is 123 meters tall and was finished in 1518. The wooden lantern on the other tower was meant to be temporary. It's still there.

Anna: A temporary roof that lasted nearly five centuries.

Ben: Close enough. Then the lanes toward the Friday Market, the Plantin-Moretus Museum, and the Rubens House.

Anna: Practical tips?

Ben: Comfortable shoes. A rain plan. If it pours, the cathedral, the printing museum and the Rubens House are all indoors.

Anna: And somewhere to sit with a drink.

Ben: There's a champagne bar on the Meir, a short walk from the Rubens House. Check its opening hours before you count on it.

Anna: Excellent. That's my afternoon.

## Wrap-up

Anna: Five things to take with you. Give me the short version.

Ben: Antwerp is a river port with a legend about a hand. It got rich because Bruges silted up and the merchants came. It lost much of it to a sack, a siege, and a closed river. It came back with docks and railways. And it has Rubens.

Anna: And the thing to remember?

Ben: That the river made it, broke it, and made it again.

Anna: Sleep well. Big walk tomorrow.

Ben: Safe travels.
