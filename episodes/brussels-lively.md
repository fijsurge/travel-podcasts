## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

This is a pilot. It covers the same ground as the Welcome and Chapter 1 of "The Story of Brussels", written in a looser, more conversational style, with shorter turns, reactions, and some friendly disagreement. If you prefer it, the other episodes will be redone this way.

## Welcome

Anna: Okay. Brussels. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. And I'll admit, I've been looking forward to this one.

Anna: Why? It's the city with the waffles and the... what is it, the European Union?

Ben: Ha. Yes. The waffles and the European Union. Which is a funny pairing, when you think about it.

Anna: Is it?

Ben: Well, one city has a Gothic town hall, a glass tower full of EU officials, a medieval square that was rebuilt in four years after a bombardment, and a whole boulevard built right on top of a river.

Anna: Wait. A river?

Ben: A river. We'll get there.

Anna: And I've heard it's a city where every street sign is in two languages.

Ben: Two languages on the signs, and nearly four in ten residents hold a foreign passport.

Anna: Four in ten. That's a lot.

Ben: It is. So here's what I want to know. How does a small town on a muddy river end up as the working capital of Europe?

Anna: Trade, I'm guessing.

Ben: Trade. Guilds. Dukes. Emperors. Revolutionaries.

Anna: Revolutionaries.

Ben: And a great deal of stubbornness.

Anna: Right, well, I'm sold. Where do we start?

Ben: With the mud, naturally.

## Chapter 1: A Settlement in the Marsh

Anna: So, mud. Walk me through it.

Ben: The river is called the Senne. And you have to imagine it nothing like a proper river. It's a small, slow stream that used to wander across a wide, wet valley, splitting into channels, making little islands as it went.

Anna: Not exactly a glamorous start.

Ben: Not at all. But people had been living around the edges of that valley for a very long time. There are traces of Iron Age settlers at a site called Champ Saint-Anne, in the neighborhood of Anderlecht.

Anna: And I'm going to guess the Romans showed up, because the Romans always show up.

Ben: They did, sort of. Roman farms and country houses were scattered across the countryside. But no real Roman town on the spot.

Anna: Huh. I'd have bet money on a Roman town.

Ben: You'd have lost. After the empire faded, Frankish settlers moved in. And in that same field in Anderlecht, they've found a Frankish cemetery with more than three hundred graves, from roughly the years five hundred to seven hundred.

Anna: Three hundred graves. So that's not a passing visit.

Ben: No. That's farmers and fighters living in small villages on the dry ground above the river, inside the new Frankish kingdom, the one that eventually grows into Charlemagne's empire.

Anna: Okay. I can feel a legend coming.

Ben: You're right. There's a legend.

Anna: Go on.

Ben: Tradition says that around the year five eighty, a holy man named Gaugericus, who's known in French as Saint Géry, the bishop of Cambrai, built a little chapel on an island in the Senne.

Anna: Is that true?

Ben: Honestly? Historians are cautious. The sources are later and a bit shaky. But it's a good candidate for the seed of the city. And here's the nice part. You can still go there. It's called Place Saint-Géry, in the old center, and there's an old market hall close to where the island used to be.

Anna: So what about the name? Brussels.

Ben: Earliest forms look something like Bruocsella. And most scholars read that as a home in the marsh. Or a dwelling by the swamp.

Anna: Oh, that's honest.

Ben: It's what a farmer would call a wet bit of ground. And it tells you something true. This was a place that had to deal with water.

Anna: But hang on. If it's a swamp, why would anyone build a town there?

Ben: Good question. Geography. The Senne valley sat on an important route. Trade roads ran from the cloth-making cities of Flanders in the west, to Cologne and the Rhine in the east. Merchants went through carrying wool, cloth, wine, metalwork.

Anna: And they needed somewhere to stop.

Ben: Exactly. A place to cross the river, or bring boats upstream. And the islands gave them safe ground.

Anna: And the hills?

Ben: The hills to the east were a good place for a fortress.

Anna: Okay, so it's a market town waiting to happen.

Ben: Waiting to happen, yes. But for a long time it's still small, in a much bigger political world. In the ninth century, Charlemagne's empire is split up among his heirs.

Anna: As happens.

Ben: As always happens. And the region that becomes Belgium gets caught in the middle, in a long contested territory called Lotharingia, named for the Frankish king Lothar. It bounces back and forth between the western and eastern halves of the old empire.

Anna: Who's actually in charge, though? On the ground.

Ben: Not the dukes, really. They ruled Lotharingia on behalf of the German emperors, but they kept a loose grip. So the local lords hold most of the real power.

Anna: Right. So when do we get a proper date?

Ben: Late tenth century. In the year nine seventy-seven, the German emperor Otto the Second gives the duchy of Lower Lotharingia, which is roughly Belgium and part of the Netherlands, to a man called Charles.

Anna: Who's Charles?

Ben: A member of the old Carolingian family. A son of one French king and a brother of the next one. Basically an exile from the throne of France, hunting for a power base.

Anna: Oh, so he needs a castle.

Ben: He needs a castle. And within a couple of years, he builds a fortification on one of the islands in the Senne. The same island as the old chapel.

Anna: Is that the founding?

Ben: Hold on, because something else matters even more. In about the year nine seventy-nine, Charles moves the relics of a saint named Gudula, a pious woman from the seventh century, to the chapel on the island.

Anna: Gudula!

Ben: You know her.

Anna: I love Gudula. Is it true that she went to church before dawn carrying a lantern?

Ben: It is. And the devil kept blowing out her light.

Anna: Every time.

Ben: Every time. And every time, an angel lit it again.

Anna: I like the angel.

Ben: Everyone likes the angel. Anyway, Gudula becomes the patron saint of Brussels. And that move of her relics is the date most often given as the official founding of the city.

Anna: But I sense a caution.

Ben: Always. Modern historians point out that the story of Charles building a castle and founding the city rests on sources written long afterwards. The real picture is probably messier. A growing settlement, a chapel, a market, local lords, all developing over several generations.

Anna: So it's not like somebody cut a ribbon.

Ben: Nobody cut a ribbon. But nine seventy-nine is the date that stuck. Brussels celebrated its thousandth birthday in 1979.

Anna: And the relics moved again, didn't they?

Ben: They did. In 1047, a powerful nobleman named Lambert the Second, Count of Leuven, which is a city about twenty-five kilometers to the east, moves them to a new church on a hill overlooking the valley. And he sets up a college of priests there, called canons, to look after it.

Anna: Wait, so that's the cathedral?

Ben: That's the ancestor of the cathedral. The hill church was dedicated to Saint Michael, and in time to Saint Gudula too. And it grows into the twin-towered cathedral you can still see today.

Anna: And Saint Michael is the dragon slayer.

Ben: He is. He becomes the other patron saint of the city. And he ends up standing on top of the town hall, which we'll get to.

Anna: Okay, so by now the pieces are in place.

Ben: They are. Lowland market settlement on the river, with merchants and craftsmen. A church on the hill, with money and influence. A castle for protection. And the counts of Leuven, who increasingly see this place as an important part of their growing territory.

Anna: What about walls?

Ben: Around the year eleven hundred, the people build their first defensive wall. Nothing grand, a modest circuit around the main settlement. Inside, maybe a few thousand people. They make cloth, bake bread, brew beer, trade.

Anna: A few thousand. So not a great city. Not yet.

Ben: Not yet. But a real town now. Walls, a market, a church, a lord.

Anna: And it's about to take off.

Ben: It is. Next time, the counts become dukes, and the craftsmen start flexing their muscles.

Anna: And that muddy market square...

Ben: Begins turning into one of the most famous squares in the world.
