Anna: And De Wever is a Flemish nationalist.

Ben: Who had long dreamed of a more independent Flanders. The first Flemish nationalist to lead the Belgian government.

Anna: Running the country he'd like to split.

Ben: The kind of irony Belgian history delivers on a regular basis.

Anna: And the king?

Ben: Philippe, who succeeded his father Albert the Second in 2013. The seventh king of the Belgians.

Anna: Okay. Governments come and go. Who actually lives here?

Ben: On the first of January, 2025, the region counted about one million, two hundred fifty-six thousand people. A bit over thirty-seven percent of them held a foreign passport.

Anna: And that doesn't count the Belgians with a foreign background.

Ben: No. Residents come from about a hundred and eighty-three different nationalities.

Anna: A hundred and eighty-three. There are only a hundred and ninety-some countries.

Ben: So almost the whole world is represented in one very small region.

Anna: Who are the biggest groups?

Ben: The largest foreign group is French. Then Romanians and Italians, then Spaniards and Moroccans. Brussels also has an important Congolese community, centered on a district called Matongé.

Anna: And I read something surprising about the population.

Ben: In 2025, for the first time since the mid-nineteen-nineties, it stopped growing.

Anna: And a lot of those foreign residents work for the institutions.

Ben: The European Commission, the Council of the European Union, and parts of the European Parliament all have their seats here, around the Schuman roundabout and the Rue de la Loi. NATO's headquarters, a striking new complex in the north of the city, opened in 2018.

Anna: How many people work for international institutions?

Ben: By the most recent count, more than fifty thousand in the region. Around them are embassies, trade associations, law firms, think tanks, and the lobbyists who try to influence the laws of the European Union.

Anna: So a city that once lived by cloth and tapestries.

Ben: Now lives largely by words and rules.

Anna: Which is still a kind of weaving, if you think about it.

Ben: With a lot more meetings.

Anna: It hasn't all been easy, though. The city has had dark days.

Ben: It has. In November of 2015, after the terror attacks in Paris, some of whose planners had lived in the Brussels district of Molenbeek, the city went into a security lockdown that lasted days. Soldiers on the streets, the metro closed.

Anna: And then, in March.

Ben: On the eighteenth of March, 2016, police captured one of the key suspects, Salah Abdeslam, in Molenbeek. Four days later, on the morning of the twenty-second, three suicide bombers struck.

Anna: Where?

Ben: Two bombs exploded in the departure hall of Brussels Airport at Zaventem. An hour later, a third went off on a metro train leaving Maalbeek station, in the heart of the European Quarter, among the offices of the institutions.

Anna: How many people were killed?

Ben: Thirty-two people, not counting the attackers. More than three hundred were injured. The Islamic State group claimed responsibility.

Anna: And the city remembers.

Ben: Every year since, the city has marked the anniversary. And the little statue of the Manneken Pis has been dressed in a firefighter's uniform to honor the rescuers.

Anna: And yet life goes on, and the city keeps reinventing itself.

Ben: It does. In 2015, the central boulevards around the Bourse were closed to cars, creating one of the largest pedestrian zones in Europe. The old Boulevard Anspach, built over the buried Senne, became a place to walk and sit.

Anna: The river we buried.

Ben: Now with a sidewalk café on top.

Anna: And the rest of the city?

Ben: A low-emission zone limits older cars. And a traffic plan called Good Move, which reduces through traffic in residential neighborhoods, has been bitterly debated.

Anna: Like most such efforts.

Ben: Brussels is trying to become a city for people rather than just for commuters. And, like most such efforts, it's argued over fiercely.

Anna: Let's turn to the fun. Because the culture is as eclectic as the population.

Ben: Brussels is the capital of the comic strip. There are dozens of giant murals of comic heroes painted on building walls across the city, a museum devoted to the art form, and a tradition that gave the world Tintin and the Smurfs.

Anna: Okay, I would like to do a comic-strip wall walk right now.

Ben: It exists. You can.

Anna: And beer and chocolate.

Ben: In 2016, UNESCO recognized Belgian beer culture as part of the world's cultural heritage. And according to tradition, the praline, the filled chocolate, was invented in 1912 by a Brussels chocolatier named Jean Neuhaus, in the elegant glass-roofed Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert. One of the oldest covered shopping arcades in Europe.

Anna: Waffles. Fries. Mussels.

Ben: And a small green vegetable that carries the city's name.

Anna: The Brussels sprout.

Ben: Which was grown in the area around the city centuries ago.

Anna: The vegetable everyone loves to hate, named after the city that loves everything.

Ben: I'd say that's unfair to the sprout.

Anna: Fair. Now, the city is also reckoning with its past.

Ben: Yes. The Royal Museum for Central Africa, in Tervuren, which Leopold the Second built as a showcase of his colony, was closed for years. It reopened in 2018 after a major renovation that tried to confront its colonial origins.

Anna: And the statues.

Ben: In 2020, during worldwide protests against racism, statues of Leopold the Second in Belgium were defaced, and some were removed.

Anna: And there was a royal apology.

Ben: A parliamentary commission examined Belgium's colonial past. In 2022, King Philippe visited the Democratic Republic of the Congo and expressed deep regret for the suffering caused by colonial rule. The debate is ongoing, and it will shape how the city understands itself in the years ahead.

Anna: Okay. If you had to pick one symbol for Brussels, what would it be?

Ben: Not a lion, or a saint, or a king.

Anna: A dog.

Ben: A dog. In the local dialect, a zinneke is a mongrel. A mixed-breed street dog that belongs to no one breed.

Anna: And Brusselers say it with affection.

Ben: For their city. A place of mixed languages, mixed origins, and mixed styles, where no one is quite purebred. There's a small bronze statue of a dog, relieving itself against a post, on a street in the old center. And a colorful parade, held every two years, that celebrates the zinneke spirit.

Anna: A dog that is also, let's be honest, a bit rude.

Ben: A very Brussels dog.

Anna: All right. Let's step back and look at the whole journey.

Ben: It began as a settlement in a marsh, with a chapel on an island. It became a market town on the road between Flanders and Cologne, and a ducal capital. Its guilds won a seat at the table.

Anna: It became the capital of the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands, the home of great painters and weavers.

Ben: It was nearly erased in 1695, and rebuilt in four years into one of the most beautiful squares in the world.

Anna: It passed from Spain to Austria to France to the Dutch.

Ben: And in 1830, after a night at the opera and four days of street fighting, it became the capital of a new nation.

Anna: It grew rich under Leopold, on the profits of an empire built on terrible cruelty.

Ben: It was occupied twice, and spared the worst of two world wars. And then, almost by accident, it became the working capital of a united Europe.

Anna: Through all of it, Brussels has been a place where different peoples and languages meet, argue, trade, and build together.

Ben: It has never had one identity.

Anna: And perhaps that is its identity.

Ben: Which brings us to the final stubbornness tally.

Anna: Final count. Eight.

Ben: Eight. For a city that survived fire, bombardment, two occupations, and a government that took twenty months to form. And somehow still serves excellent fries.

Anna: That is the story of Brussels. Thank you for listening.

Ben: And enjoy the city.
