## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A short walk with three stops, designed for Thursday, December 3. It comes in two pieces. The first two stops fit a lunch walk to Bongiorno's on Wabash Avenue, about six minutes from the hotel. The third stop is the evening nightcap after dinner at Steak 48. Pause the audio between pieces, because the work day is in the middle. Stop positions are approximate. Opening hours and the exact look of the street change, so keep a map open. The tavern on Division Street is described in the wrap-up as the alternative nightcap, and it needs a short cab ride.

## Welcome

Anna: Hello, and welcome to a short walk. I'm Anna.

Ben: And I'm Ben. This is Chicago on a Thursday in early December, in three stops.

Anna: And it's a work day, so it's a walk in pieces.

Ben: Right. First piece is the lunch hour. Stops one and two, on the way to a pizza lunch. Second piece is later, after dinner, when you wander to a nightcap.

Anna: So pause button, essentially.

Ben: The pause button is the star of every one of these walks.

Anna: Okay, I'll make a bet. By the end of this walk, somebody will say "that's just a bridge."

Ben: And I'll tell you why it isn't.

Anna: Challenge accepted.

## Stop 1: The gold tower

Anna: Okay. Where are we?

Ben: You're on North Michigan Avenue, outside the building you're staying in. Cross the street if you can, and look up. This is the Carbide and Carbon Building, finished in 1929.

Anna: Wait. I just slept in a landmark?

Ben: You'll sleep in one tonight. It was built for a company called Union Carbide and Carbon, and the architects were the Burnham Brothers, the sons of the famous planner Daniel Burnham.

Anna: Famous planner. You always say that like I'm supposed to know him.

Ben: You will by the end of the walk. Now look at the colors. The base is polished black granite, with black marble and bronze trim. The tall middle part is dark green and gold terra cotta. And the top is trimmed in gold leaf.

Anna: Real gold?

Ben: Twenty-four karat. The building's own history says the architects looked at imitation gold and rejected it.

Anna: A gold hat on a green skyscraper. Bold.

Ben: Here's the story everyone tells. That it was designed to look like a dark green champagne bottle with gold foil on the top.

Anna: Oh, I love that.

Ben: It's a legend. A popular one, going back to the 1920s, but the designers never said so. The documented story is better. The leaf patterns refer to how carbon forms from ancient plants that decayed. And the letter C appears again and again, because of the company name.

Anna: So it's a building about coal. In gold.

Ben: Carbon, yes. Roughly. The company's ground floor was a showroom for its products.

Anna: A showroom. For skyscraper-sized carbon. Okay.

Ben: Look at the top again. Since 2007 the gold crown has been lit every night, which helped the champagne story along. The building is five hundred and three feet tall, thirty-seven floors, and Chicago made it a landmark in 1996.

Anna: And then it became a hotel.

Ben: Eventually. A hotel opened in it in 2004. It closed and reopened more than once. Then in 2020 a new owner bought it, and the Pendry opened in 2021.

Anna: I notice you said "more than once," in a very careful voice.

Ben: It's a landmark with a complicated career. The lobby has Belgian marble and frosted glass light fixtures, if you want a look on the way out.

Anna: Let's go. Which way?

Ben: Walk north on Michigan Avenue, toward the river. It's about a three-minute walk, and the bridge is right in front of you.

Anna: Teaser?

Ben: We'll get there.

Anna: I knew you'd say that.

## Stop 2: The DuSable Bridge

Anna: Okay, I'm on a bridge. Here's my bet. This is just a bridge.

Ben: Let me make you lose. You're standing on the DuSable Bridge, the Michigan Avenue bridge. It opened in 1920, after construction began in April 1918.

Anna: A bridge that took two years.

Ben: And a plan that took much longer. A grand boulevard linking the south and north parks was proposed as early as 1891, and Daniel Burnham's 1909 plan for Chicago laid it out.

Anna: There he is.

Ben: There he is. Now look at the bridge itself. It's a bascule bridge, which means the road lifts up like a drawbridge. And it has two decks.

Anna: Wait, two? I'm only on one.

Ben: The upper deck was for faster, non-commercial traffic. The lower deck was for slower commercial traffic serving the docks and industries beside the river. It's believed to be the first double-deck bridge with a roadway on both levels.

Anna: So there's a lower road under me.

Ben: Yes. Now look at the four stone houses at the corners. Those are the bridgehouses, and in 1928 they were given sculptures. The north ones show Joliet, Marquette, La Salle and Tonti, early explorers, and John Kinzie, an early settler. And the person who paid for that work was William Wrigley Junior.

Anna: Not the gum Wrigley.

Ben: The gum man. Wrigley commissioned the sculptor James Earle Fraser. The south ones were paid for by a monument fund. One of them shows the 1812 battle at Fort Dearborn, and the other, called Regeneration, shows Chicago rebuilding after the 1871 fire.

Anna: Fort Dearborn. I know that name.

Ben: The fort was built in 1803, and the south half of this bridge passes over its site. Look at the sidewalk on the south side. There are brass markers that outline the old blockhouse.

Anna: That's hidden in the pavement?

Ben: Hidden in plain sight, yes. And on the north side, near the Pioneer Court plaza, a plaque marks the homesite of Jean Baptiste Point du Sable.

Anna: The name of the bridge.

Ben: In October 2010 the bridge was renamed for him. He was a fur trader of African descent who married into the Potawatomi people, and he built a homestead and trading post near the river's mouth in the 1780s. He's often called Chicago's first permanent resident.

Anna: So the bridge you were going to call just a bridge...

Ben: Has a boulevard plan, a double deck, a fort, and a founder.

Anna: Fine. You win. It's not just a bridge. [laugh]

Ben: One more. In the southwest bridgehouse there's a small museum about the river and the bridge. It's called the McCormick Bridgehouse and Chicago River Museum, and it opened in 2006. In spring and fall, when the bridge is lifted for boats, you can see the gears work. In December, check the hours first.

Anna: So here's where the lunch walk ends.

Ben: For lunch, cross the bridge, turn left on Kinzie Street, and right onto Wabash Avenue. The pizza place is inside the River Plaza building. Pause the walk, and the next piece is the evening.

## Stop 3: The Irish bar on Clark

Anna: Okay, it's evening, and I've eaten a steak. Where are we?

Ben: You walked about six minutes northwest from Steak 48, along Ontario Street and Clark Street, to the corner of Clark and Erie. This is the Kerryman, an Irish bar.

Anna: Authentic Irish saloon, said the itinerary.

Ben: Careful there. The owners, two brothers from County Kerry, describe it more as a modern Irish cocktail lounge than an old pub. It has a main bar, a mezzanine, and a second room that gets DJs on Friday and Saturday nights.

Anna: So not a cozy fireside.

Ben: Not exactly. But the building has a past. In the 1920s this was a bar called McGovern's, and it was reportedly a hangout for Chicago's Prohibition-era gangsters. In the late 1970s it reopened as a punk bar, and one source calls it the original Chicago punk bar.

Anna: A punk bar, in a place with a steak house on the corner.

Ben: River North was different then. The name Kerryman, by the way, is said to come from Saint Brendan the Navigator, the Kerry saint who may have sailed the Atlantic.

Anna: Said to?

Ben: Even the source says it may be legend. Same with Brendan's voyage. It's a medieval story, not documented history.

Anna: I'm good with that. A saint, a punk bar, and a gangster rumor. Tell me about the other option.

Ben: Well, there is one. If you'd rather see a Chicago Christmas tavern, you can take a cab about six minutes north to Butch McGuire's, at 20 West Division Street, in the Gold Coast.

Anna: And that's holiday decorated?

Ben: Very. Robert "Butch" McGuire opened it in 1961. He borrowed five hundred and sixty dollars from his mother to buy a former speakeasy.

Anna: Five hundred and sixty dollars. For a bar.

Ben: It was a different century. He became known as the unofficial Mayor of Division Street. He wanted a place where unescorted women were welcome, which was unusual then, and the bar claims it has led to more than six thousand five hundred marriages.

Anna: Claims. You said claims.

Ben: Because it's the bar's own count, not an independent one. The Christmas decorations are the real draw, with about forty thousand lights. They go up in October and stay until mid-January, so in December you'll see them at their best.

Anna: So Kerryman is the stroll, and Butch is the cab.

Ben: Exactly. Either is fine.

## Wrap-up

Anna: So that's the walk. A gold tower, a double-deck bridge, and a nightcap.

Ben: And a legend, a plaque, and a mayor of a street.

Anna: And I lost my bet.

Ben: You did. It's the bet you always lose.

Anna: I like losing to a bridge, though. [chuckle]

Ben: Tomorrow is Friday. Another work day, and another walk in pieces, this time through the Loop.

Anna: And under the trains.

Ben: Under the trains.
