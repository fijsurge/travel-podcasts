## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A walking tour of the old center of Antwerp, about two kilometers in all, in six stops. It is planned for a river cruise day: you start at the river dock and end near the Rubens House, with an easy walk back to the ship. Each chapter is one stop, so you can pause between them, and the audio carries on as you walk. Streets and landmarks are described loosely, and the stop positions on the map are approximate, good to about 50 meters at best, so keep a map open as well. If a guided tour fills part of your morning, you can do this walk in the afternoon, or do only the stops you have time for. If it rains, the cathedral, the Plantin-Moretus Museum and the Rubens House are all indoors. At the time of writing, the main artist's residence section of the Rubens House is closed for renovation, so check what is open before you go. Opening hours, queues and any construction change often, so check them on the day.

Anna: Starting at the river, where your ship is.

Ben: And ending near the home of the most famous painter this city ever had.

Anna: And you're not sitting down.

Ben: You're out on the street, with headphones. Each chapter is one stop. We tell you where you are, what you're looking at, and the story behind it. Then we point you toward the next one.

Anna: And if I want to stand there and stare?

Ben: Press pause. Stare as long as you like. The tour waits for you.

Anna: What do I need?

Ben: Comfortable shoes, because it's cobblestones. Something for the rain, because you might need it. And a map on your phone, because we describe where things are, but we'd rather you not get lost.

Anna: And what if the weather is bad?

Ben: Three of the stops are indoors. The cathedral, the printing museum, and the Rubens House. We'll flag them as we go.

Anna: Right. And I'll make a bet with you, right now. Before this walk is over, we will see more than one giant hand.

Ben: Is that a bet?

Anna: It's a bet. I'm keeping a hand tally.

Ben: Fine. Then let's find the first one. Stand by the river, near the little stone fortress, and press play when you're ready.

## Stop 1: Het Steen and the Scheldt riverfront

Anna: Okay. Where are we?

Ben: On the riverbank, beside the Scheldt. The wide brown river in front of you is the reason this city exists.

Anna: It's not very blue.

Ben: It's a working river. Blue is not its job.

Anna: And that small stone building behind us?

Ben: That's Het Steen. The name means, roughly, the Stone. Look at the gateway, the arched part by the bridge. It was built between 1200 and 1225.

Anna: So it's older than almost everything we'll see today.

Ben: Older than the town hall, older than the cathedral's tower, older than the printing houses. People say it's the oldest building in Antwerp. We'll say by some counts.

Anna: Why the hedge?

Ben: Because the whole site is older than the gateway. There was a settlement and a fortification here by the 9th century. The gateway is what survives of a bigger castle of the Dukes of Brabant. The rest was torn down in the 19th century.

Anna: So it's the one piece left.

Ben: The front door. Then around 1520 it was rebuilt under Emperor Charles V, and that's when the name Het Steen took hold.

Anna: And what did they use it for?

Ben: Many things. A place for the city's aldermen and a court of justice. And from 1303 to 1827, a prison.

Anna: That's over 500 years of prison.

Ben: Yes. Then an archaeology museum from 1890, and a maritime museum annex from 1952. After a big renovation that finished in October 2021, it reopened as a visitor center, with a modern concrete extension.

Anna: Can we go in?

Ben: It's worth checking. It works as a gateway for visitors now. But for this walk, the outside is enough.

Anna: Now look at the bridge. There's a statue.

Ben: A giant. That's Lange Wapper.

Anna: Is that the giant from the legend?

Ben: Not quite. Different giant. Lange Wapper is a figure from local folklore who is said to have terrorized the medieval townspeople. He's not the one with the toll.

Anna: There's more than one giant?

Ben: It's a big city. Now, the giant with the toll. This is where the city's name story starts.

Anna: The hand.

Ben: The hand. The legend says a giant called Druoon Antigoon lived by the Scheldt, right about here. Any ship that wanted to pass had to pay a toll.

Anna: And if you didn't pay?

Ben: He cut off your hand.

Anna: Charming man.

Ben: Then a hero named Brabo came along. He fought the giant, cut off his hand, and threw it into the river.

Anna: That's one hand, in the river. My tally says one.

Ben: Hold the tally. The story says that's how the city got its name. Hand throwing. Whether that's true, nobody knows. But the city likes the story so much that it keeps telling it.

Anna: A city founded on a throw.

Ben: A good throw.

Anna: And the river itself. How big a deal was it?

Ben: Enormous. About 1500, the channel to the old trading city of Bruges silted up. Foreign merchants moved their trading houses here, because Antwerp's river was deep.

Anna: So this spot was the front door of a trade empire.

Ben: For a while. By 1560, Antwerp was the second-largest city in Europe north of the Alps.

Anna: And then?

Ben: And then came the Spanish Fury in 1576, a siege in 1585, and in 1648 the river itself was closed to shipping by a treaty. We'll hear about the Fury in a minute, at the town hall.

Anna: What happened to the river afterward?

Ben: Napoleon eased the restrictions and built docks. The last restriction ended in 1863. Today Antwerp is one of the major seaports in the world, which is a nice kind of revenge.

Anna: So the river made it, broke it, and made it again.

Ben: That's the whole city in one sentence.

Anna: Okay. Time to pause. Look at the river. Look at the gateway. Find the giant by the bridge.

Ben: When you're ready, here is the way on. Turn your back to the water and look for the street that runs straight inland from the square, the Suikerrui. It leads toward the church towers. A few minutes' walk, a little uphill, and it opens into the main square.

Anna: Is that the one with the fountain?

Ben: It is. And the fountain has Brabo on top, holding up something.

Anna: Oh no. Is it the hand?

Ben: We'll get there.

## Stop 2: The Grote Markt, with the town hall and the Brabo fountain

Anna: And we've arrived. Wow. That's a lot of gold.

Ben: That's the Grote Markt, the Great Market. The main square of the old city.

Anna: It's a bit of a stage set.

Ben: In a good way. Turn around slowly. On one side, the town hall. On the other sides, tall narrow houses, with gilded statues on the roofs. Those are guild houses.

Anna: Guild houses. So, clubs for craftsmen.

Ben: Associations for trades. Each one wanted a grand house on the best square. It was a way of showing off.

Anna: So it's the original who-has-the-nicest-front-door contest.

Ben: Basically.

Anna: Now the big building. The town hall. When was that built?

Ben: It was inaugurated in 1565. It's a Renaissance building, designed by Cornelis Floris and Willem van den Broeck.

Anna: And it's still standing.

Ben: It is, but not without trouble. And this is the part where we stop joking.

Anna: Understood.

Ben: In November 1576, Spanish troops sacked Antwerp. It's known as the Spanish Fury. About 6,000 people were killed and about 800 houses burned.

Anna: And the town hall?

Ben: The courtyard of the town hall had been used as an arsenal, and the fire in the sack gutted the building. It was rebuilt in its original style in the years that followed.

Anna: That's hard to imagine, standing here.

Ben: Most of what you see around you was either built before that, rebuilt after it, or both. The city lost something that day it never fully got back.

Anna: And then it got worse.

Ben: A few years later, in 1585, a long siege ended with Alessandro Farnese, the Duke of Parma, taking the city for Spain. Protestants were forced to leave. Many of the skilled people and their money moved north, to Amsterdam.

Anna: And that's the end of the golden age.

Ben: It's the beginning of the end. Then in 1648 the river was closed. By 1800, fewer than 40,000 people lived here.

Anna: So the grand square we're standing in was built by a city at the top of its power.

Ben: And has outlived it. Now. Let's lighten things up a little. Turn to the fountain in the middle.

Anna: Okay. That's dramatic. A man on top, throwing something.

Ben: That's Brabo. The fountain was made by the sculptor Jef Lambeaux in 1887. And Lambeaux lived in a house right on this square.

Anna: So the artist could look out the window at his own fountain.

Ben: I'd have done the same. Now look at the figures.

Anna: A man at the top. And a giant at the bottom, lying on the rocks.

Ben: Without his head and without his hand. And Brabo, up high, is just about to throw that hand into the river.

Anna: Oh, so it's the moment before the throw.

Ben: The moment of the throw. Look at the arm.

Anna: Okay, so that's my second hand. My tally says two. Even if one's on a statue.

Ben: I'll allow it. Statues count.

Anna: And the women?

Ben: Three nude women, around the base. They're holding symbols of Antwerp, a boat, and a fort, which is Het Steen, where we just were.

Anna: So it ties up the whole story. Boat, fort, giant, hero.

Ben: It's an advertisement for the town, in bronze.

Anna: Anything else to spot?

Ben: Look up at the guild house roofs. The golden figures. Look at the town hall's front, the flags, and the central tower of the building. And notice how much of it is a pedestrian zone. You can wander the whole square.

Anna: And terraces. I can see cafés.

Ben: Plenty of them. A coffee here is a fair price for the view. There's also a famous old brown café on the square, Den Engel, at number 3, a historic place with a smoke-darkened ceiling and a mix of locals and visitors. If you want to sit for ten minutes, that's the sort of place.

Anna: Is that the champagne spot?

Ben: No. Save that one. We'll point you at one near the end.

Anna: Good. I'm saving myself.

Ben: Pause here as long as you like. Walk around the fountain. Take your photo.

Anna: Where next?

Ben: Look over the rooftops. You'll see a very tall tower. That's the cathedral. Keep it in front of you, and take the short streets toward it. You'll be at the church in about three minutes.

Anna: Just follow the tower.

Ben: Just follow the tower.

## Stop 3: The Cathedral of Our Lady

Anna: Okay. That is a very big tower.

Ben: The north tower is 123 meters tall. It's the tallest in the city.

Anna: And it looks a bit lopsided. There's another one next to it.

Ben: There is. The southern tower is shorter, and it has a wooden lantern on top.

Anna: A wooden lantern. On a stone cathedral.

Ben: It was meant to be temporary. In 1533, a fire destroyed the wooden roof. Money meant for a big expansion went to repairs instead. In 1536, they put up a wooden lantern as a stopgap.

Anna: And?

Ben: It's still there.

Anna: Nearly 500 years. That's one very successful stopgap.

Ben: It's a very Belgian solution. Temporary, and permanent.

Anna: How old is the church?

Ben: The story goes back to 1124, when a Romanesque church was founded. The Gothic church began in 1352, with the choir. The big north tower was started in 1420 and finished in 1518.

Anna: So almost 100 years on the tower alone.

Ben: And in 1521, Emperor Charles V backed a project called the Nieuw Werck, the New Work, which was meant to make the church about three times larger.

Anna: Three times? It's already huge.

Ben: And then the fire came, and the money went elsewhere. Today the church has six naves and it's 53.6 meters wide. The part that was never finished is now a garden.

Anna: I love that. A cathedral extension that became a garden.

Ben: Now, if you're going in, here is what to look for. There are four paintings by Rubens inside.

Anna: Four in one church. That's a good start.

Ben: Over the high altar, The Assumption of Mary. To the left of the altar, The Raising of the Cross. To the right, The Descent from the Cross. And in a chapel on the right, The Resurrection of Christ.

Anna: Do they still hang where they were meant to?

Ben: Three of the four hang in their original settings, which is rare.

Anna: And the Resurrection?

Ben: That one was commissioned as a memorial for the son-in-law of a printer called Plantin. We'll meet his house in a few stops. Remember the name Moretus.

Anna: Moretus. Printer. Got it.

Ben: Rubens himself is buried in a different church, Saint James's, not here.

Anna: So don't go looking for his tomb in the cathedral.

Ben: Correct.

Anna: And practical bits?

Ben: Check the opening hours and whether there's a ticket. Churches can close for services or events. And if it's raining, this is your first dry stop of the day.

Ben: These paintings were made for people to pray in front of, so give them a few quiet minutes.

Anna: Of course.

Ben: Pause here. Go in, if you can. Take your time.

Anna: And outside, afterward?

Ben: Step out into the small square by the church front. That's the Handschoenmarkt, the Glove Market. That's where we'll start the next stop.

## Stop 4: The Handschoenmarkt and the old lanes to the Vrijdagmarkt

Anna: Okay. Glove Market. I'm guessing there were gloves.

Ben: That's what the name says. The Handschoenmarkt, the market for gloves, right at the foot of the church.

Anna: And this is a really tiny square.

Ben: Small and sheltered, right under the cathedral. Look up, and you'll see the tower from very close.

Anna: Why is it called the glove market? Because the church hung gloves outside?

Ben: I can't tell you. We only know the name. We'd rather not make up a story.

Anna: Good rule.

Ben: Now. This stop is mostly a walk. From here to the Vrijdagmarkt, the Friday Market, it's a few minutes through the old lanes.

Anna: Which way do we go?

Ben: With the cathedral behind you, head south and a little west. The lanes here are narrow and cobbled, and they bend. If you lose your way, look for signs to the Plantin-Moretus Museum. Or keep your map open.

Anna: And what are we looking at as we go?

Ben: The small things. Old gables. Little shop windows. Doorways with carved details. This is a part of town that was rebuilt over and over, so you'll see different centuries in one street.

Anna: Which is why my tally still says two.

Ben: I'll give you a hint. Look at the wall plaques and the corner statues. Some of them carry the hand.

Anna: Really?

Ben: I can't promise. But the symbol is all over the city.

Anna: And what's the Friday Market? Is it a market on Fridays?

Ben: It was a market square, and the name comes from that. The Vrijdagmarkt is still an open square with old buildings around it, and the Plantin-Moretus Museum stands on it.

Anna: Does anything still happen on Fridays?

Ben: I can't confirm the current market days, so check locally. But the square is a good place to pause, and there's a bar on it, Marigold, at number 18, listed by a local magazine among the city's cocktail bars.

Anna: Cocktails at lunchtime. I'm tempted.

Ben: Hold on. We said we'd point you to the champagne one near the end.

Anna: Right. I'm being good.

Ben: Now, a little thread to carry through the lanes. Remember Christophe Plantin?

Anna: The printer. Moretus's father-in-law.

Ben: Right. He wasn't from Antwerp. He moved here from France around 1550, and started in the lace trade and in printing.

Anna: Lace. Of all things.

Ben: And in 1576, the same year as the Spanish Fury, the family moved into the building we're walking to. It was called De Gulden Passer, the Golden Compass.

Anna: The same year as the sack.

Ben: The same year. It's a reminder that life carried on, even in the bad years.

Anna: Alright. Walk with me. Which way again?

Ben: South and a little west, down the lanes, toward the Friday Market.

Anna: And while we walk, give me something to think about.

Ben: Think about how many hands it took to print a book in the 1500s. Every letter was set by hand, one at a time.

Anna: There it is. Another hand.

Ben: You're counting that one?

Anna: Tally says three. Don't argue.

Ben: I won't. Pause the tour here, and press play again when you reach the open square with the museum.

## Stop 5: The Plantin-Moretus Museum

Anna: Okay. Arrived. It looks like a normal house.

Ben: From the outside, it does. But this is one of the most important buildings in the history of printing. It's a World Heritage Site, listed in 2005.

Anna: A house?

Ben: A house, a workshop and a printing business, all together. The family lived above and printed below, for about four centuries.

Anna: Four hundred years in one building.

Ben: Plantin moved in in 1576. After he died, his son-in-law Jan Moretus took over the business, and the Moretus family kept it going, generation after generation.

Anna: Until when?

Ben: In 1876, Edward Moretus sold the company to the city of Antwerp. And a year later, in 1877, the public could visit the living rooms and presses.

Anna: So it has been a museum for nearly 150 years.

Ben: Over 140, yes. Now, here is the headline. The museum holds the two oldest surviving printing presses in the world.

Anna: The oldest in the world. In Antwerp.

Ben: In a house on a market square. Not in a grand hall.

Anna: What else is in there?

Ben: A Gutenberg Bible, the 36-line one. And the rooms where the family lived and worked.

Anna: So it's a time capsule.

Ben: A very good one. And there's a courtyard garden, so even if you're short on time, it's a calm place to stand for a minute.

Anna: Why was a printer so important to Antwerp?

Ben: Because in the 16th century, Antwerp was a place where ideas and goods and money moved fast. Printing is the same business. You make something, you send it far.

Anna: A bit like the port.

Ben: A paper port. Books left Antwerp the way pepper and cloth did.

Anna: And what's the link to Rubens?

Ben: This is the thread we promised. The Resurrection of Christ in the cathedral was painted as a memorial for Jan Moretus. So the painter and the printer worked in the same circle of the same city.

Anna: Good. The printing house, the painter, the church. All connected.

Ben: One city, one small world.

Anna: Practical?

Ben: This is an indoor stop, so it's your rain plan. Check the opening hours and ticket details before you go. It's a place where you could spend an hour or more if you read the labels.

Anna: And I should stay under an hour, I guess, because of the schedule.

Ben: If you're short on time, go for the presses, the printing room, and the courtyard.

Anna: Wait. A thought. The first printers made their letters by hand. They cut them in metal, they set them by hand, they pulled the press by hand.

Ben: And you're counting it.

Anna: Tally says four. Including that one.

Ben: I'm not sure that's what the bet meant.

Anna: The bet said hands. I'm just being generous.

Ben: Pause here. Go in if you can.

Anna: And then, the last stop?

Ben: Walk east, toward the shopping street called the Meir. It's about 10 minutes on foot. The Rubens House is just off it, on a short street called the Wapper. If you lose the way, ask for the Rubenshuis. Everybody knows it.

## Stop 6: The Rubens House

Anna: Okay. We're at the last stop. Is that it?

Ben: That's the front of the Rubens House. In Dutch, the Rubenshuis. It's on the Wapper, which used to be called Vaartstraat.

Anna: And this was his house?

Ben: In 1610, Rubens bought the property. A year earlier, in 1609, he'd married Isabella Brant. And he rebuilt it in the style of an Italian palace.

Anna: A palace in Antwerp.

Ben: With a studio, a portico, a courtyard, and a Baroque garden.

Anna: So he wasn't just a painter. He was a developer.

Ben: A very successful painter, with a studio full of students and assistants. A lot of his famous works came out of here.

Anna: So it was a workshop.

Ben: A workshop, a home and a showroom in one. He lived here for most of his life, until he died in 1640.

Anna: What happened to it after?

Ben: His widow, Helena Fourment, leased the house to William Cavendish and his wife Margaret. After Margaret left in 1660, the house was sold.

Anna: And how did it end up a museum?

Ben: A legacy from an architect named Henri Blomme helped the city buy the house in 1937.

Anna: Good for him.

Ben: And after extensive restoration, it opened as a museum in 1946, with period furniture and works by Rubens and his contemporaries.

Anna: Can we go in?

Ben: This is where I have to be careful. At the time of writing, the main section, the artist's residence, is closed for renovation. So please check the official website before you plan on going inside.

Anna: And if it's closed?

Ben: Look at the outside, and think about the garden. The courtyard opens onto the Baroque garden Rubens planned himself. Behind it is the Rubenianum, a study center and archive for scholars.

Anna: Right. So even if the house is shut, we've got the story.

Ben: And the four paintings in the cathedral. If you went inside earlier, you've already seen Rubens at work.

Anna: What else should we know about him?

Ben: He led the Antwerp school of painting in the 17th century. Another painter, Anthony van Dyck, belongs to the same school. And Rubens is buried in Saint James's church. If you have time left, ask for directions and check the hours.

Anna: And the city around his house. What did it look like in his day?

Ben: Poorer than in the golden age. The river was closed after he died, in 1648. But the art, the printing, and the sense of itself as a great city stayed.

Anna: So Rubens was at the end of the story.

Ben: Rather the middle of the story. The city came back, later, in a different way.

Anna: Okay. I think we need to do the tally.

Ben: Go on.

Anna: The hand in the river. The hand on the fountain. The printers' hands. The painter's hands. That's four. I won.

Ben: You said more than one giant hand. You got one in the legend, one on the fountain. Technically, yes.

Anna: I'll take it.

Ben: Pause here. Look at the house. Walk through the doorway, if you can.

Anna: And then?

Ben: Then one more thing, a drink. You said you'd been good.

Anna: I've been very good.

Ben: There's a champagne bar called the Laurent-Perrier Champagne Bar, at Meir 78, inside the old Stadsfeestzaal shopping building. It's a few minutes' walk from here, on the same shopping street.

Anna: Champagne. At last.

Ben: Check its opening hours first. It was listed as open until 6:30 in the evening on the day we looked, but hours change. And I can't promise it serves cocktails. If you want a cocktail with a view, there's a small bar called Jones and Co, on Pelgrimstraat, with a terrace that looks toward the cathedral. Again, check hours.

Anna: A toast to Rubens.

Ben: A toast to Rubens.

## Wrap-up

Anna: That's the walk. What have we done?

Ben: About two kilometers, in six stops. A riverside gateway from the 1200s. A square with a fountain and a rebuilt town hall. A cathedral with a lantern that was meant to be temporary. A lane to a printing house with the oldest presses in the world. And the home of a painter.

Anna: What's the theme?

Ben: That Antwerp is a city made by a river. It got rich when the river opened, it suffered when the river closed, and it came back when the river did.

Anna: And the hand?

Ben: The hand is the joke it tells about itself.

Anna: Final tally.

Ben: Four, according to you.

Anna: Four, according to me. Which means I won, and you owe me a drink.

Ben: I'm paying for champagne?

Anna: You said it, not me.

Ben: Back to the ship now. From the Rubens House, it's an easy walk back toward the river. Head west along the shopping street, then down to the water. Give yourself 20 minutes or more, and keep a map open.

Anna: And if you still have energy?

Ben: If you want the whole story of the city before tonight, play Antwerp in 15 Minutes. It covers the history in short.
