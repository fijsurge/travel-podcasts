## Notes before you listen (skip this part when listening)

A day walk through the museum quarter and De Pijp, in five stops. Three of them come from your plans: the Rijksmuseum at 1 PM with tickets, the Heineken rooftop tour at 4:45 PM with tickets, and dinner at Cafe de Klos at 6:30 PM, walk-ins only. The two in between, Museumplein and the Albert Cuyp market, are suggestions to fill the afternoon with sights and a champagne break, and you can skip either one. Distances are short, about two kilometers in all, so most of your day is spent standing still.

Stop positions are approximate, good to about 50 meters at best, and the position of the dinner stop is the least certain, so use the street address on a map. Opening hours, ticket rules, tour times and what is on show change often, so check them on the day. The museum may rearrange its rooms. This episode was written without being able to open web pages, so every fact comes from well-known reference history and should be checked against the list at the end. We found no famous ghost story attached to these places and did not invent any. The spooky material is real art and real history, and the one frightening true story, at the brewery, is told plainly.

## Stop 1: The Rijksmuseum

Anna: So, can I ask a rude question about the biggest museum in the country?

Ben: Always.

Anna: Why does it look like a cathedral that took a wrong turn?

Ben: Ha. People said that when it opened.

Anna: Really?

Ben: Really. The architect was Pierre Cuypers, a Catholic from the south of the Netherlands. The museum opened in 1885. Some Protestant critics in Amsterdam thought it looked far too much like a church.

Anna: To a country that had spent centuries being proudly Protestant.

Ben: Not quite proudly. Very, very seriously.

Anna: Fair.

Ben: And here's a thing. Cuypers also designed Amsterdam Central Station.

Anna: Wait. Both buildings?

Ben: Both. The station where you arrived, and the museum where you're standing. Same architect, same red brick, same fussy towers.

Anna: So he basically bookended the city.

Ben: More or less. The station is the front door, and this is the family treasure room.

Anna: Okay, where are we exactly?

Ben: You should be on the side facing Museumplein, the big lawn behind the museum, or at the main entrance. With a ticket for one o'clock, you go to the entrance, and you go in. We'll tell you the story while you queue, or later, if you pause us.

Anna: And the collection started where?

Ben: Not in Amsterdam. In 1800 it was founded in The Hague, as a national gallery.

Anna: Then it moved.

Ben: In 1808. Louis Bonaparte, Napoleon's brother, was king of the Netherlands, and he moved it to Amsterdam. For a while, it lived in the Royal Palace on the Dam. Then in a different old building, and finally here.

Anna: A museum that had to find a home.

Ben: Like a very expensive student.

Anna: Ha. Okay, so what do I look at?

Ben: Let's choose three things, because nobody can see all of it in an afternoon.

Anna: Three. Bet I get one right.

Ben: Go on.

Anna: The Night Watch.

Ben: Yes. Rembrandt, finished in 1642. And here's something people get wrong.

Anna: It's not at night.

Ben: It's not at night.

Anna: Ben!

Ben: It was a daytime scene. Over the centuries, the varnish darkened and the painting looked like a night scene, and the name stuck. The real title is much longer, something like the militia company of Captain Frans Banninck Cocq.

Anna: So a group portrait of a neighborhood militia.

Ben: Exactly. Amsterdam's civic guard companies commissioned group portraits, and Rembrandt made his dramatic instead of lined up in rows.

Anna: And people liked that?

Ben: History is mixed. We do know it's a huge painting, and it was cut down on the sides, in 1715, when it moved to the Town Hall on the Dam, because it didn't fit between two doors.

Anna: Wait. They cut a Rembrandt to fit a wall?

Ben: They did.

Anna: That is the least Dutch thing I've ever heard, and the most Dutch.

Ben: Practical to the bone. Look at the faces, and look for the girl in yellow, who gets lots of attention. Our advice is to go there first, before the crowds thicken.

Anna: And the other two?

Ben: Something quieter. Vermeer's painting of a kitchen maid pouring milk. It's small, and everybody who sees it stops talking.

Anna: And the third is for the spooky request.

Ben: Right. You wanted spooky.

Anna: I did.

Ben: So find a vanitas. That's a type of still life, very popular in the Netherlands in the seventeenth century. A table with fruit, flowers, a pocket watch, sometimes a skull.

Anna: A skull on a table.

Ben: As a reminder. The word means emptiness, or vanity. The message is that wealth and beauty don't last, so enjoy them but don't be fooled. The Latin phrase is memento mori, remember you will die.

Anna: That's very cheerful for a lunch break.

Ben: To be fair, it was the opposite of a threat. It was a gentle reminder, painted by artists who were selling to rich merchants.

Anna: Rich merchants who wanted a skull above the fireplace.

Ben: Next to the silver. Yes.

Anna: Okay, I love it. What else?

Ben: The model rooms. A cabinet house of the early eighteenth century, with tiny furniture, belonging to a woman named Petronella Oortman. It cost about as much as a real house.

Anna: A toy house the price of a house.

Ben: And the Gallery of Honour, the long hall leading to the Night Watch. It's the museum's spine.

Anna: And I've heard about the bicycles.

Ben: Ah. The passage.

Anna: There is a passage?

Ben: There's a tunnel under the central part of the building, with a public road and a bike path. For over a hundred years, people have cycled and walked straight through the museum.

Anna: Through it, not around it.

Ben: And when the museum was rebuilt, with work that started in 2003 and finished in 2013, a long ten years, a plan to close the passage ran into the cycling lobby.

Anna: Of course.

Ben: Of course. They won, and the passage stayed open.

Anna: So the national treasure house has a bike lane.

Ben: And it works. When you walk out the door, you'll see.

Anna: Okay. How long do we stay?

Ben: Your ticket is for one o'clock. Two hours is comfortable. Three if you love it. There's a garden outside, free to walk into, with sculptures, if you need air.

Anna: And then?

Ben: And then we leave through the back and go onto the lawn. Pause here as long as you like, and when you're out, press play.

## Stop 2: Museumplein

Ben: That's the Concertgebouw, the concert hall. It opened in 1888, just three years after the Rijksmuseum. Its orchestra is among the best known in the world, and the acoustics are famous.

Anna: A neat little quarter.

Ben: A deliberate one. This was open ground at the edge of the city in the nineteenth century. In 1883 it was a world fair site, and then it was turned into this cultural district, so the museums stand together.

## Stop 3: The Albert Cuyp Market and De Pijp

Anna: Ben, why is this neighborhood called the Pipe?

Ben: Good question. De Pijp. The name means the pipe, and the story is that the streets are long and narrow, straight and tall, like a pipe, with rows of tall houses.

Anna: Are you sure?

Ben: It's the common explanation. There's some doubt about the exact origin.

Anna: Okay. And it's a pipe with a market in it.

Ben: Right. The Albert Cuypstraat. You're probably on it by now.

Anna: I can hear it.

Ben: It runs for nearly a kilometer. The market has been here since 1905, and it's named after Albert Cuyp, a painter of the seventeenth century, who painted rivers and cows.

Anna: Cows.

Ben: Beautiful cows. A calm, golden-light painter. You might even have seen one of his paintings in the museum.

Anna: Cows then, cows now.

Ben: The market is mostly food and goods. Cheese, fish, flowers, and stroopwafels. A stroopwafel is a thin waffle with syrup between two layers, and if you find a stand making them fresh, buy one.

Anna: Is that the real thing?

Ben: Warm, yes. Cold, it's a cookie.

Anna: I'm going to bet that I buy three.

Ben: I'm not taking that bet.

Anna: Where's the champagne?

Ben: Side streets. Look for a small bar with a terrace. If you'd rather beer, nobody will stop you, and the brewery is coming.

Anna: One more question. De Pijp used to be a poor area?

Ben: In the nineteenth century, yes. It was built fast, in the 1870s and 1880s, as housing for workers outside the old canal ring. These days it's one of the most popular neighborhoods, with artists, students and cafes.

Anna: Who moved in?

Ben: Everyone. It's a standard story. First the workers, then the artists, then the people who like artists.

Anna: Then the people who like the people who like the artists.

Ben: Then the tourists.

Anna: Hello.

Ben: Hello. Enjoy it. The tour at the brewery is at 4:45 PM, so aim to be walking by 4:15.

Anna: Ten minutes?

Ben: From the western end of the market, ten minutes. Pause and eat something. We'll be here.

## Stop 4: The Heineken Brewery and the Rooftop Tour

Anna: Okay, this is huge. A giant brick building with a red star.

Ben: Number 78, Stadhouderskade. The old Heineken brewery.

Anna: It looks like a factory.

Ben: It was one. It sits right next to the canal that marks the old edge of the city.

Anna: So it's the actual place where beer was made.

Ben: For over a hundred years. The story starts in 1864. A young man named Gerard Adriaan Heineken, just twenty-two years old, bought a brewery called De Hooiberg, which means the haystack.

Anna: He bought a brewery at twenty-two.

Ben: With help. His mother put up money. And the family had money.

Anna: Naturally.

Ben: He soon built a new brewery on this spot, and it began producing in 1867. The city was growing, and beer was a big business.

Anna: And the beer is the one with the star.

Ben: The red star, yes. That became the trademark, and it's still on every bottle.

Anna: What's special about the beer?

Ben: One thing is the yeast. In the 1880s the company hired a scientist, Doctor Elion, who isolated a pure strain of yeast. The company calls it the A-yeast, and still uses a descendant of it.

Anna: Like a family pet.

Ben: Rather like a family pet. And a company secret.

Anna: Huh. So a brewery, a hundred years, then?

Ben: Then it closed. Making beer here ended in 1988, because the plant was too small for a modern brewery. The beer moved to bigger sites elsewhere in the country.

Anna: And the building became a museum.

Ben: A visitor attraction, now called the Heineken Experience. The tour is the main thing.

Anna: And we have the rooftop version.

Ben: Yes. Your ticket is for 4:45 PM. Don't be late, because tours start on the dot. Details vary, so follow the instructions you were given when you booked.

Anna: And what's on top?

Ben: We're not going to describe what we can't confirm. What we can say is that the building is one of the tall brick landmarks of the neighborhood, so a roof gives you a view over the south of the city.

Anna: And beer.

Ben: Tastings are part of the experience, yes. Please enjoy them responsibly, and get home safely.

Anna: Fair. Okay, but you promised spooky, and I think there's something here.

Ben: There is. It's not a legend. It's real, and it's serious, so we're going to talk about it plainly.

Anna: Okay.

Ben: In November 1983, the owner of the company, Freddy Heineken, was kidnapped, together with his driver, Ab Doderer. They were taken from the street near the company's offices and held captive for about three weeks.

Anna: By whom?

Ben: A group of Dutch criminals. They demanded a very large ransom, around 35 million guilders at the time, which was one of the largest ransoms ever paid in the world.

Anna: And they were freed?

Ben: Yes. Both men were released at the end of November. The kidnappers were later caught, and several were convicted.

Anna: That must have been terrifying.

Ben: It was. The case is still remembered here as one of the most shocking crimes in modern Dutch history.

Anna: Thank you for telling it straight.

Ben: It's part of the story of the place. We'll say no more.

Anna: Okay. Let's gather ourselves. Enjoy the tour.

Ben: Pause here for as long as the tour takes. About an hour and a half should be plenty, and then, dinner.

Anna: I'll be the one humming.

Ben: Of course you will.

## Stop 5: Cafe de Klos

Anna: Tour done, a few beers in. And it's evening. How are we doing for dinner?

Ben: Dinner is at 6:30 PM, at Cafe de Klos, and it's walk-ins only.

Anna: So no booking.

Ben: No booking. You turn up and wait. It's a small place and it fills up fast, so we'd suggest going straight there from the brewery.

Anna: How far?

Ben: About a kilometer, fifteen minutes on foot. Walk north, away from the canal, into the old town. Keep a map open. You're aiming for Kerkstraat, a long street between the canals, near the Vijzelgracht.

Anna: And the cafe?

Ben: Kerkstraat 41. It's a small, dark, cozy bar with a lot of character.

Anna: And what do we eat?

Ben: It's known in Amsterdam for spare ribs. Big plates of them, grilled, with sauces, and garlic. Local guides often mention the garlic.

Anna: Garlic. Spooky, if you think about vampires.

Ben: Ha. Nicely done. Garlic against vampires is folklore, and of course there are no vampires here. But if you ate a lot of garlic tonight, they'd leave you alone.

Anna: So the spooky request is covered.

Ben: Technically.

Anna: You said walk-ins only. What if there's a queue?

Ben: Then wait, or have a drink nearby. There are plenty of bars on the nearby streets. We can't promise a table, and we can't promise opening hours, so please check before you leave the brewery. A phone call or a look at a map will do.

Ben: No. They have other dishes. But you don't get this reputation for nothing.
