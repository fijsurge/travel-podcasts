## Welcome

Welcome to Brussels. Over the next hour or so, we are going to tell the story of a city that has been a marsh settlement, a ducal seat, the capital of a vast empire, a battlefield, a revolution, and today the working capital of Europe.

Brussels does not look like one thing. It is a Gothic town hall and a glass tower of the European Union. It is a medieval square rebuilt in four years after a bombardment, and a boulevard built over a river. It is a city where two languages share every street sign, and where nearly four in ten residents hold a foreign passport.

How did a small town on a muddy river end up here? The answer is a story of trade, guilds, dukes, emperors, revolutionaries, and a great deal of stubbornness.

We will move in order, from the first settlement to the present day. Let's begin with a river.

## Chapter 1: A Settlement in the Marsh

Every city has an origin story, and the story of Brussels begins with mud.

The river is called the Senne. It is a small, slow stream that once wandered across a wide, wet valley in the middle of what is now Belgium, splitting into channels and forming islands as it went. People have lived along the edges of that valley for a very long time. Archaeologists have found traces of Iron Age settlers in the Brussels area, at a site called Champ Saint-Anne in the neighborhood of Anderlecht. Roman farms and country houses were scattered across the surrounding countryside, though the Romans never built a real town on the spot.

After the Roman Empire faded, Frankish settlers moved in. In the same field in Anderlecht, a Frankish cemetery with more than three hundred graves has been uncovered, dating from roughly the years 500 to 700. These were farmers and fighters, living in small villages on the dry ground above the river, in the new Frankish kingdom that would eventually grow into the empire of Charlemagne.

Now comes the part where history shades into legend.

Tradition says that around the year 580, a holy man named Gaugericus, known in French as Saint Géry, who was bishop of the city of Cambrai, built a small chapel on an island in the Senne. Historians are cautious about this story, because the sources are later and less certain. But it is a good candidate for the seed of the city. The place is still there. It is a square in the old center called Place Saint-Géry, and a former market hall stands on it, close to where the island once was.

The name Brussels itself points to the landscape. In its earliest forms, it appears as something like Bruocsella, and most scholars read it as meaning a home in the marsh, or a dwelling by the swamp. It is not a grand name. It is the name a farmer would give to a wet piece of ground. And it tells us something true about this place: it began as a settlement that had to deal with water.

So why did a town grow here at all? The answer is geography. The Senne valley sat on an important route. Trade roads ran from the rich cloth-making cities of Flanders in the west to Cologne and the Rhine in the east. Merchants carrying wool, cloth, wine, and metalwork passed through this region, and a spot where a river could be crossed or where boats could be brought upstream was a natural place to stop, unload, and do business. The islands in the Senne offered safe ground. The hills to the east offered a good place for a fortress.

For several centuries, the settlement was just a small place within a much larger political world. In the ninth century, Charlemagne's empire was divided among his heirs, and the region that would become Belgium found itself caught in the middle, in a long, contested territory known as Lotharingia, named for the Frankish king Lothar. The area went back and forth between the western and eastern halves of the old empire. Later, the dukes who ruled Lotharingia on behalf of the German emperors kept a loose grip, and local lords held most of the real power.

Then, in the late tenth century, we get our first clear date.

In 977, the German emperor Otto the Second gave the duchy of Lower Lotharingia, which is roughly today's Belgium and part of the Netherlands, to a man named Charles. Charles was a member of the old Carolingian family, a son of a French king, and a brother of the reigning French king. He was an exile from the throne of France, looking for a power base. Within a couple of years, he built a fortification on one of the islands in the Senne, the same island with the old chapel.

In about the year 979, Charles did something else that mattered even more. He moved the relics of a saint named Gudula, a pious woman from the seventh century, to the chapel on the island. Gudula became the patron saint of Brussels, and that move is the date most often given as the official founding of the city. The tradition goes that Gudula was a noblewoman who loved to go to church before dawn, carrying a lantern, and that the devil kept blowing out her light, and each time an angel lit it again.

A caution is in order. Modern historians point out that the story of Charles building a castle and founding the city is based on sources written long after the fact. The real picture is likely messier, with a growing settlement, a chapel, a market, and local lords all developing over several generations. But 979 is the date that stuck, and Brussels celebrated its thousandth birthday in 1979.

Gudula's story had another long-term effect. In 1047, a powerful nobleman, Lambert the Second, Count of Leuven, which is a city about twenty-five kilometers to the east, moved her relics again, this time to a new church on a hill overlooking the river valley. He founded a college of priests there, called canons, to serve the church. That hill church, dedicated to Saint Michael and, in time, to Saint Gudula as well, is the origin of the twin-towered cathedral that still stands in Brussels today. Saint Michael, the dragon slayer, became the other patron of the city, and he stands on top of the town hall, as we will see.

By this point, the pieces were in place. There was a lowland market settlement on the river, where merchants and craftsmen gathered. There was a church on the hill, with wealth and influence. There was a castle for protection. And there was a ruling family, the counts of Leuven, who increasingly saw this place as an important part of their growing territory.

Around the year 1100, the people of Brussels built their first defensive wall, a modest circuit around the main settlement. Inside, the town was small, perhaps a few thousand people. They made cloth, baked bread, brewed beer, and traded. It was not yet a great city. But it was now a real town, with walls, a market, a church, and a lord. And it was about to enter a time of rapid growth.

## Chapter 2: Dukes, Guilds and the Grand Place, 1100 to 1430

In the twelfth century, Brussels came into the hands of an ambitious family that would turn a market town into the capital of a duchy.

The counts of Leuven, who held Brussels, steadily rose in rank. They became dukes of Lower Lotharingia, and then, as the old title lost its meaning, dukes of Brabant. The duchy of Brabant was one of the richest territories in northwest Europe. It stretched across the heart of what is now Belgium and part of the southern Netherlands, and it included great towns like Leuven, Antwerp, and Brussels. For a long time, Leuven was the leading city. But gradually the dukes spent more time in Brussels, in a castle on the hill called the Coudenberg, which means cold hill. By the late Middle Ages, Brussels was the main seat of the dukes, and with the dukes came money, officials, and prestige.

But Brussels owed its growth just as much to its own people. The town made cloth. Wool came in from England, weavers and dyers worked it into fabric, and merchants sold it across Europe. Alongside the cloth trade grew many other crafts: brewing, baking, butchery, metalwork, carpentry, and eventually luxury goods like tapestries. The people who practiced these trades organized themselves into guilds, which were part trade union, part business association, part social club, and part political party.

At the center of it all was the market. Down in the valley, on low ground close to the river, there was an open space known as the Lower Market. It was a place for traders to set up stalls and for townspeople to meet and do business. Over the centuries, wooden stalls turned into stone halls, and the guilds built meeting houses around the edges. That muddy field is the Grand Place, the great square at the heart of Brussels today. By the end of the thirteenth century, the drapers' guild had its own hall there, and the foundation of the square's future was laid.

Politically, the town was run by a narrow elite. Power lay in the hands of a few wealthy families, known as the Seven Lineages of Brussels, who held the city council and the best offices. Below them were the guilds, which had economic muscle but little say in government. This was a recipe for tension, and over time it boiled over.

Here is where we meet one of the great documents of Brussels history. In 1355, the Duke of Brabant, John the Third, died without a male heir. His eldest daughter, Joanna, became duchess, and her husband was a man we have met before if you listened to our story of Luxembourg: Wenceslas of Luxembourg, the half brother of the Emperor Charles the Fourth. In January of 1356, before they could take up their rule, Joanna and Wenceslas had to sign a charter promising to respect the rights of the towns and nobles of Brabant. This charter is called the Joyous Entry. It was named for the ceremonial entry that a new ruler made into the cities of the duchy.

The Joyous Entry laid out limits on the power of the duke. He could not declare war, sign treaties, or raise taxes without the consent of the estates, and the charter included a remarkable clause that allowed the people of Brabant to withdraw their obedience if the duke broke the rules. It is often compared to the Magna Carta in England. Historians caution that its later reputation was somewhat exaggerated by romantic writers of the nineteenth century, but it remained an important symbol for centuries, and Brabantine rulers swore to uphold it for generations.

Almost immediately, the charter was tested. Louis of Male, the Count of Flanders, a powerful neighbor with his own claim to Brabant, invaded and seized Brussels in 1356. But he did not hold it for long. A citizen of Brussels named Everard t'Serclaes, from one of the great families, led a night raid with a small band of men, slipped into the city, and drove out the Flemish garrison. Everard became a folk hero. He was killed by a rival lord a few decades later, and today a bronze monument to him lies against a wall just off the Grand Place. According to local custom, rubbing the bronze arm of the statue brings good luck, and you will see that the metal is polished bright by the hands of passersby.

The liberation of Brussels helped fuel another great project. With the city growing quickly, the old walls had become too small. Between 1356 and 1383, the people of Brussels built a second, much larger ring of walls, with seven main gates and dozens of towers. This wall was a huge undertaking for a town of this size. It enclosed about eight kilometers of ground and made room for the city to expand. Most of it was torn down in the nineteenth century, and its path is now traced by a ring of boulevards around the old center, known as the Petite Ceinture, or the small ring. If you look at a map of central Brussels, the five-sided shape of the boulevards is the outline of the old medieval city. One gate survives, the heavy, castle-like Porte de Hal, on the southern side of the ring.

The early fifteenth century was the great age of civic building. In 1402, work began on the Brussels Town Hall, on the Grand Place. The left wing was built first, and the right wing followed in the 1440s. In 1449 the architects began the soaring tower, a lace of stone that rises about ninety-six meters, topped by a gilded statue of Saint Michael slaying the dragon. A famous detail is that the tower is not centered above the main doorway of the building, and the wings are of slightly different lengths. According to an old story, the architect was so horrified by the asymmetry that he threw himself from the tower. Historians think that is just a legend, but it is a good one.

There is another city landmark from this period, and it is the smallest. A fountain figure of a little boy, relieving himself into a basin, is first mentioned in a document from 1388. It is the Manneken Pis. The bronze statue you can visit today is a copy of a version made in 1619 by the sculptor Jérôme Duquesnoy, and it stands on a street corner a few minutes from the Grand Place. The boy has become an icon, and he has a wardrobe of well over a thousand costumes, which are changed regularly on special occasions. Many legends try to explain him. In one, a small duke of Leuven, hung in a basket from a tree during a battle, urinated on the enemy and inspired his soldiers. In another, a boy put out a burning fuse and saved the city. No one knows the real story, and Brussels does not mind.

Meanwhile, the tension between the Seven Lineages and the guilds finally exploded in 1421. A group of guildsmen, angry about the patricians' control of the government and rising taxes, stormed the town hall, and several city magistrates were killed. The duke, John the Fourth, intervened to restore order, but the result was a victory for the craftsmen. From then on, the guilds, organized into nine groups known as the Nations of Brussels, had a formal share of power on the city council. For the first time, Brussels had a government in which ordinary working people had a say.

In the same period, the dynasty that ruled the duchy kept changing. Wenceslas died in 1383 without children. Joanna lived until 1406 and left the duchy to her grandnephew, a son of the powerful Duke of Burgundy, and from then on, Brabant's rulers were a succession of Burgundian princes, one of whom was killed at the Battle of Agincourt in 1415. The family line died out. And in 1430, the duchy fell to the most powerful of all of them: Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy.

If you are out walking in Brussels, here is what to look for. On the Grand Place, look up at the Town Hall tower and notice that it is off-center. Then walk a few steps to the Rue Charles Buls, just off the square, to find the monument to Everard t'Serclaes. And a few streets farther, you will find the Manneken Pis, surprisingly small, usually surrounded by a crowd.

## Chapter 3: Capital of the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands, 1430 to 1568

In 1430, Brussels hit the jackpot. The duchy of Brabant passed to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, one of the richest and most glamorous princes in Europe. And Philip made a choice that changed the city's destiny. Out of all his great towns, he chose Brussels as his main residence.

To understand why that mattered, you need to picture the Burgundian state. It was not a kingdom on a map the way we think of kingdoms. It was a patchwork of territories collected through marriage, inheritance, and purchase: Flanders, Brabant, Holland, Hainaut, and soon the duchy of Luxembourg, which Philip took in 1443, as we heard in the story of Luxembourg. These territories covered roughly what is now Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, and a good slice of northern France. Philip ruled them from a court that was the most dazzling in Europe, full of knights, painters, musicians, and diplomats. In 1430, the same year he inherited Brabant, he founded a famous chivalric brotherhood, the Order of the Golden Fleece.

And the heart of that court, more and more, was Brussels. The dukes lived at the Coudenberg Palace, on the hill above the town, which they enlarged into a huge complex of halls, chapels, and courtyards. Government offices followed the court. Money followed the offices. Artists and craftsmen followed the money.

The art of this period is extraordinary. In 1436, the city appointed a painter named Rogier van der Weyden as its official city painter. Rogier, who had been born in Tournai and trained there, became one of the great masters of what we now call Early Netherlandish painting. His portraits, with their sharp, searching faces, and his emotional religious scenes, were admired across Europe. For the town hall, he painted a series of panels about justice, scenes of rulers hearing the cases of ordinary people. Those paintings were destroyed in 1695, but the artist's reputation lived on. Rogier is buried in the cathedral of Brussels.

Brussels also became famous for tapestry. Weavers in the city produced huge, richly colored wall hangings, made of wool, silk, and threads of gold and silver. Tapestries were the luxury art of the age, and a prince would pay more for a fine set than for a painting. Brussels workshops became so renowned that, in the 1510s, Pope Leo the Tenth commissioned a set of tapestries for the Sistine Chapel, based on designs by Raphael, and the weaving was done in Brussels. The city soon introduced a quality mark, a pair of the letter B around a red shield, woven into the edge of authentic pieces to protect the brand.

The Burgundian dream ended abruptly in 1477. Philip's grandson, Charles the Bold, was killed fighting the Swiss and Lorrainers outside the city of Nancy, leaving a single daughter, Mary of Burgundy. Mary had been born in Brussels, and she was the most eligible heiress in Europe. She married Maximilian of Habsburg, an Austrian prince who would become Holy Roman Emperor, and when she died in a riding accident in 1482, her lands passed to their children. Through that marriage, the Burgundian Netherlands fell into the hands of the Habsburgs, the most powerful dynasty of the next three centuries.

The most important Habsburg for Brussels was Mary's grandson, Charles. He was born in Ghent in the year 1500, but he was raised in the Netherlands and he saw Brussels as home. When he was six, he inherited the Burgundian lands, and in 1516, he was proclaimed King of Spain in a ceremony in the church of Saint Gudula in Brussels. In 1519, he was elected Holy Roman Emperor, as Charles the Fifth, ruler of an empire that stretched from Spain and Italy to Germany, the Netherlands, and the new Spanish colonies in the Americas.

Charles was usually traveling, so he needed trusted regents to govern the Netherlands in his absence. His aunt Margaret of Austria ruled from nearby Mechelen. Later, his sister Mary of Hungary took over, and in 1531 the central government institutions were fixed in Brussels. That is when Brussels truly became the capital of the Low Countries, a role it has kept, in one form or another, ever since.

It was also a time of intellectual brilliance. In 1521, the great humanist Erasmus of Rotterdam, the most famous scholar in Europe, spent several months in Anderlecht, then a village outside Brussels, living in a house that you can visit today as a museum. In 1514, a boy named Andreas Vesalius was born in Brussels. He grew up to be a pioneer of modern anatomy, and his groundbreaking book on the structure of the human body, published in 1543, corrected centuries of errors by examining real corpses. And in 1563, the great painter Pieter Bruegel the Elder moved to Brussels. He married the daughter of a well-known local artist, and in the remaining years of his short life he painted some of his most famous scenes of peasant life and village feasts. He died in Brussels in 1569 and was buried in a church in the Marolles district.

Charles made one more big mark on the city. In 1549, there was a great festival in Brussels to present his son and heir, Philip, to the people of the Netherlands. The procession of guilds, nobles, and giant figures that wound through the streets is remembered today in a summer pageant called the Ommegang, held every year on the Grand Place. And in October of 1555, in the great hall of the Coudenberg Palace, Charles gave up his crown. Exhausted, ill, and weary of rule, he announced that he was handing the Netherlands over to Philip the Second. Witnesses recorded that he leaned on the shoulder of a young nobleman, the twenty-two-year-old William of Orange, and that the hall wept as he spoke. Soon afterward, he handed Spain over too, and retired to a monastery.

By now, Brussels was a city of tens of thousands of people, a great center of government, art, and trade. But dark clouds were on the horizon. In 1517, a German monk named Martin Luther had challenged the Catholic Church, and the new Protestant ideas spread quickly through the Low Countries. In 1523, in the very center of Brussels, two young Augustinian monks, Henry Voes and Johannes van den Esschen, were burned at the stake on the Grand Place for refusing to renounce Luther's teachings. They are considered the first martyrs of the Protestant Reformation. It was a sign of things to come.

Philip the Second, the new ruler, was a devoutly Catholic king who lived in Spain and never really understood the Netherlands. He intended to stamp out heresy, enforce Catholic orthodoxy, and tighten royal control over the proud cities. The cities, with their ancient privileges, were not going to give in without a fight.

If you want to touch this chapter, go to the Place Royale, at the top of the hill. Under the square, an archaeological site called the Coudenberg lets you walk through the surviving cellars and streets of the old ducal palace. You will be walking the same ground where Charles the Fifth gave up the biggest empire in the world.

## Chapter 4: Revolt, Bombardment and Reinvention, 1568 to 1795

The Netherlands of the sixteenth century was a powder keg, and the spark came from Spain.

Philip the Second sent troops and a harsh general, the Duke of Alba, to put down any sign of rebellion. Alba set up a special court in Brussels that the locals called the Council of Blood, which tried thousands of people for heresy and treason. Among those arrested were two of the most prominent noblemen in the country, the Count of Egmont and the Count of Hoorn. Both were Catholics, loyal subjects who had only asked the king to show some moderation. It made no difference.

On the fifth of June, 1568, Egmont and Hoorn were beheaded on a scaffold in front of the town hall on the Grand Place. The execution shocked the Netherlands. Egmont became a martyr, and in later centuries his story inspired a play by Goethe and an overture by Beethoven. It helped turn a local protest into a full-scale war, which we call the Eighty Years' War.

The revolt was long and bloody. In the north, the Dutch provinces under William of Orange eventually broke away and formed the Dutch Republic. In the south, the fighting seesawed. Brussels itself joined the rebels and, for a few years in the 1570s and 1580s, was run by a militant Calvinist regime. But in 1585, a Spanish army under the Duke of Parma retook Brussels after a long siege. Many Protestants fled north, taking their skills and their money with them, and they helped build the great Dutch cities of the seventeenth century. The southern Netherlands, including Brussels, stayed under Spanish control and stayed Catholic. A border had been drawn through the Low Countries, and it is, roughly, the border between Belgium and the Netherlands today.

Under the Spanish, Brussels recovered slowly. From 1598 to 1621, the southern Netherlands were governed by a popular and cultured couple, the Archduke Albert and the Archduchess Isabella, who made Brussels a glittering capital again. Their court attracted artists, including the great Peter Paul Rubens, who painted for them. A wave of baroque churches and palaces rose across the city. It looked like a golden age.

But Spain was declining, and a new threat was rising in the south: France, under King Louis the Fourteenth, the Sun King. Louis wanted to expand France's frontiers to the north and east, and the Spanish Netherlands lay right in his path. For decades, French armies marched back and forth across the region, and the country suffered from endless war.

The worst blow fell in August of 1695, during a conflict called the Nine Years' War. A French army of tens of thousands, under Marshal Villeroi, marched on Brussels, not to capture it, but to punish it. The French wanted to draw their enemies away from another city under siege, the fortress of Namur. Brussels was a rich city that was poorly defended, so it made a tempting target.

On the evening of the thirteenth of August, French cannons and mortars began to fire red-hot shot and incendiary shells into the city. The bombardment went on for about two days and nights. The spire of the Town Hall was the obvious target, since it was the tallest landmark, and the shells rained down around it. Fires spread through the narrow medieval streets. When it was over, about a third of the buildings in Brussels had been destroyed or badly damaged. The Grand Place was in ruins. All that remained of the town hall was the shell of its stone walls, still standing, the spire miraculously intact. It was the most destructive event in the city's long history. The warning had been sent ahead, so the casualties were far fewer than they might have been, but the damage to property was staggering.

What happened next is the best part of the story. The people of Brussels did not despair. They did not abandon the center, and they did not rebuild on a new plan. Instead, the guilds, which owned the plots around the Grand Place, set to work. The city council and the governor required that every plan be submitted for approval, which meant that the new buildings would fit together. Within about four years, the square had risen again, in stone this time rather than wood. Each guildhall was a showpiece, decked with gilded statues, carvings, and symbols of its trade. Look at the facades today and you can read the trades: the butchers, the bakers, the brewers, the boatmen, the cabinet-makers, and the painters. One house, the one that belonged to the guild of the boatmen, has a gable shaped like a ship's stern. Another crowned its roofline with a golden phoenix, rising from the flames, as a symbol of the rebirth. The mixture of Gothic, baroque, and Louis the Fourteenth styles should not work, but it does, and in 1998, UNESCO placed the Grand Place on its World Heritage list.

In the early eighteenth century, the Spanish Habsburg line died out, and after a long war of succession, the southern Netherlands passed to the Austrian Habsburgs in 1714. For the next eighty years, Brussels was the capital of the Austrian Netherlands, and the country enjoyed a long period of relative peace and prosperity.

The most beloved ruler of this period was Charles of Lorraine, a brother-in-law of the Empress Maria Theresa, who governed the Austrian Netherlands from 1744 to 1780. He was a genial, sociable man who loved hunting, science, and a good table. Under his rule, Brussels was modernized in the elegant neoclassical style. In 1731, the old Coudenberg Palace had been destroyed by a fire, and its ruins were cleared away and covered. In its place, the authorities built a handsome new district: the Place Royale, a harmonious square lined with uniform facades, and a park laid out in geometric patterns, now the Parc de Bruxelles, with straight avenues and statues. These are among the most graceful spaces in the city today, and they date from this period.

The old order was about to crack. The Enlightenment had arrived, and reforming ideas were in the air. The Emperor Joseph the Second, who ruled from Vienna from 1780, tried to modernize his lands at breakneck speed, closing monasteries, reorganizing the courts, and curbing the power of the church. Many people in the Austrian Netherlands, who loved their ancient privileges, were furious. In 1789, the year the French Revolution began, a rebellion broke out in Brabant. It drove the Austrians out, and in January of 1790 a new state, the United Belgian States, was proclaimed. It was the first time the word Belgian appeared in the name of a country, but the republic quickly collapsed in quarrels between conservatives and democrats, and the Austrians returned in December of 1790.

They did not last long. In 1792, French revolutionary armies marched in, and after a few years of back and forth, they won a decisive victory in 1794. In 1795, France formally annexed the southern Netherlands, as it did the Duchy of Luxembourg, which became the Department of the Forests. Brussels became the capital of a French department called the Dyle. The old privileges of the Joyous Entry were swept away. The ancien régime was gone, and Brussels, like the rest of what would become Belgium, was now part of a new France.

## Chapter 5: Napoleon, Waterloo and the Revolution of 1830

The French years were short, but they changed Brussels more than almost any other period.

After the annexation of 1795, the revolutionary government set about erasing the old order. Monasteries and convents were closed, and their lands and buildings were sold off. Many medieval churches and abbeys, some of them centuries old, were demolished or turned into barracks, warehouses, and factories. The guilds were abolished. Feudal privileges disappeared. In their place, France introduced new laws, new weights and measures, and eventually the Napoleonic Code, which became the basis of Belgian civil law. For ordinary people, the most unpopular novelty was conscription. Young men from Brussels and its countryside were drafted into Napoleon's armies, and many never came back.

Napoleon himself visited Brussels more than once, and he used the royal estate at Laeken, on the northern edge of the city, as a residence. It would later become the home of the Belgian royal family. Even after his fall, the artist who had painted his coronation, Jacques-Louis David, chose Brussels as his place of exile. He lived there from 1816 until he died in 1825.

The end of the empire came with startling closeness to Brussels. In 1814, Napoleon was defeated and exiled, and the Congress of Vienna, as we heard in the story of Luxembourg, redrew the map. The great powers decided that France's northern neighbors needed to be stronger, so they combined the old Austrian Netherlands with the Dutch Republic to create a single United Kingdom of the Netherlands, under King William the First. The new kingdom had two capitals. Government and parliament met alternately in The Hague and in Brussels.

Then, in March of 1815, Napoleon escaped from the island of Elba and returned to France. For about a hundred days, he ruled again, and he marched north to strike the allied armies that were gathering in Belgium, under the Duke of Wellington and the Prussian Field Marshal Blücher.

Brussels, in June 1815, was filled with British and allied officers and their families. On the evening of the fifteenth of June, the Duchess of Richmond held a famous ball in the city for Wellington and his officers. In the middle of the evening, news arrived that Napoleon's army had crossed the border and was advancing. Officers slipped out of the ball, still in their dress uniforms, and marched south to fight. The scene became so famous that the poet Lord Byron, who visited Brussels a year later, wrote some of his best-known verses about that night of dancing and the sound of guns.

Three days later, on the eighteenth of June, the decisive battle was fought at a village about fifteen kilometers south of Brussels. It was called Waterloo. On that field, the Allied armies, with Wellington's British, Dutch, and Belgian troops and Blücher's Prussians, crushed Napoleon's army and ended his career for good. In Brussels, people could hear the cannon, and in the days that followed the city was filled with thousands of wounded soldiers, who were treated in houses, churches, and makeshift hospitals.

With Napoleon gone, the new Kingdom of the Netherlands got to work. King William was a hardworking, practical ruler who promoted industry, canals, schools, and a modern economy. Brussels flourished. The city got a new opera house, the Théâtre de la Monnaie, which opened in 1819, on the site of the old royal mint. But the kingdom had a basic flaw. The north was mostly Protestant and Dutch-speaking, and the south was mostly Catholic, with a French-speaking upper class and a Dutch-speaking, Flemish population. The king favored the Dutch language and the north in appointments and taxation. French-speaking liberals resented his controls over the press and the schools, and Catholics resented his interference in the church. In 1828, these two groups, who normally disliked each other, joined forces against the king. It was the beginning of the end.

And then came the night at the opera.

On the twenty-fifth of August, 1830, there was a performance at the Monnaie of a popular French opera called The Mute Girl of Portici. It told the story of a revolt in seventeenth-century Naples against Spanish rule, and it contained a stirring duet about love of country. As the singers reached that number, the audience was swept up. They poured out of the theater into the streets, shouting, and joined crowds that were already angry about food prices and unemployment. Over the next days, mobs attacked the houses of unpopular officials and the offices of a pro-government newspaper. Similar riots broke out in other towns.

At first it was a protest, and its leaders wanted autonomy, not independence. But King William hesitated, then sent troops under his son, Prince Frederick, to restore order. On the twenty-third of September, some ten thousand Dutch soldiers entered Brussels through the gates on the upper side of the city and tried to push down the hill toward the old center. They were met by a ragged force of volunteers: workers, students, craftsmen, and men from other towns, including Liège, who had come to help. For four days, from the twenty-third to the twenty-sixth of September, fighting raged around the Royal Park, in the streets, from house to house, over barricades made of paving stones, carts, and furniture. By the end of the fourth night, the Dutch had pulled back.

A provisional government had already taken control in Brussels, and on the fourth of October, it declared Belgium independent. A National Congress was elected, and in February of 1831 it adopted a constitution that was among the most liberal in Europe, with guarantees of freedom of the press, of religion, and of association, and a king with limited powers. The Congress chose Brussels as the capital, and it sought a king acceptable to the great powers. After much negotiation, they picked a German prince, Leopold of Saxe-Coburg, a widower of the British royal family and an uncle to the future Queen Victoria.

On the twenty-first of July, 1831, Leopold stood on the steps of a church on the Place Royale and swore an oath to the constitution. That day, July twenty-first, is still Belgium's National Day.

The new country had to defend itself at once. A few weeks later, in August of 1831, the Dutch army invaded in what is called the Ten Days' Campaign, and a French army had to march in to save Belgium. It took years of negotiation before the Dutch would accept the loss. In 1839, a treaty signed in London finally confirmed Belgium's independence, and it guaranteed the new country's perpetual neutrality, under the protection of the great powers. The same treaty, as we heard in the story of Luxembourg, split the old duchy of Luxembourg in two, and handed its western half, including the town of Bastogne, to Belgium.

If you are walking in the center of Brussels, there are two places worth a stop. At the Place des Martyrs, a quiet neoclassical square near the city center, a monument and a crypt honor the fighters who died in the September days of 1830. And at the Parc de Bruxelles, the same park where the Dutch troops fought their way in and lost, you can walk among the trees and statues where the battle for Belgian independence was won.

## Chapter 6: Leopold's Capital, the Belle Époque and the Congo, 1831 to 1914

The new Belgium turned out to be a remarkable success story, and Brussels was its showcase.

Belgium was the first country on the European continent to undergo an industrial revolution, following the example of Britain. Coal and iron in the south, textiles in Flanders, and a good banking system created real wealth. In 1835, the first railway on the continent opened between Brussels and the nearby city of Mechelen, and within a few years, the country had a dense network of tracks, with Brussels at the center. The population grew fast, and the capital swelled with civil servants, bankers, merchants, and workers.

The city also became a haven for ideas, and for exiles. In 1834, liberal Brussels founded a new university, the Free University of Brussels, dedicated to free inquiry, independent of both state and church. Because the new Belgian constitution was so liberal, the city attracted writers, thinkers, and political refugees from across Europe. The French poet Charles Baudelaire stayed there, and so did the novelist Victor Hugo, who lived for a time on the Grand Place. In 1873, in a Brussels hotel room, the poet Paul Verlaine shot and wounded his young lover, the poet Arthur Rimbaud, and then went to jail in Belgium.

One exile, above all, left a mark. In 1845, the young German philosopher Karl Marx, expelled from France, came to Brussels with his wife and family, and he stayed until 1848. With his friend Friedrich Engels, he wrote a pamphlet in Brussels, in the winter of 1847 and 1848, called the Manifesto of the Communist Party. It was published in London in February of 1848, and Marx was expelled from Belgium a few weeks later. The pamphlet he wrote there went on to shape the history of the twentieth century.

Meanwhile, the city itself was in trouble. The Senne river, which had made Brussels possible, had become a disaster. It ran through the middle of the city, an open sewer, choked with refuse, and it flooded regularly. It carried cholera. The stench in summer was notorious. In the 1860s, the city's reforming mayor, Jules Anspach, decided to bury it. Between 1867 and 1871, the river was covered with a long brick vault, and above it, the city built a grand new avenue lined with elegant buildings, modeled on the Paris boulevards of Baron Haussmann. That avenue is called the Boulevard Anspach, after the mayor who built it. The river is still there today, flowing in a tunnel beneath the city. In the nineteenth century, they built a Stock Exchange, the Bourse, in the heart of the new district, and the whole center was transformed. It was an enormous achievement, and also a loss: hundreds of old houses and streets in the working-class neighborhoods were torn down, and whole communities were uprooted.

There was another giant project. In 1866, work began on a vast new Palace of Justice, designed by the architect Joseph Poelaert, on a hill above the Marolles. When it was finished in 1883, it was often described as the largest building constructed anywhere in the nineteenth century, a colossal mass of columns, domes, and staircases that dwarfs the city around it. To build it, authorities flattened part of the old working-class Marolles district. Locals have grumbled about it ever since.

In 1865, Leopold the First died, and his son took the throne as Leopold the Second. Leopold the Second reigned for forty-four years, and he was ambitious in a way that his father was not. He felt that his country was too small, and he dreamed of an empire. The great European powers were carving up Africa, and Leopold wanted a piece of it.

At a conference in Berlin in 1884 and 1885, the great powers recognized Leopold as the ruler of a vast territory in central Africa, about eighty times the size of Belgium. This territory was not a Belgian colony. It was the king's personal property, called the Congo Free State. And what followed was one of the worst atrocities of the colonial era. Leopold's agents forced villagers to collect rubber and ivory, under a system of terror: hostages were taken, villages burned, and people who failed to meet quotas were beaten, mutilated, or killed. Historians estimate that millions of Congolese died from violence, starvation, and disease during these years, although the exact numbers are debated. Reports by missionaries, journalists, and the British diplomat Roger Casement, and a campaign led by the journalist Edmund Morel, eventually produced an international outcry. In 1908, under heavy pressure, the Belgian state took over the colony from the king, and it became the Belgian Congo, which Belgium ruled until 1960.

The money that Leopold drew from the Congo went partly into Brussels. He used it to build, and to impress. He built the great Cinquantenaire park and its triumphal arch, to commemorate fifty years of Belgian independence. He built wide avenues, such as Avenue Louise, and monuments, and royal greenhouses at Laeken, and a huge museum of Central Africa in the suburb of Tervuren. For the Brussels world's fair of 1897, he organized a colonial exhibition in which Congolese people were put on display in a replica village. The grand showpiece of Belgium's capital had been paid for, in part, by a terrible human cost, and that is a story that Brussels has been grappling with ever since.

In the same decades, a very different kind of beauty emerged in Brussels. In the 1890s, a group of young architects, led by Victor Horta, created a new style that rejected the heavy historical imitations of the time. They used iron, glass, and sweeping curves inspired by plants and flowers, in what came to be known as Art Nouveau, or the new art. In 1893 and 1894, Horta built a townhouse for a professor named Emile Tassel, in which iron columns shaped like stems and stairs twining like vines flowed through the whole house, with light pouring in from above. It is widely regarded as the first Art Nouveau building. Other architects, including Paul Hankar and Henry van de Velde, followed. The style spread to Paris, Vienna, and Barcelona, but it started in Brussels. Several of Horta's townhouses are now a UNESCO World Heritage site.

By 1900, Brussels was a prosperous, confident metropolis of more than half a million people in its wider urban area, a city of cafés, department stores, glass-roofed shopping arcades, and boulevards. It was also a French-speaking city. At the time of independence, a large share of ordinary Brusselers spoke a Dutch dialect, but French was the language of government, business, education, and prestige, and over the nineteenth century, it steadily took over. Today the city is officially bilingual, and the question of language, which was just beginning to stir in the late nineteenth century, will return again and again in the story of modern Belgium.

But the fates of small countries rest on the decisions of large ones. The guarantee of Belgian neutrality, signed in 1839, was now seventy-five years old. And in the summer of 1914, a crisis in the Balkans pushed Europe toward war. Germany's plan to defeat France called for an army to march through neutral Belgium.

If you want to see this chapter in stone, walk the Boulevard Anspach and look at the grand facades of the Bourse. Visit the Palace of Justice, whose terrace has a famous view over the whole city. And if you have time for one museum, make it the Horta Museum in Saint-Gilles, which is the house where the architect lived and worked.

## Chapter 7: Two Wars, Expo 58 and the Capital of Europe, 1914 to 1989

On the fourth of August, 1914, German troops crossed into Belgium. The German government had offered the Belgians a deal: let our army pass through your country to attack France, and you will be spared. King Albert the First refused. The Belgians, he said, would defend their independence. Germany's chancellor dismissed the neutrality treaty that Germany itself had signed, the treaty of 1839, as nothing more than a scrap of paper. Britain, which had guaranteed it, declared war.

The Belgian army fought hard, and the German advance was slowed, but it could not be stopped. On the twentieth of August, German troops marched into Brussels, which had been declared an open city to spare it from destruction. The occupation would last more than four years. Brussels lived under German military rule, with curfews, requisitions, food shortages, and harsh punishments. Meanwhile, King Albert and what was left of the Belgian army held a narrow strip of land in the far west of the country, along the river Yser, for the entire war.

The occupation also gave the city one of its best-known heroines. Edith Cavell was an English nurse who had come to Brussels in 1907 to run a training school for nurses. When the war began, she and a network of Belgian and French helpers began hiding Allied soldiers who had been cut off behind enemy lines, and guiding them across the border into the neutral Netherlands. She is believed to have helped about two hundred men escape. In August of 1915, the Germans arrested her. She openly admitted what she had done. She was tried, sentenced to death, and on the twelfth of October, 1915, shot by a firing squad, in the early morning, at a shooting range in the Brussels suburb of Schaerbeek. Her execution caused an outcry around the world. The night before, she told a chaplain that patriotism was not enough, and that she must have no hatred or bitterness toward anyone.

The war ended in November of 1918, and on the twenty-second of that month King Albert rode into a liberated Brussels. The next two decades were a time of recovery and glamor. Art Deco buildings appeared across the city. The great Palais des Beaux-Arts, designed by Victor Horta, opened in 1928. In 1927, the physicist community gathered in Brussels for the fifth Solvay Conference, where the leading scientists of the age, including Einstein, Bohr, Marie Curie, Heisenberg, and Schrödinger, debated the strange new science of quantum mechanics. The surrealist painter René Magritte lived and worked in the city. And a young Brussels cartoonist named Georges Remi, who signed his work Hergé, published the first adventure of a young reporter with a quiff of red hair in a children's supplement of a newspaper in January of 1929. Belgium's comic strip tradition, which would make Brussels a world capital of the form, had begun.

Then, once again, the Germans came. On the tenth of May, 1940, they invaded Belgium. King Leopold the Third, who had succeeded his father Albert, surrendered the army eighteen days later, against the wishes of his government, which went into exile in London. The king stayed in Belgium as a prisoner. That decision would split the country for years afterward.

Brussels was occupied for four years. Resistance networks sprang up, helping Allied airmen to escape, hiding Jewish children, and publishing underground newspapers. But the occupation was brutal. Tens of thousands of Belgians were sent to forced labor in Germany, and about twenty-five thousand Jews, most of them from Brussels and Antwerp, were deported to the death camps through a transit camp in the city of Mechelen. Only a small fraction survived. Many Belgian families risked their lives hiding Jewish neighbors.

Liberation came on the third of September, 1944, when British tanks of the Guards Armoured Division rolled into Brussels to scenes of overwhelming joy. Compared to many other European cities, Brussels came through the war relatively unscathed. Its buildings were mostly intact, and its position in the center of Western Europe was about to become its destiny.

But first, the country had to settle the question of the king. After the war, many Belgians, especially in Wallonia and in Brussels, wanted to punish Leopold the Third for his surrender and for some of his actions during the occupation. Others, mostly in Catholic Flanders, defended him. In 1950, a referendum showed that a majority of Belgians wanted him back, but the split followed the language line almost perfectly: strong support in Flanders, and a majority against him in Wallonia and in Brussels. Strikes, riots, and violence followed. In 1951, Leopold abdicated in favor of his twenty-year-old son, Baudouin, to calm the country. It was an early sign of how deeply the language divide could cut.

In the postwar years, Belgium chose a very different path from its prewar neutrality. It became a founding member of NATO in 1949 and of the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951. And a Belgian socialist politician, Paul-Henri Spaak, became one of the leading architects of European unity. In 1955 and 1956, Spaak chaired the committee that wrote the report that led to the Treaties of Rome of 1957, creating the European Economic Community, the ancestor of today's European Union. When the new community needed a home for its executive, the Commission, it was set up in Brussels in 1958, intended as a temporary arrangement. It never left.

That same year, 1958, Brussels hosted the first world's fair after the Second World War. Expo 58 opened in April on the Heysel plateau, on the northern edge of the city, and it was meant to show a confident new age of science and peace. The pavilions of the United States and the Soviet Union stood across from each other, in the middle of the Cold War. And at the center was the most striking structure of all: the Atomium, a giant model of an iron crystal, magnified about a hundred and sixty-five billion times, made of nine shining steel spheres linked by tubes, rising a hundred and two meters into the air. It was supposed to be demolished after the fair, but Brusselers loved it too much. It is now the most recognizable symbol of the city.

Then came 1960, and the end of an empire. On the thirtieth of June, 1960, the Belgian Congo became independent. At the ceremony, King Baudouin praised Belgium's colonial work, and Patrice Lumumba, the first prime minister of the new country, gave an angry reply about the humiliations and brutality of colonial rule. Within months, the Congo collapsed into crisis, and in January of 1961, Lumumba was murdered. Decades later, a Belgian parliamentary commission concluded that Belgian officials bore a moral responsibility for his death, and the Belgian government apologized in 2002. In 2022, Belgium returned a tooth, a relic of his remains, to his family.

The 1960s were also the decade when Brussels began to look like the capital of Europe. In 1967, NATO moved its political headquarters from Paris to Brussels, after the French president, Charles de Gaulle, withdrew France from the alliance's military command. The European Commission moved into a huge, star-shaped office building called the Berlaymont, finished in 1967. A whole district of offices spread across the eastern side of the city, around the Rue de la Loi.

This building boom had a cost. In the 1960s and 1970s, developers and the government tore down entire neighborhoods and many fine old buildings to make room for offices, highways, and towers. One of the most painful losses was Victor Horta's Maison du Peuple, a masterpiece of Art Nouveau, demolished in 1965 despite protests. The destruction became so notorious that a new word was invented, Bruxellisation, meaning the careless, speculative destruction of a historic city. By the late 1960s, citizens' groups fought back and began to protect the old districts. The metro opened in 1976, and the city started, slowly, to be more careful with its past.

And all the while, the question of language and region was growing hotter. In 1962 and 1963, Belgium fixed a border between its Dutch-speaking and French-speaking areas, and Brussels was left as a bilingual island inside Flemish territory. In 1968, the great Catholic university in Leuven split in two, along language lines. Over the next two decades, a series of constitutional reforms gradually turned Belgium from a centralized country into a federation of regions and language communities. In 1989, the third region took shape: the Brussels-Capital Region, made up of nineteen municipalities, officially bilingual. In 1993, Belgium formally became a federal state.

If you want to see this chapter, take the metro to Schuman station. Walk out and you will face the Berlaymont, with its star-shaped wings, the headquarters of the European Commission. Nearby is the Cinquantenaire park, where Leopold the Second's arch stands. And if you have time for a trip to the north end of the city, the Atomium is still there, with a view from the top sphere over the whole region.

In the final chapter, we come to the Brussels of today.

## Chapter 8: Brussels Today, 1989 to 2026

So what is Brussels now?

On paper, it is the Brussels-Capital Region, a territory of nineteen municipalities with about one and a quarter million people. The City of Brussels, the historic center with the Grand Place, is only one of those nineteen. The others have their own mayors and councils, and names that Brusselers use as proudly as the name of the city itself: Ixelles, Saint-Gilles, Schaerbeek, Anderlecht, Molenbeek, Uccle, and more. The region is the capital of Belgium. It is the capital of the Flemish Community and of the French Community. And it is, informally but unmistakably, the capital of Europe.

The governance is a puzzle. Belgium is a federation of three regions and three language communities, layered on top of each other, and Brussels sits at the point where they all overlap. The region's parliament has French-speaking and Dutch-speaking groups, and the government has to include members of both. That sounds like a clever safeguard, and it is. It also makes things slow. After regional elections in June of 2024, the parties could not agree on a coalition. It took 613 days, more than twenty months, until a seven-party government finally took office in February of 2026, led by a French-speaking liberal, Boris Dilliès. The parties had to promise to balance a budget that was deeply in deficit, and to merge various regional agencies. For almost two years, the capital of Europe had run on a caretaker government.

At the national level, the story has been similar. After elections in June of 2024, it took more than two hundred thirty days for a federal government to form. In February of 2025, Bart De Wever, a Flemish nationalist who had long dreamed of a more independent Flanders, was sworn in as prime minister by King Philippe, in a five-party coalition. It was the first time a Flemish nationalist had led the Belgian government, which is one of those ironies that Belgian history delivers regularly. King Philippe, who succeeded his father Albert the Second in 2013, is the seventh king of the Belgians.

The real face of modern Brussels is its population. On the first of January, 2025, the region counted about one million, two hundred fifty-six thousand people, and a bit over thirty-seven percent of them held a foreign passport. That is not counting the many Belgians with a foreign background. Residents come from about a hundred and eighty-three different nationalities. The largest foreign group is French, followed by Romanians and Italians, then Spaniards and Moroccans. Brussels also has an important Congolese community, centered on a district called Matongé. In 2025, for the first time since the mid-1990s, the population stopped growing.

Many of those foreign residents work for the international institutions around which the modern city has grown. The European Commission, the Council of the European Union, and parts of the European Parliament all have their seats here, around the Schuman roundabout and the Rue de la Loi. NATO's headquarters, rebuilt in a striking new complex in the north of the city and opened in 2018, is in Brussels. By the most recent count, more than fifty thousand people work for international institutions in the region. Around them are embassies, trade associations, law firms, think tanks, and the lobbyists who try to influence the laws of the European Union. Brussels, a city that once lived by cloth and tapestries, now lives largely by words and rules.

The city's recent history has also had dark days. In November of 2015, after the terror attacks in Paris, some of whose planners had lived in the Brussels district of Molenbeek, the city went into a security lockdown that lasted days, with soldiers on the streets and the metro closed. Then, on the eighteenth of March, 2016, police captured one of the key suspects, Salah Abdeslam, in Molenbeek. Four days later, on the morning of the twenty-second of March, three suicide bombers struck. Two bombs exploded in the departure hall of Brussels Airport at Zaventem, and an hour later, a third went off on a metro train leaving the Maalbeek station, in the heart of the European Quarter, right among the offices of the institutions. Thirty-two people were killed, not counting the attackers, and more than three hundred were injured. The Islamic State group claimed responsibility. Every year since, the city has marked the anniversary, and the little statue of the Manneken Pis has been dressed in a firefighter's uniform to honor the rescuers.

The city has also been rethinking how it lives. In 2015, the central boulevards around the Bourse were closed to cars, creating one of the largest pedestrian zones in Europe, and the old Boulevard Anspach, once built over the buried Senne, became a place for people to walk and sit. A low-emission zone limits older cars, and a traffic plan called Good Move, which reduces through traffic in residential neighborhoods, has been bitterly debated. Brussels is trying to become a city for people rather than just for commuters, and, like most such efforts, it is argued over fiercely.

And then there is the culture, which is as eclectic as the population. Brussels is the capital of the comic strip, with dozens of giant murals of comic heroes painted on building walls across the city, a museum devoted to the art form, and a tradition that gave the world Tintin and the Smurfs. It is a city of beer: in 2016, UNESCO recognized Belgian beer culture as part of the world's cultural heritage. It is a city of chocolate. According to tradition, the praline, the filled chocolate, was invented in 1912 by a Brussels chocolatier named Jean Neuhaus, in the elegant glass-roofed Galeries Royales Saint-Hubert, one of the oldest covered shopping arcades in Europe. And it is a city of waffles, fries, and mussels, and of a small green vegetable that carries its name, the Brussels sprout, which was grown in the area around the city centuries ago.

The city is also reckoning with its past. The Royal Museum for Central Africa, in Tervuren, which Leopold the Second built as a showcase of his colony, was closed for years and reopened in 2018 after a major renovation that tried to confront its colonial origins. In 2020, during worldwide protests against racism, statues of Leopold the Second in Belgium were defaced, and some were removed. A parliamentary commission examined Belgium's colonial past. In 2022, King Philippe visited the Democratic Republic of the Congo and expressed deep regret for the suffering caused by colonial rule. The debate is ongoing, and it will shape how the city understands itself in the years ahead.

If there is a symbol of the identity of Brussels, it is not a lion or a saint or a king. It is a dog. In the local dialect, a zinneke is a mongrel, a mixed-breed street dog that belongs to no one breed. Brusselers have adopted the word with affection for their city, a place of mixed languages, mixed origins, and mixed styles, where no one is quite purebred. There is a small bronze statue of a dog, relieving itself against a post, on a street in the old center, and a colorful parade, held every two years, that celebrates the zinneke spirit.

So let's step back and look at the whole journey.

It began as a settlement in a marsh, with a chapel on an island. It became a market town on the road between Flanders and Cologne, and a ducal capital. Its guilds won a seat at the table. It became the capital of the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands, the home of great painters and weavers. It was nearly erased in 1695, and rebuilt in four years into one of the most beautiful squares in the world. It passed from Spain to Austria to France to the Dutch, and in 1830, after a night at the opera and four days of street fighting, it became the capital of a new nation. It grew rich under Leopold, on the profits of an empire built on terrible cruelty. It was occupied twice, and spared the worst of two world wars. And then, almost by accident, it became the working capital of a united Europe.

Through all of it, Brussels has been a place where different peoples and languages meet, argue, trade, and build together. It has never had one identity, and perhaps that is its identity.

That is the story of Brussels. Thank you for listening, and enjoy the city.
